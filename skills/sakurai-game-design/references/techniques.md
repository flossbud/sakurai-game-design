# Techniques

Concrete, implementable recipes, with Sakurai's numbers where he gives them.

## Game essence

### ESS-T01 · Risk-reward map of every verb
- **what:** A one-page table listing each core action with its danger and its opportunity.
- **how:** For each verb (jump, dash, melee, shoot, dodge, parry, hide), write the risk and the reward. Edge jumps: longer gap vs. falling. Dash: speed vs. control, stamina or running into hazards. Melee: you can hit them, they can hit you. Guns: one-sided attacks vs. ammo, with longer range getting less ammo. Hiding: safety vs. no attacking. Add a downside wherever one is missing.
- **when:** At concept stage and whenever a verb is added.
- **pitfalls:** Listing benefits only; copying a verb from another game without writing why its risk and reward work.
- **episodes:** 6;65;193

### ESS-T02 · On-screen shot cap
- **what:** Limit how many player projectiles can exist at once so effective fire rate rises as the player closes in.
- **how:** Cap simultaneous shots (Darius allows only a few on a wide screen). Hanging back means shots travel far and you wait to fire again; moving close means shots hit quickly and recycle. Apply the same to bombs that can only be dropped quickly near targets, and to bosses that die fast to close-range rapid fire.
- **when:** Shooters and any ranged combat where safe sniping is too strong.
- **pitfalls:** Feeling of being unable to fire if the cap is too low for the screen size.
- **episodes:** 134;157

### ESS-T03 · Recovery proportional to strength
- **what:** Make stronger moves leave the attacker vulnerable for longer, so a miss can be punished.
- **how:** Keep lead-ins fast for responsiveness, then give each move a follow-through whose length matches its power: quick neutral attacks with little opening, slow smash attacks with big damage, knockback and a long vulnerable recovery. In Smash, even Smash Attacks almost never exceed one second, with the hit at about a quarter to a third of the way in.
- **when:** Any melee or fighting system.
- **pitfalls:** Strong attacks with short recovery (push and pull collapses); slow lead-ins that make attacks feel unresponsive.
- **episodes:** 77;82;238

### ESS-T04 · Reversal input through vulnerable positions
- **what:** Route the strongest reversal through the most dangerous stick positions, then pay it off at close range.
- **how:** The shoryuken command (forward, down, down-forward, attack) steps toward the opponent without guarding, crouches open to air attacks, then hits the 'most dangerous stick position possible' before the uppercut. Give it startup invincibility and peak power near its base. Tune input difficulty, input time, the opponent's jump and attack speed, and miss punishment together. Make the heavy variant's payoff the largest.
- **when:** Fighting games and any game with reversal or anti-air moves.
- **pitfalls:** Underpaying the heavy version; making the hard input mandatory in a casual game.
- **episodes:** 159

### ESS-T05 · Charge-and-release on the risky action
- **what:** Turn a risky maneuver into squeeze and release: charge during the danger, cash out at the end.
- **how:** Kirby Air Ride: braking starts a charge, the charge continues through the drift, and releasing at the curve's exit gives a burst of speed. Pay the reward once the risky action is completed, not during it. The result let the racer be played with one button.
- **when:** Movement and racing systems, or any action that is fun because control is briefly lost.
- **pitfalls:** Simply making the risky action faster; permanent boosts that let skilled players run away.
- **episodes:** 57;59

### ESS-T06 · Parry and dodge windows at the moment of impact
- **what:** Defensive actions that pay most when used at the last possible moment.
- **how:** Make parries trigger only right as an attack lands and reward them heavily. Give dodges a short window where other actions are harder to perform. Kid Icarus: Uprising maps dash and dodge to a flick of the Circle Pad, so drawing an attack in and evading at the last moment is simple to input yet deep.
- **when:** Action combat where defense should be a skill test.
- **pitfalls:** Dodges with no downside, which remove risk; windows so generous that early, safe use works as well as late, brave use.
- **episodes:** 125;154;193

### ESS-T07 · Last-chance mechanics
- **what:** Let players claw back from the brink, which invites them to take bigger risks.
- **how:** Later Tetris ports let players rotate and slide a piece after it lands. Look for the moment just before failure and give a skilled player one more input to save themselves.
- **when:** Games where the best payoffs require letting danger build up.
- **pitfalls:** Making the rescue so easy that the brink stops being dangerous.
- **episodes:** 86

### ESS-T08 · Greed scoring
- **what:** Escalating points for chaining risky actions, so players choose how greedy to be.
- **how:** Galaxian flagships are worth more after their escorts die. The ghost-eating maze game pays more for each fleeing ghost eaten in one power-up. The digging game pays more for crushing enemies with rocks than for inflating them. Pay escalating bonuses for chains, ordering or dangerous methods.
- **when:** Score-driven or arcade-style games.
- **pitfalls:** Making the safe play the top-scoring play.
- **episodes:** 155

### ESS-T09 · Press-your-luck depth
- **what:** Make going further a gamble with collected spoils at stake.
- **how:** Put stronger enemies and better rewards deeper in a dungeon, and make defeat cost the spoils gathered so far, so retreating vs. pressing on is a real decision. If you make exits easy or skip resource management (a more casual design), make individual battles harder to keep the tension.
- **when:** RPGs, roguelikes, extraction or exploration games.
- **pitfalls:** Removing exploration risk and not adding it back elsewhere.
- **episodes:** 15

### ESS-T10 · Stress-relief-growth-progress audit
- **what:** Map each loop into the four steps and fill in what supplies each one.
- **how:** Write the obstacle (stress), the moment it is removed (relief), the reward that makes the player stronger (growth) and what the next obstacle becomes (progress). If combat is cut, find another stress: characters in peril, indirect obstacles, costs to save up for. Pay harder solutions with bigger progress.
- **when:** Concept stage and every content review.
- **pitfalls:** A missing initial stress, which also removes relief, growth and progress.
- **episodes:** 9;71

### ESS-T11 · Opposing force for every movement power
- **what:** Design the counter-force at the same time as the power.
- **how:** Flight gets falling, a crash penalty or a requirement to fly; speed gets conditions that slow you down. Place ground, water and structures near routes (fly under the bridge), trigger flight from cliffs and ramps, and include machines or characters that can't fly so flight feels special.
- **when:** Whenever you add traversal powers.
- **pitfalls:** Open sky or space with nothing to crash into; high-altitude dogfights with no ground.
- **episodes:** 65

### ESS-T12 · Deconstruct, analyze, rebuild
- **what:** Sakurai's three-step method for turning an existing fun into an original mechanic.
- **how:** Deconstruct: pick the fun part (drifting). Analyze: state why in risk-and-reward terms (losing control with walls ahead, then coming out ahead). Rebuild: test rewards (score, permanent power-up, temporary speed), reject ones with no tangible benefit or that snowball, then fuse with another concept (charging) to get a new verb. For a genre, write 'what is the risk, what lowers it, what does the biggest reward require', then brainstorm other ways to discharge the risk (Meteos launches blocks instead of erasing them).
- **when:** Pitching, prototyping, or borrowing from a reference game.
- **pitfalls:** Changes that only rename the default action ('vaporized'); copying the action instead of the reason.
- **episodes:** 57;74;86;193

### ESS-T13 · Add one mechanic at a time
- **what:** Grow a ruleset from a minimal core and justify each addition by the decision it creates.
- **how:** Start with one move (a single punch has no push and pull), add walking (range), a kick with longer recovery than the punch (now a whiff can be countered), then jumping, guarding and throwing. Write down the new decision each addition creates and cut any that create none. Re-examine inherited genre rules at every project kickoff.
- **when:** Competitive games and any system that risks rule bloat.
- **pitfalls:** Inputs so hard players can't feel the push and pull (the original Street Fighter).
- **episodes:** 82

### ESS-T14 · One-button patterns
- **what:** Four ways one button carries a game, and which ones have game essence.
- **how:** Rapid tapping (tiring, favors natural tappers, out of fashion); timing (press or release at the right moment: Crane Fever, Megaton Punch); reaction (press on a signal; in versus play let players feint it: Quick Draw, Samurai Kirby); alternating (hold to catch eggs, release to avoid bombs: Egg Catcher). Holding alone has little essence.
- **when:** Minigames, mobile, party games, or simplifying controls.
- **pitfalls:** Mash-only or hold-only core mechanics; adding inputs just because you can.
- **episodes:** 51

### ESS-T15 · Reward and spending plan
- **what:** A table of every reward with its source, timing and where it is spent.
- **how:** Right after the core works, list what each enemy, mission and activity gives. For each currency, design the spending side at the same time and check the items are appealing and fit the game. Keep a few expensive, high-impact purchases worth saving for (Faxanadu). Make crafted outputs clearly worth more than their materials. Show current vs. needed progress (Ys EXP). Gate collectibles behind play.
- **when:** Right after the core prototype works, and again before production.
- **pitfalls:** Flooding players with money and minor gear; collecting as the only activity; unlocking everything at the start.
- **episodes:** 23;71;75;151

### ESS-T16 · Work-earn-upgrade loop
- **what:** The loop that gives task-based games staying power even without push and pull.
- **how:** Base the game on recognizable real-world work (repair, renovation, cleaning) so imagination fills gaps. Each job pays currency that buys tools or skills that make the next job easier. Balance working, profiting and growing stronger. If there is no real ending, place the goal far out of sight, as Cookie Clicker does with exponential production.
- **when:** Simulation, idle and job games.
- **pitfalls:** A work simulation with no profit or upgrade loop.
- **episodes:** 203

### ESS-T17 · Meaningful score units
- **what:** Present the score as something with real-world meaning.
- **how:** Pick a unit that fits the fantasy: an age (Brain Age, Wii Fit Age, where lower is better), money (Burnout's Crash Dollars), a power rating (Global Smash Power). Aim for a number players will say out loud. Even with plain points, count each gain up on screen.
- **when:** Any game with a score or rating.
- **pitfalls:** Adding a bare score counter by default.
- **episodes:** 200

### ESS-T18 · Praise triggers
- **what:** Explicit acknowledgment of good play beyond items and progress.
- **how:** Crowd cheers on big launches, KOs and the victory screen, a subtle 'oh!' when someone barely grabs a ledge; allies who praise based on feats such as enemies defeated; small labeled bonuses for many actions, even clumsy ones (Forza); celebratory effects aimed at the scorer (KO confetti). Match the reaction to how the player feels in that moment.
- **when:** Polish passes on any game.
- **pitfalls:** Announcing praise in the most obvious way instead of heightening the feeling.
- **episodes:** 95

### ESS-T19 · Stakes for spectators and races
- **what:** Small mechanisms that give people a side to root for or a visible race.
- **how:** Before a spectated match or replay, let viewers predict the winner with a small point wager (keep payouts small; investment is the goal) and show identifying info about the players. In versus or race modes keep both players' progress visible. To make any game competitive, start it on two screens at the same moment and judge by first failure, higher score or a set goal ('first to beat three slimes').
- **when:** Spectator modes, versus modes, party play.
- **pitfalls:** Wager features that ignore regional gambling-depiction rules.
- **episodes:** 120;241

### ESS-T20 · Knockback built from two damages
- **what:** Smash's core launch formula and its per-attack parameters.
- **how:** Knockback grows with the attack's damage and the target's accumulated damage; the arc changes with the target's weight and fall speed. Each attack has a free launch angle (with an option to follow the attacker's travel direction), a multiplier, a guaranteed minimum paired with scaling, and an optional fixed knockback used on early hits of multi-hit moves so the whole sequence connects at any damage.
- **when:** Games whose essence is launching or displacing opponents.
- **pitfalls:** Damage-dependent knockback on early hits of a multi-hit move; a minimum without scaling.
- **episodes:** 27;145

### ESS-T21 · Four elements of play audit
- **what:** Use Caillois's agon, alea, mimicry and ilinx as a checklist.
- **how:** Mark where competition (agon), chance (alea: items that appear at random), imitation (mimicry: floors that look hard, hits that sound painful) and vertigo (ilinx: a big knockback after a struggle, the fear of falling) appear in your game. Decide which is the core and add what is missing.
- **when:** Concept and mid-production reviews.
- **pitfalls:** Treating the four as the whole of play; they do not cover everything games offer.
- **episodes:** 178

## Balance and difficulty

### BAL-T01 · Risk-dial sheet
- **what:** A list of every parameter that sets how risky play is, with its current setting and target audience.
- **how:** Record player hitbox size and partial cover, threat-neutralizing range, hits the player can take (Kirby: six), escape tools (flight over pits), control inertia, game speed and input density, boss and target size, enemy durability, and how freely players can leave dangerous areas. Tighten dials for thrill and expert appeal, loosen them for beginners; if you loosen one layer, decide whether another should tighten.
- **when:** When setting the target audience and at every difficulty pass.
- **pitfalls:** Treating size and speed as art or feel decisions only.
- **episodes:** 4;6;15;20;100;108;148

### BAL-T02 · Difficulty wager (Fiend's Cauldron)
- **what:** Players stake currency on difficulty before a stage for proportional rewards.
- **how:** Default Intensity is 2.0. Pouring in hearts raises Intensity (Sakurai played live at the maximum, 9.0): enemies get more aggressive with higher attack and defense, and a clear pays more hearts and better weapons. Spending hearts lowers it; 0.0 is nearly invincible. On failure, some hearts spill out and the retry is easier.
- **when:** Single-player games with a currency and replayable stages.
- **pitfalls:** Not teaching newcomers that going below the default lowers difficulty; surrounding content (huge stages, too many enemies, no respawn where you died) that makes the whole game feel hardcore.
- **episodes:** 41;227

### BAL-T03 · Stuck-player offer
- **what:** An escape hatch after repeated failure in one spot.
- **how:** Let difficulty change at any time without restarting. After several failures, offer to lower the difficulty or skip the stage as if cleared, worded so it helps without humiliating. In action or simulation games, where leveling can't get players past a skill wall, this is essential.
- **when:** Any game with skill walls, especially long ones with late spikes.
- **pitfalls:** Locking difficulty at the start; wording that shames the player.
- **episodes:** 41;213

### BAL-T04 · Locked assist menu with presets
- **what:** Difficulty-lowering assists grouped behind an explicit unlock step.
- **how:** Group options like invisibility, slow motion, grab protection, infinite breath and puzzle skips under a locked Combat Accessibility section, and offer presets that set many options at once. Assists are fine 'because there's more to this game than just fighting'.
- **when:** Big single-player titles with diverse audiences.
- **pitfalls:** Assists that can be switched on by accident.
- **episodes:** 55

### BAL-T05 · Handicapped tuning pass
- **what:** Sakurai's way to approximate a typical player when you know the game too well.
- **how:** Play the difficulty pass one-handed, with reduced health, or with stricter self-imposed conditions, and tune so you can still clear it that way. Then lower the difficulty one more notch.
- **when:** Every difficulty tuning session by the dev team.
- **pitfalls:** Tuning to a clear by the best player on staff, or even the median one.
- **episodes:** 256

### BAL-T06 · Fresh-tester protocol
- **what:** Use unfamiliar players from the very start, once each.
- **how:** Bring in people who have never played, start them from the beginning, and treat each as fresh only once, since they grow familiar. Children (the team's kids, relatives and their friends) in a casual, snack-and-friends setting react honestly; watch them rather than asking questions.
- **when:** Throughout development, especially before locking difficulty.
- **pitfalls:** Calling the same tester back repeatedly as if they were new.
- **episodes:** 248;256

### BAL-T07 · Death-to-control stopwatch
- **what:** Time from failure to regained control, measured and cut.
- **how:** Time your build from the moment of death to the next possible input. Keep the failure effect flashy and impactful but about one second, depending on genre. Trim fades, loads and non-interactive screens; let button presses advance any remaining text. Place respawn points where enemies can't attack immediately. Keep some cost (checkpoint distance or penalty) so it never feels mindless.
- **when:** As soon as a failure loop exists, and after every content change near checkpoints.
- **pitfalls:** Respawning into enemy fire; infinite in-place respawns.
- **episodes:** 117

### BAL-T08 · Retry-path audit
- **what:** For each likely failure point, review what the player replays to get back.
- **how:** List the content between the checkpoint and the failure point, estimate how many players will fail there (assume more than you expect), then shorten the path or vary it with randomized equipment, items or events, and hint at what comes next.
- **when:** Roguelikes, permadeath and hard games; any long checkpoint gap.
- **pitfalls:** Assuming players will persist because hard games are popular.
- **episodes:** 128

### BAL-T09 · Mountain-trail curve
- **what:** Plot player power against enemy difficulty and reshape it into peaks and flats.
- **how:** Draw both curves across the game. Replace any straight diagonal (or steady late-rising curve that keeps them proportional) with peaks at bosses that demand a strategy and pay off, and flats: unexpectedly empty areas in enemy-dense zones and spots to rack up gains. Vary the scenery along the route. In multi-direction games, avoid level scaling.
- **when:** Progression and content-layout planning.
- **pitfalls:** Rest stops at fixed intervals as the only highlights.
- **episodes:** 105

### BAL-T10 · Peaks-and-valleys nerf checklist
- **what:** A procedure for weakening an option without making it average.
- **how:** List the move's parameters: attack power, speed, duration, windup, follow-through length, range, hit size, animation speed, knockback, combo ability, shield damage, shield recovery. Mark the defining one and leave it alone; change a secondary one (lengthen the vulnerable follow-through, shrink the hitbox). Don't soften weaknesses in the same patch. Ask of every proposal: does this make it more average?
- **when:** Every balance patch.
- **pitfalls:** The obvious fix of shaving the peak and filling the valley.
- **episodes:** 218;247

### BAL-T11 · Context rebalancing
- **what:** Tame an overpowered ability by changing its surroundings instead of cutting it.
- **how:** Kirby's Adventure: the larger NES screen let flight bypass nearly anything, so Sakurai enlarged the info panel (less vertical space, bigger-looking characters, room for ability art), kept mid-boss fights to one screen, and reduced flight speed. Check how size and abilities play whenever screen size or aspect ratio changes.
- **when:** When a signature mechanic becomes too strong after a platform or content change.
- **pitfalls:** Removing the mechanic outright.
- **episodes:** 11

### BAL-T12 · Win-rate band and mirror test
- **what:** Two data checks for fairness.
- **how:** Mirror test: identical setups should draw; fix any built-in side advantage. Win-rate band: gather real match data and check every option sits in a narrow range (Smash Ultimate: 87 fighters from 47.18% to 51.43% in a smooth gradation). Then make the final call on how each option feels, and check multiplayer as well as tournament usage.
- **when:** Once enough matches exist, before and after patches.
- **pitfalls:** Treating win rates as everything.
- **episodes:** 218;220;230

### BAL-T13 · Chaos-based catch-up
- **what:** Help the player who is behind without touching core stats.
- **how:** Casual extreme: Mario Kart gives trailing players speed boosts and stronger items. Skill-reflecting version: no attack boost or reduced knockback for losers; instead make the comeback power-up (Smash Ball) harder for the leader to get, charge the Final Smash Meter faster for losing players, and let random items and stage hazards shake things up. Offer an optional automatic handicap.
- **when:** Local and party multiplayer.
- **pitfalls:** Stat buffs for losers in a game meant to reflect skill.
- **episodes:** 189;202

### BAL-T14 · Per-player handicap settings
- **what:** Let a strong and a weak player share one session fairly.
- **how:** Expose difficulty and resources as separate per-player settings (The Genji and the Heike Clans: hardest difficulty and fewest candles vs. easiest and most). Use asymmetric fail conditions (one player loses on the first hit, the other plays to game over) and move on together when either fails. Offer a no-penalty 'trial time' for first-timers, as Space Harrier did.
- **when:** Couch play, family play, coaching newcomers.
- **pitfalls:** Only offering a global difficulty setting.
- **episodes:** 155;157

### BAL-T15 · Simple input with a modest bonus for the hard one
- **what:** Two ways to perform a special move, so execution is rewarded but never required.
- **how:** Smash Bros. Ryu: the shoryuken comes out with one button or with the full command; the command version is stronger with more invincibility frames, but not so much that players who can't do it are shut out.
- **when:** Games that borrow genre inputs but target casual players.
- **pitfalls:** Making the command so much stronger that it becomes mandatory.
- **episodes:** 159

### BAL-T16 · Rewindable enemy table
- **what:** Difficulty driven by an ordered spawn table that players can push back.
- **how:** Xevious has no AI reading your skill: predefined enemy tables advance as you play, and destroying the radar-station-like enemy sends you back to an earlier, easier point in the table. Drive spawns from an ordered table and add one recognizable target that rewinds it.
- **when:** Arcade-style and wave-based games.
- **pitfalls:** A rewind target players never learn about.
- **episodes:** 156

### BAL-T17 · Human-paced CPU
- **what:** Settings that keep AI opponents fair and fun.
- **how:** Give CPUs a reflex delay, let reversed controls confuse them briefly before they adapt, bias high difficulty toward offense and winning, cap perfect shields and dodges, and organize logic into modes (angry at low HP, holding optimal distance, easing off when winning too much) checked for contradictions.
- **when:** Any game with computer opponents.
- **pitfalls:** Instant reactions; perfect defense; contradictory behavior modes.
- **episodes:** 221

### BAL-T18 · Population-relative rank
- **what:** A rank display that makes most players feel successful.
- **how:** Use a number where higher is better, computed against the total player count (Global Smash Power). Hide the world #1's identity and score from general players. Add an elite tier above a threshold that matches top players with each other. Design the metric so invalid data is filtered out. Widen matchmaking range by region and hour only as far as needed.
- **when:** Online competitive modes.
- **pitfalls:** Raw positions like 'x-millionth place'.
- **episodes:** 170

### BAL-T19 · Controlled randomness
- **what:** Keep randomness where it adds variety, remove it where it decides losses.
- **how:** Put random outcomes under player control where they feel unfair (Peach's Smash Attack now chosen with up or down). Keep randomness that still needs skill (the Hero's command list). Keep random items for party play, remove randomness from strict contests. Prototype strategy combat without random evasion first. Seed the generator with an unpredictable value like the system clock. Where draws are random, let players choose among two or three.
- **when:** Any system with random rolls.
- **pitfalls:** A luck stat as the only fix; predictable generators players can reset.
- **episodes:** 120;189;230

### BAL-T20 · Late-change difficulty re-check
- **what:** Guard tuned difficulty against performance and hardware changes.
- **how:** Find gameplay timings tied to processing speed (enemy or block speed, time limits, hunger or decay rates). Freeze them before final tuning, replay difficulty-critical content after each optimization pass, and do the final check on retail hardware. For a launch problem, prefer narrow fixes like easing specific encounters or adding an easier option.
- **when:** Final months of development and post-launch patches.
- **pitfalls:** Tuning only on a dev board.
- **episodes:** 74;92;141

### BAL-T21 · Variable-strength layer for party modes
- **what:** A parameter framework that changes character strength, fenced off from serious play.
- **how:** Build a layer of stat boosts, equipment and alternate special moves (Smash for 3DS/Wii U: three options per special) and use it to power party modes, customization and trainable AI (amiibo). Keep it out of level-playing-field matches. If players overpower themselves in solo content, give fewer rewards.
- **when:** Games with both competitive and casual modes.
- **pitfalls:** Stat systems leaking into competitive play.
- **episodes:** 135;160

## Planning, structure and pacing

### PLAN-T01 · Three-minute first-boot test
- **what:** Time how long a newcomer takes to reach control and to grasp the core fun.
- **how:** Have someone who has never seen the game boot it while you time the first input and the first moment they see why it's fun; the second should be under three minutes. Count the presses from title screen to play and make repeated confirm presses lead into the main game. Introduce the main threat early (Uprising shows Medusa in under a minute).
- **when:** Every milestone build, and before cutting a trailer.
- **pitfalls:** Testing only with teammates who know the menus; counting a non-interactive intro as 'playing'.
- **episodes:** 17;235;240;113

### PLAN-T02 · Control-time audit
- **what:** Measure how much of a session the player actually controls.
- **how:** Record a full session and tag every second as in control, not in control (cutscenes, loading, menus, transitions, forced animations) or empty (nothing to do). Set reduction targets for the last two, cut, and re-measure.
- **when:** Mid-production onward, and after adding any cutscene, menu or transition.
- **pitfalls:** Cutting the breathers that work as palate cleansers; ignoring waits that look like a development oversight.
- **episodes:** 73;250;246

### PLAN-T03 · Quick-retry window
- **what:** Minimize the time from failure to regained control.
- **how:** Measure from the moment the player realizes they failed ('Ugh, I blew it!') to control returning. Make the failure effect flashy but about a second long, depending on genre, then hand back control with short loads. Don't let enemies bombard players the moment they respawn.
- **when:** Action games, hard games and anything with frequent failure.
- **pitfalls:** Infinite on-the-spot respawns can make the game feel mindless; a drawn-out death sequence cools interest.
- **episodes:** 117

### PLAN-T04 · Skip-everything pass
- **what:** Make every non-interactive moment skippable, pausable and speedable.
- **how:** List the logos, opening movie, cutscenes, dialogue and credits. Add skip (all at once if possible), pause, line-by-line skip or fast-forward, and a skip prompt on any button press during a cutscene. Let voiced lines play during gameplay where possible.
- **when:** Before any external playtest and before release.
- **pitfalls:** Unskippable company logos (Sakurai thinks guideline writers should treat skippability as a given); skippable scenes that can't be paused.
- **episodes:** 233;180;211;104

### PLAN-T05 · Bracketed parameter notation
- **what:** Mark each tunable value in a spec so it can't be misread.
- **how:** Write the behavior in plain language with each value in brackets and an 'O' placeholder, e.g. 'Initiate movement at [Special startup X-velocity is O].' and 'At the same time, decelerate by [Special X-deceleration is O/frame].' Rule: anything in brackets becomes a parameter. Keep a separate file pairing each bracketed phrase with its current value, keep it out of code, and do all tuning there.
- **when:** Character- and move-specific specs; handle global values like general attack power separately.
- **pitfalls:** Terse labels like 'X-velocity'; hard-coding values so every tweak needs a programmer.
- **episodes:** 68;46

### PLAN-T06 · One project unit for speed and time
- **what:** Make every speed and timing value unambiguous.
- **how:** Choose pixels per frame for 2D sprites, or km/h or m/frame for real-scale 3D, and put the unit in every parameter name. Write timings in frames at the target frame rate. Calibrate by eye: Mario's top speed in Mario Bros. is roughly 1 pixel per frame on a 256-pixel-wide NES screen. Judge speeds relative to character size.
- **when:** At project start, before anyone writes a movement spec.
- **pitfalls:** Reusing raw values across resolutions or platforms with different pixel counts; unitless numbers.
- **episodes:** 84;76;5

### PLAN-T07 · Hierarchical outline for specs and ideas
- **what:** Keep specs and idea lists as a collapsible tree.
- **how:** Use an outline view (in Microsoft Word: View, then Outline). Mirror the game's structure, for example one top-level heading per main-menu item, or fighter, then special moves, then each move's spec. Collapse what you're not working on and drag whole headings to reorganize. Clean it up before it goes on the shared server.
- **when:** From the first idea list through the full design doc.
- **pitfalls:** Posting a raw personal outline as the team's design document.
- **episodes:** 137;261

### PLAN-T08 · Comic-panel proposal deck
- **what:** A proposal that moves fast regardless of length.
- **how:** One screenshot or diagram and two lines of text per page, one topic per page. Group pages into categories and subcategories and show which section you're in. Say a little about each slide instead of reading it. Cut graphs, market research, grand-vision passages and plan B. Itemize the outline, then write the whole deck in one push without discussing it until the draft is done.
- **when:** Pitching a game, a feature or a sequel.
- **pitfalls:** Walls of text read aloud; trimming page count at the cost of clarity (200-plus pages is fine if each is quick).
- **episodes:** 38;52;12;144

### PLAN-T09 · Bare-rules prototype or finished-quality pilot
- **what:** Prove fun and feasibility before committing people.
- **how:** For a game, build only the core rules (Smash's prototype had no special moves, dodges or items), or mock key characters at their intended on-screen size with full movesets. For a content series, finish two or three representative pieces at final quality (Sakurai's pilot was three real episodes), use them as a go/no-go test and to win partners. Estimate each candidate concept's development time.
- **when:** Before full production or before asking for budget and permissions.
- **pitfalls:** Adding content to the prototype before the core is proven; a pilot below final quality that can't answer 'can we really do this?'.
- **episodes:** 27;100;261

### PLAN-T10 · Plan-everything content pipeline
- **what:** Plan and script a fixed-scope content project completely before production.
- **how:** Brainstorm a few hundred one-line topics; group them into categories with a fixed count each; cut weak ones; outline and script every piece; spread the categories evenly into a release order in a spreadsheet that doubles as schedule and progress tracker; use one ID scheme (category letter plus number) for folders and files. Write fast: Sakurai wrote about twelve scripts a day.
- **when:** Content whose scope can be known up front, especially when run beside another big job.
- **pitfalls:** You can't take requests or change course based on feedback mid-run.
- **episodes:** 261

### PLAN-T11 · Reward inventory
- **what:** List every reward the game gives while it's still a plan.
- **how:** In the planning doc, list each reward type (unlocks, collectibles, currency and what it buys, materials and what they make, options, objectives) and when it first arrives. If the list is thin, design more systems before production. Check each against the concept (skip stat growth if the fun needs a fixed baseline) and confirm saved currency has appealing purchases.
- **when:** As soon as the bones of the game exist.
- **pitfalls:** Unlocking everything from the start removes the joy of earning it; locking content many players want from the start (such as music) annoys them.
- **episodes:** 151;75

### PLAN-T12 · Goal grid (Checklist)
- **what:** A panel grid of objectives that reveals its neighbors as it clears.
- **how:** Lay out conditions as panels. Clearing one breaks it and shows the unlock conditions of adjacent panels. Many panels give rewards, but the main point is always having visible things to work toward.
- **when:** Games that need a sense of accomplishment during casual play.
- **pitfalls:** Going overboard with reward systems.
- **episodes:** 59;151

### PLAN-T13 · Current-versus-needed progress display
- **what:** Show how far the player has come and how far is left to the next reward.
- **how:** Put current and required values on the main screen (EXP now and EXP to the next level, or money and the price of the next big item). Make every enemy defeated or coin collected visibly move it. Keep key purchases few, expensive and powerful so saving for them is a goal.
- **when:** Any game with growth or currency.
- **pitfalls:** So much money and minor gear that no single purchase is worth saving for.
- **episodes:** 23

### PLAN-T14 · Mountain-trail difficulty plot
- **what:** Shape the difficulty curve into peaks and flats.
- **how:** Plot player strength and enemy or obstacle strength across the game. Reject straight or proportional lines. Mark peaks (bosses that need a strategy and give a clear reward) and flats (an unexpectedly empty area in a dense zone, farming spots), and vary scenery between them.
- **when:** RPGs and any game with character growth; review at each content milestone.
- **pitfalls:** Level scaling that removes any reason to get ahead; highlights only at fixed rest stops.
- **episodes:** 105

### PLAN-T15 · Errand packaging checklist
- **what:** Five checks that make a fetch quest worth the trip.
- **how:** 1) a useful reward or a story beat players want to see; 2) many small joys along the way (drops, foraging finds, a twist); 3) a choice of route, such as a long safe path or a risky shortcut; 4) a quick exit, whisking the player back once it's done; 5) a sense of future developments, such as a missing girl and a house with strange noises instead of 'Go buy me some meat!'.
- **when:** Any quest, errand or travel objective.
- **pitfalls:** Back-and-forth chains with nothing on the way.
- **episodes:** 64

### PLAN-T16 · Teach-in-place layouts
- **what:** Level layouts and modes that teach controls through play.
- **how:** Place a real early obstacle that can only be passed with the action being taught. Right after a movement skill is acquired, build a small enclosed area whose only exit needs it. Put short prompts along the opening path where the action is used next, reading the current button mapping. Show a short looping clip beside each new skill's description. Build a challenge mode around each character's own moves.
- **when:** Opening stages and every new ability.
- **pitfalls:** Text-along-the-path doesn't suit complex controls; prompts that assume default buttons break with remapping.
- **episodes:** 85;173;175

### PLAN-T17 · Constrained opening
- **what:** Start with a reduced version of the core action.
- **how:** Open with a segment that asks for only part of the control set (an on-rails segment before free movement, the basic verb before the depth system), then add controls stage by stage. Optionally give first-timers a no-penalty 'trial time'.
- **when:** Games whose full control set is demanding.
- **pitfalls:** A constrained opening that runs long enough to feel like a detached tutorial.
- **episodes:** 125;11;157

### PLAN-T18 · Kickoff constraint list
- **what:** Collect every binding rule and limit before production.
- **how:** List age rating, platform holder, storefront, online service, internal company and licensor or franchise rules, regional rules on depicting gambling, media size and the weakest target's performance. Check each planned feature and visual against the list and budget several adjustment rounds for region-sensitive features.
- **when:** Project kickoff and whenever a new platform or region is added.
- **pitfalls:** Assuming a feature is fine everywhere because it passed in one region.
- **episodes:** 139;120;135;160

### PLAN-T19 · Test-matrix estimate
- **what:** Size the testing a feature set implies before committing to it.
- **how:** Multiply characters by opponents, moves, items, player counts, stages and hazards, and progression modifiers. Smash Ultimate's 89 fighters alone give about 8,000 pairings, and a game that takes dozens of hours to finish may need thousands to tens of thousands of test hours. Put the first and heaviest passes on the likely-to-break systems, and re-check widely after every fix.
- **when:** When adding growth, parameter, unlock or multiplayer systems.
- **pitfalls:** Brute-forcing every combination; shipping a hotfix without regression checks.
- **episodes:** 136

### PLAN-T20 · Sequel kickoff inventory
- **what:** Decide what a sequel's head start buys.
- **how:** List what carries over (team, office, tools, assets, hardware architecture). Decide whether the saved effort goes to expanding the system or to content. List concrete changes for every character or major asset across all disciplines (Sakurai averaged 70 to 100 per fighter for Ultimate). Write one line on how the sequel is more fun and impactful. If the tempo changes, plan to retune every parameter.
- **when:** Sequel or follow-up kickoff.
- **pitfalls:** Porting old tuning into a game whose speed changed; same scale and content as the last game.
- **episodes:** 164;160;40

### PLAN-T21 · Cutscene triage
- **what:** Decide what each cutscene is for.
- **how:** Tag each cutscene as an emotional or clarity beat, a deliberate breather, or downtime. Shorten or cut the downtime. Check that none undoes a fight the player just won, and that none is the main reward for progress.
- **when:** Whenever story content is added.
- **pitfalls:** Cutting the breathers that fast action games need.
- **episodes:** 73;233;113;163

### PLAN-T22 · Story delivered during play
- **what:** Tell the story without stopping the game.
- **how:** Start each stage at once (Pit runs through a door and play begins) and deliver briefings as dialogue during play, with an option to turn it off. If story beats must line up with fixed-length segments and music, write the scenario on the development side.
- **when:** Action games with story.
- **pitfalls:** Dialogue that competes with critical gameplay audio or attention.
- **episodes:** 125;113

### PLAN-T23 · Neutral baseline and one-word identity
- **what:** Make every character distinct and memorable.
- **how:** Define a neutral character that fits your rules. Push specific parameters far from it for each new character, give some a one-of-a-kind mechanic and budget the code for it. Write a one- or two-word identity for each; if you can't, push further. Tune with bold differences first and fix problems after.
- **when:** Roster, enemy and option design.
- **pitfalls:** Making something so eccentric it is unusable, like a ship that fires everywhere except forward.
- **episodes:** 206

### PLAN-T24 · Short, concrete versus goals
- **what:** Turn any single-player game into a quick contest.
- **how:** Start the same game on two screens at the same moment and agree a goal (first to fail loses, higher score wins, or 'the first to beat three slimes is the winner!'). Keep both players' progress visible and move on to the next game as soon as someone wins or fails.
- **when:** Race or versus modes, party sessions, jam prototypes.
- **pitfalls:** Goals too long to keep the tension.
- **episodes:** 241

## Game feel

### FEEL-T01 · Hit stop: freeze length from strength x multiplier
- **what:** Stop both attacker and target briefly when a hit lands, with the stop length computed per hit.
- **how:** Compute stop length from the attack's strength (stronger attack = longer stop), then multiply by a per-move or per-character factor so a sweet spot or a guest character can feel stronger or lighter than its raw damage. Lengthen the stop for lightning attacks and reduce it in multiplayer battles. Compute the length up front so shake and blends can resolve over exactly that time. Sakurai gives no frame counts for stop length; his only timing figure inside hit stop is the roughly four-frame blend into the damage pose (FEEL-T03). The 'one second' stop in Fantasy Zone is a cited game's player-hit pause, not a recommended hit-stop length.
- **when:** Every melee or projectile hit; essential in any game about trading blows.
- **pitfalls:** One fixed stop for all hits; stops so long in multiplayer that the action stutters; skipping the multiplier so every move with equal damage feels identical.
- **episodes:** 47;3

### FEEL-T02 · Hit stop shake: victim more, attacker less, model only
- **what:** Add positional shake during hit stop so the freeze reads as an impact, not a pause.
- **how:** Shake the receiving character with a bigger amplitude and the attacker with a smaller one. Offset only the rendered model; leave hitboxes and hurtboxes where they are. On the ground shake side to side only (vertical shake would push 3D legs through the floor); in the air shake in all directions. Start big and decay to nothing over the precomputed stop length. When the camera is pulled back, shake characters comparatively more so the hit reads the same near or far.
- **when:** All hit stops in action games.
- **pitfalls:** Shaking the collision with the model, which makes hits that should connect miss; vertical ground shake clipping feet; constant amplitude that ends abruptly.
- **episodes:** 47

### FEEL-T03 · Hit stop poses: blend the victim, creep the attacker
- **what:** Keep motion alive inside the freeze so it looks fluid and swords look like they cut.
- **how:** Instead of freezing the victim or snapping them to one pose, blend them into the damage pose over about four frames (Sakurai's figure for Smash). Keep the attacker's animation advancing so slowly it doesn't move every frame (a sword tip still inching upward), then resume as if nothing happened when the stop ends. Put the attacker creep behind a per-attack toggle: it exists for swords, and some attacks look better without it.
- **when:** Strong hits, especially sword swings.
- **pitfalls:** A frozen victim that pops into its damage pose; attacker creep on moves where it reads as sliding.
- **episodes:** 47;142

### FEEL-T04 · Use the hit stop window for effects, sound and information
- **what:** Characters freeze, but other feedback can keep running and carry more detail.
- **how:** Let hit-mark particles keep animating during the stop and design them to unfold in that time. Time strong hit sounds so their main body plays during the freeze and is mostly over when action resumes. A one-frame blade trail still reads because hit stop holds it on screen when the attack lands. If the victim can influence their launch, show it: Smash Ultimate draws the adjusted flight path when the hit player inputs a direction during hit stop.
- **when:** Any game with hit stop.
- **pitfalls:** Freezing hit effects along with the characters; long sound tails that bleed into the next hit.
- **episodes:** 131;167;179

### FEEL-T05 · Boss stop, death stop and finishing-blow stop
- **what:** Give the biggest moments their own, larger stop.
- **how:** On a boss defeat, stop the screen and flash it for a moment before the defeat sequence (Sakurai's 'boss stop', used since Kirby's Adventure). When the player is hit or dies, pause for an instant so the point of impact is visible. On a finishing blow, stop the screen completely (Smash; Ultimate's Finish Zoom extends this). For the biggest moments layer stop, screen shake and slow motion; a brief full-screen flash covers a large area and adds impact the way shake does. Character hit flashes in Smash Ultimate change strength with camera distance (more pronounced when pulled back), because a strong flash in a close-up would make too much of the screen flash.
- **when:** Boss kills, match-ending hits, player deaths.
- **pitfalls:** Deaths players don't notice; full-screen flashes beyond photosensitivity limits; using the boss-stop treatment so often it loses meaning.
- **episodes:** 3;225;223;56

### FEEL-T06 · Screen shake presets with distinct patterns
- **what:** Author shake as a small set of patterns, not one shake scaled up and down.
- **how:** Smash uses large, medium, small and extra-small shakes. Small shakes only up and down; medium adds some horizontal shake; large shakes even more. Sustained shakes (moving terrain) use mostly vertical motion and shouldn't move much on either axis. Map each preset to an impact class.
- **when:** Any impact big enough to deserve shake.
- **pitfalls:** Shaking for every minor event; one pattern for everything.
- **episodes:** 103

### FEEL-T07 · Screen shake implementation in 3D
- **what:** Make a camera shake read at any distance and stay pleasant.
- **how:** Either move the 3D camera (more common; distant objects barely move) or move the whole rendered image (everything shakes, including the HUD, and blank edges can show). With camera shake, also shake the look-at point, in sync or even more than the camera. Scale amplitude by camera distance and field of view; retune stages with a tight FOV. Start strong and subside gradually along a slightly randomized path rather than a clean whirlpool. Keep it small enough that the screen stays readable.
- **when:** Any 3D game using shake.
- **pitfalls:** A far-off look-at point that barely moves; image shake revealing empty borders unplanned; shake big enough to hide the action.
- **episodes:** 103;47

### FEEL-T08 · Input acknowledgment on the first frame
- **what:** Guarantee every action shows that it registered immediately, even when the attack itself comes later.
- **how:** On the first frame after input, show a pose that is drastically different from standby. Light, spammable attacks can skip the lead-in entirely: Zero Suit Samus's neutral attack comes out one frame after input. Charge moves hold the lead-in pose and shake in place. Give each accepted press a distinct sound or highlight, and shape impact sounds to peak almost immediately. Measure frames from press to first visible change for every action.
- **when:** Every player action; doubly important online and on laggy displays.
- **pitfalls:** Interpolating out of idle; windups whose first frames barely change the pose.
- **episodes:** 49;36;104;167

### FEEL-T09 · Attack timeline proportions
- **what:** Lay out startup, hit and recovery so attacks are responsive but strong moves are punishable.
- **how:** Standard attacks finish in under one second; even Smash Attacks with large openings almost never exceed it (Sakurai's figure; at 60 fps that is under 60 frames). The hit registers about 1/4 to 1/3 of the way in, some fast attacks at 1/5. Everything after is follow-through, and its length scales with power. Mark cancel frames late in the recovery at the point players expect control back. Reference anchor: about 15 frames from Smash Attack input to release.
- **when:** Melee and attack design in action and fighting games.
- **pitfalls:** Long startup instead of long recovery; strong moves with short recovery; cancel windows nobody has playtested.
- **episodes:** 77;36;76

### FEEL-T10 · Basic jump model
- **what:** Implement a jump as initial upward velocity plus constant downward acceleration per frame.
- **how:** Sakurai's illustration (any unit): apply upward velocity 5, then add 1 unit of downward acceleration per frame while airborne, so velocity goes 4, 3, 2, 1, 0 and then falls 1, 2, 3, 4. On flat ground the character lands when downward velocity equals the initial upward velocity. Horizontal speed usually stays constant or decelerates slightly. Add a maximum fall speed and inertia. The 5 and 1 are teaching values, not tuning advice.
- **when:** Starting point for any jump.
- **pitfalls:** Shipping the textbook arc without shaping it to the game.
- **episodes:** 37

### FEEL-T11 · Rise/fall asymmetry and air control by goal
- **what:** Shape the arc to what the game needs.
- **how:** Mario-style: downward acceleration starts very low and rises suddenly at the peak, so the fall is much faster than the rise; pairs with hold-for-height. Metroid-style: rise even slower and fall slowly, almost floating, which helps when the height you shoot from matters. Ghosts 'n Goblins-style: low jump, no mid-air direction change, so every jump is a planned commitment and you can attack while jumping backward. For slams and throws, make the descent faster than the ascent (Zangief's Spinning Pile Driver from Street Fighter II Turbo on).
- **when:** Platformers, shooters, action games, grapples and slams.
- **pitfalls:** Copying one game's jump into a game with different goals.
- **episodes:** 37;193

### FEEL-T12 · Rescue burst jump
- **what:** A jump that still gets players out of trouble when they react a little late.
- **how:** Apply a very strong upward velocity at the start of each jump, greatly increase downward acceleration for a few frames, and once the jump snaps to a halt return gravity to normal. Used in Smash Ultimate and, before it, Kirby Super Star. It adds parameters, complicates the physics and raises bug risk, which Sakurai judged worth it.
- **when:** Action and fighting games where jumps are escapes.
- **pitfalls:** Extra parameters interacting badly with other movement states; untested edge cases.
- **episodes:** 37

### FEEL-T13 · Uniform pre-jump windup when inputs overlap
- **what:** Keep a short windup before the jump so an up-attack on the same direction can still be input.
- **how:** If jump and up-attacks share a stick direction, keep a short windup before liftoff and make it the same length for every character. Smash once had varied windups and unified them; removing the windup would make players jump before they could input an Up Smash.
- **when:** Games where tap-up jumps and up-attacks coexist.
- **pitfalls:** Removing it to feel faster and breaking the other input; per-character windups that make muscle memory unreliable.
- **episodes:** 37

### FEEL-T14 · Knockback parameter set
- **what:** Give every attack the parameters needed to make its launch land and feel right at any damage.
- **how:** Base rule: more attack damage and more accumulated target damage = more knockback. Per attack: a free launch angle; an optional modifier that bends knockback toward the attacker's direction of travel (helps aerial combos); a multiplier (strong move with slight knockback or weak move launching far); a guaranteed minimum so a barely damaged target still reacts, paired with knockback scaling so the minimum doesn't inflate big launches; and an optional fixed knockback that ignores damage. The target's weight and fall speed change the arc. Sakurai gives the structure, not the formula.
- **when:** Games built on launching opponents; any game with physical knockback.
- **pitfalls:** Minimum knockback without scaling; one formula for every move.
- **episodes:** 145

### FEEL-T15 · Fixed knockback for multi-hit moves
- **what:** Make every hit of a multi-hit move connect at any damage level.
- **how:** Give every hit except the last a fixed, damage-independent knockback so the target reacts identically whatever their damage; give only the finisher standard knockback. Test the string at low and high damage.
- **when:** Multi-hit attacks, flurries, rising combo moves.
- **pitfalls:** Damage-dependent early hits that pop the target out at high damage or fail to carry them at low damage.
- **episodes:** 145

### FEEL-T16 · Per-action ledge behavior
- **what:** Decide, move by move, whether a character can go over a ledge.
- **how:** Build a table: forward dodges and short stepping attacks stop at the edge; fast rushes carry over; long rushes stop at the ledge if it is reached late in the move; rolling moves fly off only above a speed threshold. For plain walking, consider requiring a push against the edge, steering movement along a ledge that runs nearly parallel to the direction of travel, or (in some games) an automatic jump.
- **when:** Any game with ledges or platforms.
- **pitfalls:** One global rule; ignoring momentum so it's too easy to fly off; small nudges that drop players.
- **episodes:** 102

### FEEL-T17 · Camera center point that lets motion show
- **what:** Stop the camera from hiding the character's movement.
- **how:** Track the camera's center point less, or not at all, on the Y axis during jumps so the character visibly rises. Let the character drift off center during quick moves, dodges and drifts. In side-scrollers shift the view ahead in the travel direction. Keep a fully anchored center only where precise realistic racing suits it.
- **when:** Third-person 3D games, side-scrollers, vehicle games.
- **pitfalls:** Full vertical tracking; camera lag so large the character leaves the safe area.
- **episodes:** 61

### FEEL-T18 · Co-op and knockback camera rules
- **what:** Keep scrolling cameras fair when several players or launched characters move at different speeds.
- **how:** In co-op scrollers, make one player the camera's lead and let the supporter go off-camera; warp a supporter who drifts too far back to the lead (Kirby Super Star's Space Jump). When a character is knocked flying, stop scrolling; once they settle, gently scroll back and recenter (Kirby's Dream Land's 1990 design document).
- **when:** Local co-op scrollers; games with launches.
- **pitfalls:** Letting every player pull the scroll equally in a jump-heavy game; a camera that chases every launch.
- **episodes:** 20;100

### FEEL-T19 · Flick and tilt reading on analog sticks
- **what:** Use how far and how fast the stick moves.
- **how:** Scale walking speed by tilt amount. Trigger a dash when the stick travels from neutral to its edge within a short time (Sakurai's 'flick input', localized as 'Tap' in the N64 game), replacing double-taps and button dashes. Use the same flick for dodges where drawing attacks in close is risk and reward (Kid Icarus: Uprising). Expose flick sensitivity as a setting (Smash Ultimate) and set minimum tilt detection separately for each console's stick.
- **when:** Games on analog sticks where dash or strong attacks matter.
- **pitfalls:** Accidental dashes with no sensitivity option; one stick-sensitivity value for every controller.
- **episodes:** 154;27;19;125

### FEEL-T20 · Lag check before every demo or playtest
- **what:** Verify the display and audio chain on the exact setup players will use.
- **how:** Run a game that responds the instant you press (an NES game such as Kirby; not a 3D game). Press jump; the character must move immediately. Set the TV to Game Mode or Low Latency Mode, or turn off every picture enhancement and extra signal processing. Check audio separately; through home-theater gear hit sounds can lag, so try an analog jack or low-latency audio. Read latency specs as frames: 16.67 ms = one frame at 60 fps. Record footage on a low-latency monitor.
- **when:** Before events, demos, playtests and capture sessions.
- **pitfalls:** Assuming someone else tested; testing with 3D games where lag is hard to see.
- **episodes:** 8;156

### FEEL-T21 · Frame-counting drill
- **what:** Train a sense of frame lengths at 60 fps.
- **how:** Clap seconds while saying '1-and-2-and-3-and'. Each beat is about 30 frames and half a beat about 15. Check yourself against an engine frame counter. Memorize anchors: about 15 frames from Smash Attack input to release, about 15 for a standard Tekken kick's startup, a bit under half that for a Street Fighter II heavy punch's startup. Estimate other values by distance from 0/15/30 (20 frames is a little past halfway from 0 to 30).
- **when:** Anyone who writes or reviews timing.
- **pitfalls:** Specifying frame counts you can't feel.
- **episodes:** 76

### FEEL-T22 · Frame rate choice and stability
- **what:** Decide the frame rate early and keep game speed independent of drawing.
- **how:** 60 fps is ideal; 30 fps is acceptable and frees half the rendering budget. A variable frame rate (fewer redraws) is not slowdown. Context: in the CRT era NTSC games could run at 60 via interlacing; PAL ran at 50, so games that counted time in screen interrupts ran at 5/6 speed. For timing-heavy games, hold a stable 60 and cut content until it holds (Smash for 3DS). High refresh only matters if the monitor supports it.
- **when:** Pre-production, ports and performance passes.
- **pitfalls:** Logic tied to refresh rate; dropping to 30 late; confusing frame drops with slowdown.
- **episodes:** 5;135

### FEEL-T23 · Transition and wait audit
- **what:** Cut every second the player isn't in control.
- **how:** Time a full session in three buckets: in control, not in control (cutscenes, loads, forced animation) and empty. Trim every fade and black screen to 'a hasty speed' but keep a short transition. Turn loading covers into in-world moments or themed wipes; consider random wipes for screens seen often. Use quick, smooth scrolling for screen-to-screen moves with a fixed camera. When a load can't be hidden, show a simple world-styled indicator at once, and never time presentation to dev-build loads.
- **when:** Throughout development, especially near content lock.
- **pitfalls:** Fades nobody timed; hard cuts with no transition; loading icons over disguised transitions.
- **episodes:** 246;73;78

### FEEL-T24 · Quick retry loop
- **what:** Get the player from failure back to play fast.
- **how:** Measure death-to-control time. Play a flashy, impactful failure effect of about a second (Sakurai's 'a second or so', genre-dependent), then return control. Replace auto-playing screens with player-advanced ones. Place respawn points where enemies can't hit immediately, and keep some cost so respawning isn't mindless.
- **when:** Any game with failure states.
- **pitfalls:** Long death animations; spawn camping; zero-cost respawns at the death point.
- **episodes:** 117

### FEEL-T25 · Speed units and parameter notation
- **what:** Make every speed and timing unambiguous to the whole team.
- **how:** Use pixels per frame (p/f) for sprite games (Sakurai guesses Mario's top speed in Mario Bros. at about 1 p/f on a 256-pixel-wide NES screen); km/h or distance per frame for real-scale 3D. In specs, bracket each tunable value in plain language with its unit, e.g. '[Special startup X-velocity is O]' and '[Special X-deceleration is O/frame]', and keep a separate list pairing each phrase with its value.
- **when:** Specs and parameter tables.
- **pitfalls:** Unitless speeds; reusing pixel speeds across platforms with different resolutions.
- **episodes:** 84;68

### FEEL-T26 · Button redundancy and responsive cutscenes
- **what:** Make sure no natural press is ignored.
- **how:** In menus let more than one button confirm (for example the main confirm plus L, which also allows one-handed play). In simple action games put the same function on several buttons rather than leaving them dead. During cutscenes respond to any press, at least with a skip prompt, and allow pausing. Let several buttons share an action in remapping (Smash has two jump buttons).
- **when:** Menus, simple action games, cutscenes.
- **pitfalls:** Assigning every last button for its own sake; ambiguous responses.
- **episodes:** 104;197;180

### FEEL-T27 · Remapping screen that works for groups
- **what:** Let any player change controls quickly and safely.
- **how:** Draw the controller graphically; pick the button with the cursor, then its action (so the exit button can't be accidentally reassigned); let several buttons share an action; save settings under a profile name and, where possible, on the controller itself. Confirm remaps with a long press and show each action's default.
- **when:** Every game, including simple ones.
- **pitfalls:** Press-to-assign screens that capture the exit button; vertical text lists that hide what each button does.
- **episodes:** 197;55;175

### FEEL-T28 · Comfort and readability options
- **what:** Keep presentation for those who enjoy it while protecting those who get sick.
- **how:** Add toggles for footstep vertical shake and other inertial camera effects; a mode that locks vehicle or cockpit frames to the screen; a motion-blur strength slider. Test with people prone to motion sickness. A 'the screen isn't moving' reference element doesn't help much at the edge and is intrusive in the middle.
- **when:** First-person and 3D third-person games, vehicle games.
- **pitfalls:** Stripping effects for everyone; signing off camera comfort with a team that never gets sick.
- **episodes:** 129;192

### FEEL-T29 · Screen-space budgeting
- **what:** Spend the small window on play.
- **how:** Treat the screen as roughly a 30-degree slice of vision. Compare candidate views by how much screen they leave for play. If targets are tiny, scale them up: Kid Icarus: Uprising's standard enemies are four or five times Pit's size.
- **when:** Camera and FOV decisions; handheld games.
- **pitfalls:** Cockpit views that eat play space; tiny distant targets.
- **episodes:** 42;125

### FEEL-T30 · Touch aiming with camera inertia
- **what:** Fast, precise aiming on a touch screen.
- **how:** Treat the touch screen like a trackball: a quick swipe gives the camera inertia so it keeps swinging, a tap stops it, a drag fine-tunes, and sensitivity is adjustable.
- **when:** Touch-screen shooters and camera control.
- **pitfalls:** No way to stop the spin precisely; fixed sensitivity.
- **episodes:** 125

### FEEL-T31 · One-button design patterns
- **what:** Get a whole game out of one input before adding more.
- **how:** Choose a pattern: rapid tapping (tiring, favors natural tappers, out of fashion); timing (press or release at the right moment, e.g. Crane Fever); reaction (press on a signal; in versus, make the signal feintable); alternating hold and release (hold to catch eggs, release to avoid bombs). Hold-only has little game essence.
- **when:** Minigames, mobile, accessibility modes, core-action prototypes.
- **pitfalls:** Mashing as the core skill; hold-only mechanics.
- **episodes:** 51

### FEEL-T32 · Tempo and mode retune
- **what:** Rebuild feel values whenever the game's speed or context changes.
- **how:** When raising or lowering tempo, plan to retune every movement and combat value, including jumps and knockback (Ultimate reused none of Smash for's). Give characters separate jump and movement sets for very different modes (Brawl's Subspace Emissary). Let the weakest screen cap launch speeds (Smash for 3DS). Lower input density and speed for casual audiences and laggy online play (Brawl).
- **when:** Sequels, ports, new modes.
- **pitfalls:** Porting old values; one parameter set for incompatible modes.
- **episodes:** 160;108;135

### FEEL-T33 · Feel-tuning pipeline and debug aids
- **what:** Make trying a new value take seconds.
- **how:** Keep tunable values in an external table (Smash uses Excel), hide rarely used fields, link related values with formulas, and push edits to all dev kits with one button. Add debug commands for frequent setups (set every fighter to a given damage), slow motion with backgrounds hidden for reviewing effects, and visualizations like a knockback trajectory guide. Prototype feel with a single controllable sprite and change inertia or acceleration one value at a time.
- **when:** From prototype to final tuning.
- **pitfalls:** Hard-coded values; ten-minute conversions; tuning without playing.
- **episodes:** 46;68;195;79;118

## Animation

### ANIM-T01 · Four-part attack structure
- **what:** Build every attack as standby, lead-in, attack and follow-through.
- **how:** Standby: the pose everything starts from; nail the ground and aerial standbys first and don't change them. Lead-in: often a big, unexpected pose on the very first frame. Attack: the key pose when the hit can register, plus a little motion before and after; hit stop freezes it. Follow-through: the opening after the hit, kept very distinct from standby and held as long as possible, with cancel frames late in it.
- **when:** Every attack animation.
- **pitfalls:** Changing standby poses after other animations are built on them; follow-throughs that look like standby.
- **episodes:** 36;77

### ANIM-T02 · Four-value attack request
- **what:** Give animators enough to start without over-specifying.
- **how:** For each new attack, specify: the standby pose, the attack pose, the attack start frame, and the total frames (the time until the attack can be canceled). The lead-in fills the gap between standby and the attack frame, the follow-through the gap between the attack and the total. Add direction notes as needed. Keep an animation list for the whole character: text description, length and when the attack occurs for every animation, 'Loop' or 'Any' for idles and walks, with photographed figure poses for key poses (Smash averages 70 to 80 pages per fighter).
- **when:** Pre-production and every new move request.
- **pitfalls:** Timing in words; specs nobody can verify by feel.
- **episodes:** 36;14

### ANIM-T03 · Snap-on-press lead-in
- **what:** Make the input visible on the first frame.
- **how:** Do not interpolate from standby. On the first frame after input, show a drastically different windup pose. For charge attacks hold that pose and shake in place while charging. Light, spammable moves can skip the lead-in (Zero Suit Samus's neutral attack is out one frame after input). If the windup feels slow, pull directly into the pose. Remember the windup also covers online lag and adds visual impact on heavy moves.
- **when:** All player-initiated attacks.
- **pitfalls:** Easing out of idle; tiny first-frame changes that don't read.
- **episodes:** 49;36;253

### ANIM-T04 · Hold the anticipation, flash through the key poses
- **what:** Spend frames on the windup and the strike, not on in-betweens.
- **how:** Pull the windup further back than the reference, hold it relatively long, then pass through each key pose in as little as one frame. Cut interpolated in-betweens so key frames arrive quickly. Give poses more energy than the source. Judge by eye: a kick that felt slow at 8 frames with straight interpolation felt much snappier at the same 8 frames once reworked.
- **when:** Fast attacks; converting 2D or reference animation to 3D.
- **pitfalls:** Default tool interpolation; using frame count as the only speed check.
- **episodes:** 150

### ANIM-T05 · Overshoot and pull back
- **what:** Make even frame-by-frame animation lively.
- **how:** Place one frame that goes past the target pose, then settle back to the pose on the next frame. Sakurai has done this since the NES: in Kirby's Adventure's Needle ability, frame three goes too far and frame four pulls back.
- **when:** Sprite and linear animation; strikes and extensions in 3D too.
- **pitfalls:** Overshoots so long they read as a different pose.
- **episodes:** 28

### ANIM-T06 · Line the strongest pose up with the first clean-hit frame
- **what:** Make sure hit stop freezes a good pose.
- **how:** Decide when collision starts and what pose the character is in at a likely clean hit; that is where hit stop happens and, on a finishing blow, where the screen stops completely. On sword swings, start collision partway through the swing, once the pose is strong, so hit stop kicks in after the blade has swung out a bit. Include the contact moment in reference photos.
- **when:** Every attack, especially weapon swings.
- **pitfalls:** Collision from the first frame of motion; weak poses frozen on screen.
- **episodes:** 225;62

### ANIM-T07 · Damage pose recipe and reaction set
- **what:** Make being hit instantly readable.
- **how:** Make the first damage frame (the one hit stop holds) an extreme pain pose unlike standby. Segue into it from the pose at impact with a quick interpolation, about four frames in Smash, not an instant snap. Build reactions as high, middle and low (chosen by the point closest to impact) times small, medium and large strength on the ground (nine types), with a separate set of knockback variations. Twist the torso in knockback so limbs read.
- **when:** Any game where characters take hits.
- **pitfalls:** Damage poses close to standby; stance changes that slide the target back out of combos.
- **episodes:** 142;47

### ANIM-T08 · Big follow-through on its own path
- **what:** Use the recovery to show the weight of the strike.
- **how:** Don't interpolate straight back to standby. Keep the motion going as long as possible with large movements that carry the strike's momentum. Give it a different trajectory from the attack instead of playing the attack backwards. Keep it visibly unlike standby until the cancel frames. Give landings a full follow-through.
- **when:** Attack recoveries, landings.
- **pitfalls:** Fizzling recoveries; reversed playback in 3D.
- **episodes:** 77;150;253

### ANIM-T09 · Short blends for cancels and segues
- **what:** Transition naturally without losing snap.
- **how:** When an input arrives during cancel frames, blend from the canceled pose into the new animation. Keep blend durations short and set blend strength deliberately; check automatic blends for distorted poses. For directional moves, blend a fixed center stance with poses placed around a circle by stick direction (Smash's shield shifting). In full 3D, layer upper and lower body, adapt legs to terrain and let slopes affect turns.
- **when:** Cancels, locomotion starts, damage segues, directional stances.
- **pitfalls:** Long blends that kill rhythm; distorted blends left unchecked.
- **episodes:** 194

### ANIM-T10 · Collision overlay review
- **what:** Keep animation and collision in agreement.
- **how:** Show animators each attack's active frames and hitbox placement and review attacks with collision displayed. Make every attack's range cross the median line where opponents stand. Keep attacks centered in the gameplay plane. In fast games make collision a bit wider than the weapon, but keep the animation positioned where the hitbox is. When a hit feels off, adjust both.
- **when:** Every attack review.
- **pitfalls:** Stretching collision to meet a misplaced animation; attacks angled toward or away from the camera.
- **episodes:** 225;165;62

### ANIM-T11 · Silhouette and posing checklist
- **what:** Concrete posing rules Sakurai uses.
- **how:** Exaggerate. Break left-right symmetry unless comic, and don't face straight forward. Twist the torso instead of bending it sideways. Lower the chin and tilt the head to avoid right angles. Give torso, hips and legs different lines (an S curve). Square and slightly raise the shoulders. Set the arms at different bent angles close to the torso; bend wrists slightly forward. Shift the center of gravity off the middle. Turn the feet slightly outward at different depths from the camera. Let cloth and wings show flow and weight. Check all four limbs read in profile, on both screen sides, and filled in solid black.
- **when:** Key poses, hit poses, damage poses, character art.
- **pitfalls:** Polishing details while the base pose is off.
- **episodes:** 106;62;142;243

### ANIM-T12 · Exaggeration pass
- **what:** Push past 'OK' deliberately.
- **how:** When a pose looks OK, push it further until the rig's weighting is close to breaking. Treat motion capture as a starting point and exaggerate it before it ships. For faces, once it feels done go a step further; show eye whites above and below wide-open eyes and offset upper and lower teeth. Re-check at the farthest gameplay camera distance and push anything that reads as understated.
- **when:** Attacks, damage, cutscenes, facial animation.
- **pitfalls:** Exaggeration so big it fights the game's control feel; overriding a source character's limits.
- **episodes:** 28;91;166

### ANIM-T13 · Squash and stretch with volume
- **what:** Add scale to give life and touch.
- **how:** Squash on landings, crouches and bounces, and widen on the other axes so mass stays roughly constant. On 3D models, sculpt the squashed shape with side volume (an oval-track profile) or a blend shape instead of scaling Y only. Compare side by side with and without scaling.
- **when:** Landings, crouches, impacts, bouncy objects.
- **pitfalls:** Vertical-only squash that loses presence.
- **episodes:** 121

### ANIM-T14 · Two-axis rotation and linked joints
- **what:** Make turns and twists look organic.
- **how:** Add an X-axis dip or Z-axis tilt to every Y-axis turn of a head, wrist or torso. Move the shoulder and collarbone with a twisting wrist. Offset the timing of the two axes slightly and add an occasional counter-motion instead of trusting the software's curves. Choose a downward, upward or level path on purpose for personality. Plan the eye line, then reduce the head turn.
- **when:** Any non-mechanical rotation.
- **pitfalls:** Robotic head turns; drill-like wrists.
- **episodes:** 251

### ANIM-T15 · Off-center pivots and wobble loops
- **what:** Avoid bland straight lines and perfect spins.
- **how:** Shift the rotation axis of thrown or spinning objects slightly off center (Smash Ultimate's bomb); move a swung weapon's pivot off the grip (Pyra's Blazing End). Layer a small positional loop on straight projectiles: Shotzo's six-frame sequence up, right-up, right-down, down, left-down, left-up, then loop.
- **when:** Projectiles, thrown items, spinning weapons.
- **pitfalls:** Wobble so large it confuses the projectile's real path or hitbox.
- **episodes:** 255

### ANIM-T16 · Firing recoil and short explosion sequences
- **what:** Make shooting feel forceful.
- **how:** On each shot, snap the gun or cannon back and return it (Star Force's side cannons pull back 'bam, bam, bam' with each missile). Author explosions as short, deliberate frame sequences: Star Force's five-frame explosion goes from a flash of light to smoke and disappears.
- **when:** Shooters, any weapon fire.
- **pitfalls:** Static weapons; single-sprite explosions.
- **episodes:** 156;56

### ANIM-T17 · Flipped animation for side views
- **what:** Keep faces visible from both sides.
- **how:** Mirror poses and movements when a character changes facing instead of rotating the model 180 degrees. Angle side-view runs diagonally toward the camera. Plan for right-handed punches becoming left-handed and items switching hands quickly. Skip it for characters without horizontal symmetry, such as sword fighters. Design lettering and emblems to survive mirroring.
- **when:** 2D and 2.5D side-view games with expressive characters.
- **pitfalls:** Item and handedness glitches left unplanned.
- **episodes:** 181

### ANIM-T18 · Make blade trails read through the swing animation
- **what:** Trails are only as good as the arc the animation gives them.
- **how:** Build trails as a polygon ribbon swept behind the blade with a translucent fading tail. Check every swing from the gameplay camera; if the trail looks angular or nearly vanishes from the side, change the swing animation; plain horizontal slashes rarely work. For swings that finish in one frame, show a single flat 2D trail image, which hit stop holds long enough to read. Add similar trails to punches and kicks.
- **when:** Weapon and limb attacks.
- **pitfalls:** Trusting the raw blade path; one generic trail for every weapon.
- **episodes:** 179

### ANIM-T19 · Grips and fingers
- **what:** Make held objects look held.
- **how:** For objects swung with the forearm, pose a diagonal grip across the fingers, not a perpendicular one. When processing is tight, lock finger joints so they still look like they could move and swap between a few pre-made finger patterns.
- **when:** Weapons, tools, crowded scenes.
- **pitfalls:** Action-figure grips; simulating every finger on busy screens.
- **episodes:** 210

### ANIM-T20 · Fast locomotion and landings
- **what:** Keep fast movement believable.
- **how:** For speeds a normal leg cycle can't match, use bounding or sliding (Kid Icarus: Uprising's Pit). Give too-fast secondary motion more frames (Kazuya's wings retracted in two frames on landing and needed more). Shift weight forward in walks. Keep feet from slipping on landing.
- **when:** Fast characters, landings, secondary motion.
- **pitfalls:** Sped-up realistic cycles; foot sliding.
- **episodes:** 84;165;253

### ANIM-T21 · Variant and stance QA
- **what:** Catch the mismatches between versions of a move.
- **how:** Play ground and aerial versions of every special side by side; compare poses and reach, including when aimed upward. Review poses on the left and right of the screen. Run multi-hit strings against each damage-reaction stance and fix root or top-node offsets that push targets away.
- **when:** Before locking any move set.
- **pitfalls:** Checking only the default version.
- **episodes:** 253;142;62

### ANIM-T22 · Figure-based pose reference
- **what:** Find and communicate poses physically.
- **how:** Pose a physical figure, photograph it, cut out the shape, adjust the contrast and paste it into the spec. Practice posing and 'bundodo' (zooming the figure around by hand) to train your eye. In feedback, show a posed figure or a retouched screenshot next to the original.
- **when:** Specs and reviews.
- **pitfalls:** Text-only pose feedback.
- **episodes:** 14;106;253

### ANIM-T23 · Animation inspection tools
- **what:** See the animation without obstruction.
- **how:** Add a developer-mode option that makes any part of a model, or all of it, translucent or hidden (for capes, clothes and swaying parts), plus on/off toggles for each display element. Record effects and moves in slow motion with backgrounds disabled and step through them frame by frame.
- **when:** Animation and effects review.
- **pitfalls:** Scaling or deleting the cape in the model just to check a pose.
- **episodes:** 217;79

### ANIM-T24 · Profile mouths and cel swaps
- **what:** Cheap, readable mouth motion in profile.
- **how:** In profile shots, try putting the mouth on the side of the face (anime style) and move it by swapping mouth cels or textures instead of animating the jaw. For cartoon squeezes through gaps that a skeleton can't do, try shape animation.
- **when:** Stylized characters, cutscenes.
- **pitfalls:** Rejecting the trick because it isn't anatomically correct.
- **episodes:** 232

## Visuals, characters and effects

### VIS-T01 · Collision highlight view
- **what:** A debug view that colors every surface with collision, so you can check that terrain reads against the backdrop.
- **how:** Render all colliding geometry in one flat highlight color (Sakurai's example marks it in red). Play the stage and confirm each highlighted surface is instantly distinguishable in the normal view. Push non-colliding scenery back with aerial perspective, then check the stage still looks appealing. Tell artists which pieces are walkable before they start.
- **when:** Every stage or level review, especially in perspective 3D views where collision is hard to read.
- **pitfalls:** Relying on aerial perspective alone, which hurts the overall look. Elaborate backdrops outshining the terrain.
- **episodes:** 30

### VIS-T02 · Grayscale and desaturation check
- **what:** Convert screenshots or background art to black and white to see what actually stands out.
- **how:** Desaturate full screenshots and background art. Tone down any background element that is brighter or more contrasty than the foreground. Look for fighters in shadow, platforms and costumes that disappear. It works like flipping your artwork: problems show before they spread.
- **when:** Early background art, and every stage polish pass.
- **pitfalls:** Checking assets individually instead of the whole screen.
- **episodes:** 214;165

### VIS-T03 · Color-blindness filter pass
- **what:** Simulate color-vision deficiencies on every color-coded element.
- **how:** Run screenshots through a color-blindness filter (built into Photoshop), including protanopia. Where hues look alike, give them significantly different brightness. Keep them bright enough not to vanish into the background. Recheck per level or theme if the elements change look, as Meteos blocks did per planet.
- **when:** Any game where pieces, power-ups, teams or interactive objects are told apart by color.
- **pitfalls:** Making pieces so dark they're lost against the background. Checking single colors when the elements look different in each context.
- **episodes:** 214

### VIS-T04 · Solid-black silhouette test
- **what:** Judge character and pose readability by silhouette alone.
- **how:** Fill each character in solid black and line up the cast. Exaggerate any form that is ambiguous or close to another character's. For key poses (the hitstop pose, the first damage frame), check from the game's main view, with all four limbs visible, mirrored for both screen sides. Twist torsos and break symmetry.
- **when:** Character concept, model review and every key pose.
- **pitfalls:** Symmetric, front-facing poses. Plain sideways torso bends. Features rendered at realistic proportions.
- **episodes:** 243;62;142;106

### VIS-T05 · Brighten the extremities
- **what:** Make hands, feet or socks brighter so a character's movement reads at a glance.
- **how:** When choosing costume colors, put lighter values on the extremities, for example white socks on a darker outfit.
- **when:** Character and costume color design, especially for characters that are small on screen.
- **episodes:** 158

### VIS-T06 · Effect contrast recipe
- **what:** Make effects pop on any background.
- **how:** Put crisp polygons or simple images at the focus. Use additive glow as support only. Mix in dark elements or a dark backdrop. Check every effect on your lightest and darkest stages. On tight hardware, mix white and black pixels.
- **when:** Every effect, especially hit marks, explosions and magic.
- **pitfalls:** All-bright effects. Darkening the whole game instead of fixing the effect.
- **episodes:** 16;131;253;244

### VIS-T07 · Seven-point hit-mark checklist
- **what:** Sakurai's criteria for a good hit mark.
- **how:** 1) Flashiness matches the impact. 2) Visual balance: not only additive blending or weak contrast. 3) Position matches the hit, where attack and target hitboxes meet. 4) A variety of elements: main flash, shockwave behind the character, upward spray, sparks for mechanical targets, without clutter. 5) It doesn't eclipse the characters. 6) Duration fits the hit: short for quick repeated hits, and even long ones resolved fairly quickly. 7) Contrasting brightness, with dark colors so it reads on light backgrounds. Keep the mark animating through hitstop.
- **when:** Every attack in a combat game.
- **pitfalls:** Fixing occlusion by lowering the mark's draw priority, which can leave only terrain visible. Cartoony marks used unchanged on realistic subjects.
- **episodes:** 131

### VIS-T08 · Hit flash with internal contrast
- **what:** Flash a character without losing its readability.
- **how:** Instead of alternating normal colors with a white-added version, add shadows or dark elements into the flash so the character keeps internal contrast. Scale strength with camera distance: more visible when zoomed out, restrained up close so little of the screen flashes. Keep it to a single brief moment. Check it on real LCDs.
- **when:** Hit reactions and status flashes.
- **pitfalls:** Uniform additive white, which averages dark and light areas together and vanishes on light backgrounds.
- **episodes:** 223

### VIS-T09 · Status flash color code
- **what:** Fixed meanings for flash colors and lighting.
- **how:** Smash Ultimate's code: white flash means invulnerable (for example while getting up); yellow flash marks the window after a throw when the character can't be grabbed again; rim lighting shows certain damage types and status changes such as invulnerability. Keep each meaning consistent across the whole cast.
- **when:** Any game with temporary character states.
- **pitfalls:** Reusing one color for several meanings.
- **episodes:** 223

### VIS-T10 · Four-phase explosion
- **what:** Build explosions as flash, blast, smoke and conclusion.
- **how:** Flash: instant, light rays much longer than the blast range, black elements mixed in, a shockwave and a flare. Blast: crisp flames leaping from the center, with sparks that keep the momentum; the hit is active for only part of it. Smoke: black smoke takes over while the flames are drawn waning. Conclusion: let it end naturally, with sparkles flickering one after another. Extras: a screen flash or shake where it suits. Ground version: the blast spreads upward and the shockwave follows the floor. Even Star Force (1984) went flash to smoke to gone in five frames.
- **when:** Bombs, projectiles, KO bursts, destruction.
- **pitfalls:** A single fire sprite. Cutting the smoke short. A pure white flash on white backgrounds.
- **episodes:** 56;156

### VIS-T11 · Keep the character visible inside a surrounding effect
- **what:** Shape auras and special-move effects so the character can still be seen.
- **how:** Leave room around the body. Make the parts of the effect that sit between camera and character lower than the parts behind (Chrom's Up Special peaks differ in height front and back). Add an outline to the character model. In 2D, simply draw characters above effects; in 3D, avoid forced draw priority.
- **when:** Auras, charge effects, special and super moves.
- **pitfalls:** Forced draw priority in 3D, which creates depth disparity.
- **episodes:** 35

### VIS-T12 · Slow-motion, background-off effect review
- **what:** Study effects frame by frame in isolation.
- **how:** Add debug options for slow motion, hiding the background, toggling each display element on and off, and making chosen model parts translucent. Record your own effects and reference games, and step through them. A/B a key scene with effects off.
- **when:** Effect development and reviews.
- **pitfalls:** Judging effects only at full speed.
- **episodes:** 79;217

### VIS-T13 · Blade trail construction
- **what:** Two ways to draw sword trails.
- **how:** Method 1 (common): sweep polygons behind the blade like a folding fan, with the sword as the leading edge and the tail made translucent so it fades. Method 2 (rare): for swings that finish in one frame, show a single flat 2D image for that instant; hitstop holds it on screen if the attack lands. Correct the animations so trails read (plain horizontal slashes usually don't). Add a similar blur to punches and kicks. Vary the trail per weapon.
- **when:** Melee weapons and fast limbs.
- **pitfalls:** Angular trails that trace the raw path. Trails almost invisible from the side.
- **episodes:** 179

### VIS-T14 · Particle economy rules
- **what:** Sakurai's team rules for particles.
- **how:** Short lifetimes, made up for with higher speed. Several particles per billboard panel. Somewhat large billboards rather than many tiny ones. Staggered spawn timing between emitters. Offset positions so particles don't land on top of each other. When the CPU is starved, hand-placed animation frames or random picks from a few preset motion patterns.
- **when:** Any effect with many particles, on any hardware.
- **pitfalls:** Copying lavish effects from a game with different hardware, camera distance or number of hit effects.
- **episodes:** 244

### VIS-T15 · Billboard use and checks
- **what:** Use camera-facing flat images for cheap effects and objects.
- **how:** Use two-polygon billboards for flashes, explosions and particles, and layer sparks on top. Consider a hybrid: billboard body with 3D-modeled parts. Orbit the camera around every billboarded object; if it looks wrong from angles players will see, use a model. Use Y-axis billboards only where an upright orientation makes sense.
- **when:** Cheap effects, and simple or distant objects.
- **pitfalls:** Billboarding objects seen from many angles. Effects that look too skinny from misused Y-axis billboards.
- **episodes:** 153

### VIS-T16 · Fire density fix
- **what:** Stop big flames from reading as candles.
- **how:** Place the fire next to a character. If a flame that size reads as a candle or torch, raise the number of flame loops or switch to smaller, more detailed particles. Give the effects team a reference image showing the density you want. Spend texture memory on quick, fierce motion rather than detail.
- **when:** Large fire, and any effect at character scale or bigger.
- **pitfalls:** Eating texture memory chasing detail that motion hides anyway.
- **episodes:** 201

### VIS-T17 · Lighting a repeated asset
- **what:** Turn a copy-pasted group of assets into a lit scene.
- **how:** Shine colored light down on the group for contrast. Spray to brighten spots that would get more light. Vary hue slightly across copies. Add atmosphere for depth, more carefully than a flat fog layer. Optionally add a brightness filter in the direction of sunlight.
- **when:** Forests, prop clusters, tiled environments.
- **pitfalls:** Calling base-colored assets finished.
- **episodes:** 10

### VIS-T18 · Five-minute retouch
- **what:** A paintover that directs a background's color and light.
- **how:** In Photoshop: duplicate the layer, boost contrast, shift the hue, erase the hue-shifted layer where light would hit, then adjust the colors to work together. Send it side by side with the original. It takes about 5 minutes, 10 at most; he uses only a mouse.
- **when:** Feedback on palette, contrast, mood and lighting.
- **pitfalls:** Treating the retouch as final art. Trying to retouch everything.
- **episodes:** 90

### VIS-T19 · Scale material pass
- **what:** Make large objects read as large, or small ones as miniatures on purpose.
- **how:** Enlarge texture loops and painted detail and view from farther away. Lower saturation on realistic textures. Limit gloss area and strength. Match normal maps to real size. Add dirt to big mechanical objects and mixed hues to monotone plating. Lower saturation and let bluish haze build with distance. Avoid shallow depth of field. Do this after the game's atmosphere is set. To make something look like a miniature deliberately, reverse it: blur top and bottom, raise saturation, reduce blue.
- **when:** Big set pieces, vistas, vehicles, buildings.
- **pitfalls:** Fixing the shape first. Judging scale texture by texture.
- **episodes:** 63;174

### VIS-T20 · Meet-in-the-middle style pass
- **what:** Sakurai's five adjustments for characters from different art styles.
- **how:** 1) Color: render bright primaries slightly paler and more responsive to light. 2) Texture density: add detail to flat cartoony characters, ease texture on realistic ones. 3) Proportions: small tweaks where matchups look ridiculous, judged in game. 4) Refined design detail for everyone, so everyone is treated the same. 5) Believability: ask how the character would look and behave if it really existed. Get rights holders' permission. Apply the same thinking to animation, effects and audio.
- **when:** Crossovers, licensed casts, assets from mixed sources.
- **pitfalls:** Importing official designs unchanged.
- **episodes:** 138;130

### VIS-T21 · Image boards
- **what:** A few key paintings that set the look before production.
- **how:** Have your strongest artist paint a small set showing the world, scenery, characters and items. Build and review every model and the lighting against them. Budget for style unification, which costs more as visual fidelity rises.
- **when:** Pre-production.
- **pitfalls:** Letting every artist work in a personal style.
- **episodes:** 130

### VIS-T22 · Stage contrast and lighting sweep
- **what:** A checklist drawn from his Sora-era stage reviews.
- **how:** The same material has strong contrast in the foreground and weaker contrast in the background. Character lighting isn't too dark. Fighters don't blend into busy surfaces like stained glass. Drop-through platforms are distinct and don't change hue with position because of reflections. The floor isn't so contrasty that projectiles vanish. Water reads as water (Rising Falls pushed toward green). Distant information density is balanced, not necessarily symmetric. Surfaces that look flat from far away get gradation.
- **when:** Every stage polish pass.
- **pitfalls:** Backwards contrast, stronger in the background than the foreground, which he says comes up a lot.
- **episodes:** 253;165

### VIS-T23 · Team color calibration
- **what:** Remove monitor color disparity inside the team.
- **how:** Put one reference monitor where everyone can see it and calibrate each monitor by eye against it. Finish with a visual check, since games end up on ordinary TVs. Stop using monitors that lose shadows or contrast. Light the room neutrally.
- **when:** Project start, and whenever hardware changes.
- **pitfalls:** Yellow office lighting, which pushed one company's art bluish. Trusting calibration devices alone.
- **episodes:** 184

### VIS-T24 · Motion blur done right
- **what:** Show speed with blur without hurting readability.
- **how:** Swap in a pre-drawn blurred texture on spinning or fast parts while they move. Build blur into the texture of parts that always move. Use blur to bridge gaps between fast frames and to soften the background during fast camera moves. Keep it subtle, and add a blur-strength option.
- **when:** Wheels, rotors, fast limbs, camera pans.
- **pitfalls:** Fuzzy visuals that hide what's happening.
- **episodes:** 192

### VIS-T25 · Target sizing for the screen
- **what:** Scale what players must hit and watch.
- **how:** Treat the screen as about 30 degrees of the player's vision. Scale enemies up when they look tiny; Kid Icarus: Uprising's standard enemies are four to five times Pit's size. Make beginner bosses big enough that slightly off-target attacks still connect.
- **when:** Shooters, handhelds, beginner-friendly games.
- **pitfalls:** Realistic cockpit-style views that leave little screen for play. Tiny distant targets.
- **episodes:** 42;125;100

### VIS-T26 · Detail budget by visibility
- **what:** Allocate detail by where players look.
- **how:** Put skysphere detail in front of the camera. Detail the playable surface more than off-screen foundations. Budget models by on-screen size, raised for close-up uses. Build LOD only when worth the labor. Build stage art for the worst-case load (Ultimate: eight fighters at 60 fps on Switch).
- **when:** Environment production and optimization.
- **pitfalls:** Unseen details, such as backdrop tires that actually turn.
- **episodes:** 44;99

### VIS-T27 · Believable breakage and plausibility sweep
- **what:** Make broken and moving scenery look natural.
- **how:** For broken wood: avoid perpendicular, sawn-looking cuts; give the fronts and backs of boards jagged edges; keep one axis flat so boards don't twist like metal; break up even spacing; don't copy-paste splinters; raise splinters out of the play space; check from gameplay distance. For every stage: windmills turn with the wind, clouds and rocking move naturally and not too much, debris is spread out, light shafts fall where ceilings are broken, environmental wind flutters cloth, and explosions make small gusts.
- **when:** Destructible objects, and every stage's final pass.
- **pitfalls:** Too-even, manufactured-looking breaks. Edges that come out rounded like stalactites.
- **episodes:** 224;165;205

### VIS-T28 · Manga-style profile mouth
- **what:** Express faces in profile the anime way.
- **how:** Place the mouth on the side of the face in profile shots and swap mouth cels instead of animating a jaw. Compare it with the anatomical version from the angles players see. For squash and stretch through gaps, try shape (blend-shape) animation.
- **when:** Stylized characters, cutscene profile shots.
- **pitfalls:** Rejecting it because it isn't realistic.
- **episodes:** 232

### VIS-T29 · Pixel-art basics
- **what:** Rules for small, low-color canvases.
- **how:** Kirby is a 16x16 sprite; draw circles at 15x15 so they have center lines. Outline plus highlight reads as a bubble; a mesh (dither) makes a shape look flat, and flipping the mesh changes the feel; solid black plus highlight reads as a cannonball. Spend a third color on edge softening (check it against the real background) or a floor reflection. Stacking two 1-bit layers gives three colors plus transparency. Checkered patterns look different on a CRT.
- **when:** Pixel-art and retro-style games.
- **pitfalls:** Even-sized small circles. Mesh shading on something meant to read as round.
- **episodes:** 115;183;252

### VIS-T30 · Mirror-safe side-view design
- **what:** Make art survive horizontal flipping.
- **how:** Mirror animation sets when characters turn so they face the camera from both sides, and angle side-view moves such as a run slightly toward the camera. Design lettering and emblems that still read mirrored. Plan for right-handed moves becoming left-handed and held items switching hands. Skip mirroring for characters that aren't horizontally symmetrical. To save memory, build look-alike variants from shared or flipped halves.
- **when:** Side-view games.
- **pitfalls:** Expressive characters showing the back of their head on one side of the screen.
- **episodes:** 181;100

### VIS-T31 · Ground and air variant check
- **what:** Check every effect and move in each context it appears in.
- **how:** Make separate ground and air versions of explosions. Play the ground and aerial versions of every special side by side; check the poses match and that range doesn't shrink in some directions, such as when aimed upward.
- **when:** Special moves and explosions.
- **pitfalls:** Drastically different poses between versions. Direction-dependent range bugs.
- **episodes:** 56;253

## Audio

### AUD-T01 · Relevance volume ladder
- **what:** A ranked volume order for an action game's sound effects.
- **how:** Smash's order, loudest first: KO sound; hits (giving and taking) and perfect shields; special-move activation sounds and voices (voices cut through, so balance them); explosions midway between hits and specials, louder the bigger; dodges, shield blocks and ledge grabs; attack swings without impact, and stick-flick dashes; landings (louder than jumps); jumps; walking; ambience lowest. Scale specials and explosions by strength within their tier.
- **when:** Setting up the mix for any action game; rebuild the tiers from your own game's priorities.
- **pitfalls:** Ordering by the size of the action rather than its relevance.
- **episodes:** 32;204

### AUD-T02 · Strong attack, light reverb envelope
- **what:** The waveform shape for impact sounds.
- **how:** Give the sound a short attack that peaks near the start, reaching a strong level almost immediately rather than literally starting at peak. Keep the reverb tail short. Trim jab and flurry sounds to the shortest length that still reads as consecutive hits. For hits with hitstop, let the main body finish during the freeze. Review in a four- to eight-player mix.
- **when:** Impact sounds, especially in busy multiplayer action.
- **pitfalls:** Long-tailed pre-check sounds approved because they sound strong solo.
- **episodes:** 167

### AUD-T03 · Three-setup listening check
- **what:** Approve sound on the setups players actually use.
- **how:** Listen on headphones, then PC speakers, then a cheap TV, which matters most because it's the most common setup. Watch low-frequency impact. Check good speakers for ambience quality. Built-in handheld speakers are lower priority if they're already clear. Keep the same reference gear for the whole project.
- **when:** Every important sound approval, and before the final mix.
- **pitfalls:** Studio monitors only. Over-correcting for weak speakers until the creative vision is lost.
- **episodes:** 54;204

### AUD-T04 · Audio latency check
- **what:** Catch late sound separately from video lag.
- **how:** Through a home theater system, impact sounds can arrive late. Switch to an analog connection such as a standard audio jack, or low-latency audio settings, and research mismatched equipment. Set displays to Game Mode or Low Latency Mode. At 60 fps, 16.67 ms of delay is one frame.
- **when:** Before demos, playtests, events and footage capture.
- **pitfalls:** Assuming someone else already tested the setup.
- **episodes:** 8

### AUD-T05 · Dead-silence walk
- **what:** Find holes in the atmosphere.
- **how:** Stop in each area and just listen. Add subtle, fairly realistic ambient layers wherever there's dead silence. Confirm they never cover gameplay-critical effects. Check with the volume raised and on good speakers.
- **when:** Each area's audio pass.
- **pitfalls:** Ambience so loud or busy it buries what matters.
- **episodes:** 204

### AUD-T06 · Give every sound a job
- **what:** Decide exaggerated or realistic per sound category.
- **how:** For each category, mark its job: impact (exaggerate) or material, information and place (keep realistic). A/B a real recorded hit against an exaggerated one. When graphics are simple, give materials distinct sounds, such as wood versus stone. Calibrate exaggeration against how realistic the visuals are.
- **when:** Sound-design kickoff, and when adding a new sound family.
- **pitfalls:** Literal realism in combat.
- **episodes:** 89;204

### AUD-T07 · Footstep and landing matrix
- **what:** Movement sounds that keep characters grounded.
- **how:** Give each character unique footsteps. Match landings to footwear or costume (Cloud's change with his costume). Vary walking and landing sounds by floor material. Record several variations and rotate them. Make landings louder than jumps.
- **when:** Character audio in any game with visible movement, especially 3D.
- **pitfalls:** One sample everywhere; footsteps so quiet characters seem to hover.
- **episodes:** 32

### AUD-T08 · Clap-count tempo targeting
- **what:** Set and record a BPM for every scene.
- **how:** Clap along: one clap per second is 60 BPM, two is 120. Smash battle arrangements generally run a little over 120, 130 to 170 on average, with around 200 as rare exceptions. Give each scene its own target. If affordable, tie tempo to state, as the first Dragon Quest slows its dungeon music the deeper you go.
- **when:** Writing the music spec.
- **pitfalls:** Picking tempo by genre default rather than the game's actual speed.
- **episodes:** 25

### AUD-T09 · Main theme and arrangement map
- **what:** Plan a soundtrack around one melody.
- **how:** Write the main theme early. Map the scenes where its arrangements will play. Audit the track list for songs without a hummable melody. For each scene, write one line on what the player should feel and check the music supports it.
- **when:** Music pre-production.
- **pitfalls:** Commissioning many unrelated songs.
- **episodes:** 69

### AUD-T10 · Match-game arrangement brief
- **what:** Smash's rules for arranging battle music.
- **how:** Tempo and energy suited to competition. An intro that plays over '3, 2, 1, GO!', or music that starts after the countdown. If the source piece is short, mix in another song with the main theme. Big shifts, since the game is highly dynamic. A climax at the end. Loop point before or after the intro. Ideally finish within a timed match (2 minutes, 2.5 in Ultimate) with only a little loop.
- **when:** Commissioning stage or battle music.
- **pitfalls:** Loops that restart mid-match; arrangements that ignore the countdown.
- **episodes:** 127

### AUD-T11 · Arrangement review loop
- **what:** Supervise arrangements toward fidelity.
- **how:** Shortlist far more candidate songs than you'll use. Let composers pick pieces they're attached to or confident with, or assign by their strengths. Run several review rounds through a sound director. Most common notes: tempo, intonation, mistranscriptions of the original, overall direction. Play the reference together to agree on the 'main melody'. Keep quirks, mirror hardware-driven structure and keep language versions distinct.
- **when:** Any arrangement of existing music.
- **pitfalls:** Music theory overriding what players heard; revisions that come back opposite to your notes because of a terminology mismatch.
- **episodes:** 127;146

### AUD-T12 · Sound request template
- **what:** Write sound requests that land.
- **how:** 1) The feedback and feeling the sound must give the player. 2) Its role in concrete terms ('strong attack'). 3) A comparison everyone knows ('a jet engine'). 4) A link to a similar example, such as a video clip. 5) Optionally, onomatopoeia as an extra clue.
- **when:** Every sound request, especially remote or by email.
- **pitfalls:** 'More clang' with nothing else; assuming people know a specific sound.
- **episodes:** 185

### AUD-T13 · Reference-track brief
- **what:** Use a reference without getting stuck on it.
- **how:** Attach one reference track. Write what to keep (for example, 'the surfer feel') and what not to copy ('not the famous riff'). Before locking it, list two or three other directions. Judge drafts by the scene, not by closeness to the reference. If an existing song fits the theme exactly, consider licensing it as-is.
- **when:** Composer briefs.
- **pitfalls:** Fixation by the director or the composer; 'make it like this' with no notes.
- **episodes:** 222

### AUD-T14 · Voice session plan
- **what:** Prepare and run voice recording.
- **how:** Script every vocalization: basics (attacks, jumps, damage reactions) and unique lines (special moves, super moves, reveal lines). Prepare reference audio from original games. Cast the original or current series actor. Brief the actor and ease them back into the voice. Direct over talkback. Expect about an hour per Smash fighter, two at most with extra lines. Record banter-heavy casts together when schedules allow. Budget substantial editing afterward. Check how loud voices overlap.
- **when:** Any voiced project.
- **pitfalls:** Missing grunts; no editing time; battle chatter that breaks immersion.
- **episodes:** 107

### AUD-T15 · Channel yield plan
- **what:** Budget limited voices the way Famicom composers did.
- **how:** Decide which music part drops when an effect plays. Famicom roles: two pulse channels for the main music (changing the duty cycle changes texture), a triangle channel for bass, a noise channel for hits, explosions, cheers and waves, and a DPCM channel for samples, which cost cartridge space. Check expansion features per region or hardware.
- **when:** Constrained platforms, retro-style games, any hard polyphony limit.
- **episodes:** 239

### AUD-T16 · Sync music to play segments and trailers
- **what:** Line music up with gameplay and footage timing.
- **how:** Use segments of known length (Kid Icarus: Uprising's air battles last about five minutes) to time music to exact beats and moods. For variable events such as a mid-boss kill, plan cue points where the music waits for or follows the player. Start stage play when the musical intro ends. In trailers, write where the music comes in, cut footage to it, and build the sound from the game's own effects.
- **when:** Scripted segments, set pieces, trailers.
- **episodes:** 125;227;4;163

### AUD-T17 · Crowd and praise sounds
- **what:** Reward players audibly.
- **how:** Cheers for big launches, KOs and the victory screen. A subtle 'oh!' for close saves such as a barely caught ledge. Spectators clapping along to the victory music. Allies or NPCs praising measurable feats.
- **when:** Competitive, party and action games.
- **pitfalls:** Treating item drops as the only praise.
- **episodes:** 95

### AUD-T18 · Implied-world sound
- **what:** Suggest an off-screen world cheaply.
- **how:** Call a handful of simple sound effects tied to events, such as doors clattering open and passengers' feet shuffling, and players perceive activity beyond what's shown.
- **when:** Simulations, and any scene that needs life without new visuals.
- **episodes:** 26

### AUD-T19 · Redundant cue pass
- **what:** Make sure no critical cue lives in one sense only.
- **how:** List every audio warning and give it a visual tell. Offer optional audio cues for traversal and combat and vibration cues for players who need them. Consider vibration matched to the frequency of impact sounds.
- **when:** Accessibility pass and enemy design reviews.
- **pitfalls:** Warnings that exist only as sound.
- **episodes:** 157;55

### AUD-T20 · Early compression test
- **what:** Fit big audio sets on limited media.
- **how:** Test compression on your largest asset sets, such as the music library, before deciding to cut content or move it to a download. Smash Ultimate's cartridge was capped at about 16 GB, and compression fit its music into a quarter of the space it took in Smash for.
- **when:** Early in production on size-limited media.
- **pitfalls:** Cutting content before trying compression.
- **episodes:** 160

## UI and clarity

### UI-T01 · One-press skip and pause for non-interactive sequences
- **what:** Every logo, movie, intermission, cutscene and the credits can be skipped whole or paused.
- **how:** Any button press during a sequence shows a skip prompt; confirm skips the whole sequence. Allow it on a new save. Pause works mid-cutscene. Skippable logos are the default in your own guidelines.
- **when:** Every game with non-interactive content.
- **pitfalls:** Online games that keep advancing may not be able to pause. Don't lock skip behind a first viewing.
- **episodes:** 180;233;104

### UI-T02 · Text that finishes on one press and skips by line
- **what:** Text boxes and voiced scenes move at the reader's pace.
- **how:** First press completes the current box instantly, next press advances. In voiced cutscenes add skip-to-next-line (jumps to the start of the next line) and a whole-scene skip or fast-forward. Where you can, play dialogue as voiced lines during gameplay with an option to turn it off.
- **when:** Any game with dialogue or story scenes.
- **pitfalls:** Most non-Japanese games ship subtitles off by default; he raises this as something to think about, not a rule.
- **episodes:** 211;233;125

### UI-T03 · Key config screen recipe
- **what:** A remapping screen that cannot trap players.
- **how:** Draw the controller graphically with each button labeled by its action. Cursor onto a button, then choose its action from a list (never press-to-assign, which can capture the exit button). Allow several buttons per action, each reassignable. Save under profile names picked before a match; store on the controller where possible. If directions can be bound, confirm changes with a long press so short presses navigate. Show the default button beside each action. Split maps by context (aiming, swimming, minigames, menus). Validate that no essential action is unbound.
- **when:** Every game, however simple.
- **pitfalls:** Console-wide remaps (Switch) affect all games and can leave actions unmapped, so don't rely on them.
- **episodes:** 197;55;175

### UI-T04 · Prompts driven by the live mapping
- **what:** Button prompts and tutorial icons always show the button the player actually uses.
- **how:** Read prompts from the current input map, and draw an icon for every button a player could map an action to. Follow each platform's confirm and cancel placement (Switch is reversed relative to PlayStation and Xbox; PS5 in Japan now uses X to confirm).
- **when:** Any game with on-screen control prompts.
- **pitfalls:** Prompts hardcoded to default buttons break the moment players remap.
- **episodes:** 85;197

### UI-T05 · Dead-button audit and redundant confirm
- **what:** Make every press do something sensible.
- **how:** For each state (menus, gameplay, cutscenes), list every button on the controller and what it does. Make confirm work on the main button plus a shoulder button such as L. In one- or two-action games, put each action on several buttons. In cutscenes, any press brings up the skip prompt. Give each accepted press a sound, highlight or animation.
- **when:** Before any external playtest.
- **pitfalls:** Not every last button needs a function, and deliberate silence is fine.
- **episodes:** 104

### UI-T06 · Help-text line and the no-same-words rule
- **what:** A one-line description for every highlighted menu item.
- **how:** Show the line at the bottom of the screen. Write it without any word from the item's name (Options: Adjust a variety of game settings). Cut words until only the key meaning is left. Name modes by what the player does (Play Solo, not 1P Mode). Replace jargon such as config or key repeat.
- **when:** Every menu.
- **pitfalls:** Literal translation of help text loses meaning; translate meaning.
- **episodes:** 229

### UI-T07 · Mash-confirm path to the main game
- **what:** Pressing confirm repeatedly from the title reaches the main mode.
- **how:** Set default cursor positions on every screen from the title onward so the chain lands in the main mode, tutorial included.
- **when:** Every game; check after any menu change.
- **pitfalls:** It often happens by accident; set it on purpose and re-test after menus change.
- **episodes:** 240

### UI-T08 · Unreadable-language test
- **what:** Check menus are usable without reading.
- **how:** Switch the game to a language you cannot read and try to navigate every menu. Where you get lost, add pictures or characters to icons, or color to sections.
- **when:** Once menus are laid out, and again before release.
- **pitfalls:** Pictures help only somewhat; still write good text.
- **episodes:** 240;133

### UI-T09 · Icon menu sized by frequency
- **what:** Bigger tiles for the most used modes.
- **how:** Rank options by expected use. Largest tiles for the top modes, small for rare ones. Mock labels in the longest target language before locking the grid.
- **when:** Mode-select and main menus where a few options dominate.
- **pitfalls:** Use a plain list when options are used equally; wide languages are hard to fit into icon tiles.
- **episodes:** 147

### UI-T10 · Color-section menus with a color-blindness check
- **what:** Each menu section has its own color, carried into its submenus.
- **how:** Assign a color per top-level section (Smash red, Spirits green in Ultimate). One dominant color per screen. Run screens and color-coded items through a color-blindness filter and separate merging hues by brightness. Convert screens to black and white to check what stands out.
- **when:** Menus and any color-coded gameplay elements.
- **pitfalls:** Heavy color can make a game look aimed at kids; don't darken items until they vanish into the background.
- **episodes:** 133;214

### UI-T11 · Input-count audit for menus
- **what:** Measure and cut the steps to frequent choices.
- **how:** Count inputs from the title to the three most common choices. Support D-pad with crisp one-step moves and a clicky feel per step instead of repeated stick taps. Add optional shortcuts on top of the default path.
- **when:** Whenever menus change.
- **pitfalls:** Expert-only shortcuts used as the main path lose beginners.
- **episodes:** 169

### UI-T12 · HUD declutter rules
- **what:** Keep useful HUD elements without blocking play.
- **how:** Auto-hide tracking markers (like player cursors above heads) based on distance and situation. Make HUD panels semi-transparent when a character overlaps them. Keep essentials out of edges a display may crop; CRTs showed only about 490 of 525 scanlines. If you shake the whole rendered image, expect the HUD to shake and blank edges to show.
- **when:** HUD layout and polish.
- **pitfalls:** Players differ: offer HUD size and hiding options rather than one fixed amount.
- **episodes:** 254;252;103;55

### UI-T13 · Lively, state-reactive HUD
- **what:** A HUD that informs and entertains.
- **how:** Portraits of small on-screen characters, posed toward the player. Backgrounds animated with upward UV scrolling. Meters that change per ability and break when the vehicle is destroyed. Icons for opponents hit or defeated in 3D. Score gains counted up point by point.
- **when:** Action, racing and party games with small characters or busy 3D scenes.
- **pitfalls:** Keep it readable; liveliness must not cost clarity.
- **episodes:** 254;200;11

### UI-T14 · Next-reward counter
- **what:** Show progress as current versus required.
- **how:** Put current and required values (EXP now / EXP to next level) on the main screen; move the counter on every kill or pickup. Consider a checklist grid where clearing a square reveals its neighbors' conditions.
- **when:** Any game with levels, unlocks or purchases to save toward.
- **pitfalls:** If money and gear are too plentiful, no single goal is worth tracking.
- **episodes:** 23;59

### UI-T15 · Translucent full-screen map overlay
- **what:** A map players can read while moving.
- **how:** Lay a translucent map over the whole game screen, usable during play, with one button to hide gameplay and show only the map. Add in-world guidance (corner-sharpness markers) and terrain that leads, so the map is needed less.
- **when:** Exploration, dungeon and racing games.
- **pitfalls:** Won't fit every genre; 3D spaces need a deliberate way to show height.
- **episodes:** 119

### UI-T16 · One reusable, world-styled loading indicator
- **what:** A load indicator that can appear anywhere for any duration.
- **how:** Design one minimal animated indicator styled after the world and show it immediately on undisguised loads. Use load-masking presentations only if they still work when final loads are shorter. Never place an icon over a transition that already hides the load. Time each fade and black screen and cut it to the fastest that still isn't abrupt.

- **pitfalls:** Load times usually shrink before release; don't set presentation length from dev builds.

### UI-T17 · Settings with live preview
- **what:** Players see a setting's effect as they change it.
- **how:** Give HUD size and similar options a preview window. On brightness or HDR screens show both a dark and a bright reference scene. Let players change these mid-game to compare in real play.
- **when:** Options menus.
- **pitfalls:** A setting players can't see the effect of tends to be left at default or set wrong.
- **episodes:** 55

### UI-T18 · Accessibility menu checklist

### UI-T19 · Localizable text-effect markup
- **what:** Inline emphasis that translators control.
- **how:** Add inline tags for size, color, animation and font on single words or letters. Keep the tags in the localizable script. Add speech-bubble styles and a few emotion symbols.

- **pitfalls:** May not suit serious titles; copying source-language emphasis into other languages reads unnaturally.

### UI-T20 · Per-language text layout and on-device check
- **what:** Text that fits and reads in every language.
- **how:** Size boxes for each language: larger glyphs for kanji, longer strings for English and longer still for French. Review every localized screen on the actual target display (on the handheld itself for handheld games). Offer a font-size option where screen space allows, and keep the interface clean so text can grow.
- **when:** From layout onward, every localized build.
- **pitfalls:** Text so big it stops looking stylish.

### UI-T21 · Customization screen checklist
- **what:** Make customizing fun, not a chore.
- **how:** Illustrate each part's effect (draw its attack range). Show pros and cons side by side, and give options clearly different trade-offs. Add sorting and categories, visually distinct icons and a one-press equip-strongest. Let players test the build immediately, ideally on the fly. Make every part look cool or cute.

- **pitfalls:** Options without meaningful differences make the screen pointless.

### UI-T22 · One screen, many styles
- **what:** Find the UI style that fits the world.
- **how:** Mock up one representative screen (for example options) as slick, cute, horror, flashy, digital and regional or historical versions. Pick the one that fits the world and target audience; theme frames, icons, colors and decorations to match; check legibility at real size.
- **when:** Early in art direction.
- **pitfalls:** Budget time for a custom font if nothing existing fits.
- **episodes:** 111

### UI-T23 · Inverse rank display
- **what:** Show standing so higher is better.
- **how:** Compute a power number against the total player population (how many players someone is above) so it rises with improvement. Hide the world number one from general players. Add an elite tier for top players that also keeps them matching each other.

- **pitfalls:** Matchmaking ranges too narrow find no matches; too broad gives lopsided matches.

## Directing and teams

### TEAM-T01 · Daily Report
- **what:** A short internal page the director posts every workday so the whole team sees progress, news and the current build.
- **how:** Put one current screenshot at the top (work in progress the public can't see is fine), then 3-5 topics: project news, what you're working on, checklists, issues to fix, and sometimes a game you played, current events or your thoughts. Enable comments on each topic. Post key announcements here instead of mass email. Invite teammates to post what they finished that day. Vary the topics so a team working on the same thing gets some stimulus.
- **when:** Every working day, from the start of production; especially useful when you join an existing team as an outsider.
- **pitfalls:** Using it only for orders or sternness; letting it go stale; relying on email for the important things.
- **episodes:** 43;123;261;190

### TEAM-T02 · Same-day review pipeline
- **what:** A predictable route for review requests that guarantees a reply the day work is delivered.
- **how:** Team members email a request with a fixed subject (his is "Review Request") once production work is finished. Handle requests first come, first served: check the request, gather materials, review, and explain any changes to everyone involved. Reply the same day, as soon as the current review ends. Set a daily delivery cutoff for same-day review (his was 8 PM) and send feedback as bullet points. Reserve a fixed block (such as mornings, or after a cutoff time) with no reviews for your own work.
- **when:** Whenever one person approves other people's work.
- **pitfalls:** Review requests scattered across chat and ticket tools (he avoids both for this because each has drawbacks); reviewing late at night; letting the review queue eat your whole day.
- **episodes:** 177;261

### TEAM-T03 · Five-minute retouch
- **what:** A quick paintover of the artist's own image that shows the change instead of describing it.
- **how:** Spend about five minutes, ten at most, on a rough retouch, mouse sketch or diagram. Send it side by side with the original and one line on what to change and why. For a flat or monochrome background, his Photoshop sequence: duplicate the layer, boost contrast, shift the hue, erase the shifted layer where light would hit, then harmonize the colors. Label it as direction; the artist executes the final version.
- **when:** For any visual note that's hard to put into words: palette, contrast, lighting, pose, silhouette, shape.
- **pitfalls:** Retouching everything; treating the retouch as final art; text-only notes on visual problems.
- **episodes:** 90;165;224;253

### TEAM-T04 · Animation list with posed-figure key poses
- **what:** A spec package that tells animators every move's timing and shows the key poses before they start.
- **how:** List every animation with a text description, its total length and when the attack becomes active; mark loops like idle and walk "Loop" or "Any". Set timings from gameplay push and pull. For key poses (idle, movement, attacks), pose a physical figure, photograph it, cut out the shape, raise the contrast and paste it into the spec. Add screenshots from a character's original game when a move references it; use text alone for non-humanoid characters.
- **when:** Before animation production on any character with many moves.
- **pitfalls:** Treating the spec as the finished animation: figures move far less than models, so animators must fill gaps.
- **episodes:** 14;253;36

### TEAM-T05 · Four-value attack request
- **what:** The minimum data an animator needs to start an attack animation.
- **how:** For every new attack, specify: (1) the standby pose, (2) the attack (key) pose, (3) the frame the attack starts, (4) total frames, meaning the time until the attack can be canceled. The lead-in fills the gap between standby and attack, the follow-through between attack and total frames. Add an overlay or note of when and where collision is active, and check the result by feel against the frame counts.
- **when:** Any action game with timed attacks.
- **pitfalls:** Timing words like "quick" instead of frame numbers; weak poses on the hit frame; animation that sits away from the hitbox.
- **episodes:** 36;14;76;225

### TEAM-T06 · Bracketed parameters
- **what:** Spec notation that marks exactly which values programmers must expose for tuning.
- **how:** Write each tunable value as a plain-language phrase in brackets with a placeholder, for example "Initiate movement at [Special startup X-velocity is O]" or "decelerate by [Special X-deceleration is O/frame]". Team rule: anything in brackets becomes a parameter. Keep a separate list pairing each bracketed phrase with its current value, and state units for every speed.
- **when:** Character- or move-specific specs; less useful for global values like general attack power.
- **pitfalls:** Terse names like "X-velocity" that others misread; speeds without units; hard-coding values so every tweak needs a programmer.
- **episodes:** 68;84

### TEAM-T07 · All-hands feedback session
- **what:** One review where every discipline that touches a feature hears the director's feedback together.
- **how:** Book a room with a large monitor showing the build or your screen. Require the planner, programmers, modelers, animators, effects and sound designers on the feature, plus their leads, to attend; let anyone else sit in. Use a mic so everyone hears, explain what's on screen, and take questions at the end. Separately, have each discipline hold its own group reviews. Keep it brisk.
- **when:** When feedback would otherwise be relayed through one person, or when a mixed team keeps misreading intent.
- **pitfalls:** Meetings that drag; inviting people but not showing the actual build.
- **episodes:** 114;14

### TEAM-T08 · Problem package
- **what:** A standard way to raise an issue so the decider can act at once.
- **how:** Raise it the moment you notice it, to someone who can act. Include: what's wrong, everything you know (accurate, nothing left out), at least one proposed solution, and, if it's your own task, an honest status well before the deadline. Skip the debate about how to phrase it. If the team keeps things as they are, ask for the reasons.
- **when:** Any time anyone on the team spots a problem or gets stuck.
- **pitfalls:** Complaints with no suggestion; holding problems until the postmortem; last-minute "I can't do it".
- **episodes:** 60;29;258;191

### TEAM-T09 · Decide-before-you-leave meeting rule
- **what:** Meetings end with decisions, not with "later".
- **how:** Make sure someone with authority to decide attends. Have the team put every option and the facts on the table, pick the solution that works in the short and long term, and decide before the meeting ends. If information is missing, assign someone to research it right away. Leave leeway to correct the call later with a small adjustment.
- **when:** Any decision meeting, and any time a teammate brings the decider an issue.
- **pitfalls:** Decisions without the decider present; postponing for second opinions while the team waits; refusing to revisit a call you now know is wrong.
- **episodes:** 191;29;158

### TEAM-T10 · Opinion board
- **what:** An always-open place where anyone can post opinions, suggestions and requests about how development is going.
- **how:** Create a simple shared board. The director reads it, replies with their thinking or forwards items to the right team, so posts don't get lost. Set it up as soon as you join a team.
- **when:** From the start of any project, and especially when joining a team.
- **pitfalls:** Posts that vanish without a reply; assuming silence means there are no problems.
- **episodes:** 60;123

### TEAM-T11 · Kid playtest session
- **what:** Children playing the current build while the team watches, for honest reactions.
- **how:** Get permission first (parents, company confidentiality, child well-being). Invite staff members' children, relatives and their friends; let them come in groups and provide snacks so it feels like a fun gaming session, not a test. Don't ask them questions: stand back and note where the controls give them trouble and what they get bored with. Share the reactions with the team, especially during hard stretches.
- **when:** Whenever controls or pacing need an honest check, and when the team needs a boost.
- **pitfalls:** Interviewing the kids; running sessions without permission.
- **episodes:** 248

### TEAM-T12 · Fresh-tester rotation and handicapped self-test
- **what:** Ways to see the game the way a newcomer will.
- **how:** Bring in people who have never played and have them start from the very beginning; treat each person as a one-time fresh tester. Between sessions, tune difficulty while handicapping yourself (playing one-handed, lower health or stricter conditions), then make it easier than feels right. Recruit testers prone to motion sickness for camera checks.
- **when:** Difficulty tuning, onboarding checks, camera comfort.
- **pitfalls:** Calling the same outsider back as if they were still fresh; balancing for the best player on staff.
- **episodes:** 256;129;248

### TEAM-T13 · Task tickets
- **what:** Every production task as a tracked ticket with one owner.
- **how:** Split each content piece (one character, say) into tickets: spec and documentation, model and rig, animation, programming, effects, sound effects, balancing. Give each exactly one owner, a status (in progress, complete, on hold, rescinded) and a deadline a lead tracks. Link dependent jobs (animation waits on the model) and leave slack so downstream people don't start on unfinished input.
- **when:** Any team bigger than one person; Sakurai says nothing would get done without it today.
- **pitfalls:** Work that lives only in conversation; chaining dependent tasks back to back.
- **episodes:** 94

### TEAM-T14 · Bug-ticket pipeline
- **what:** A loop that finds, routes, fixes and re-checks bugs without losing them.
- **how:** Everyone tests the latest daily build. Testers work from design documents and checklists in subteams per area. A bug becomes a ticket once it has reproduction steps. A manager grades severity (Class S: hangs or save corruption; Class A: progression blockers; lower classes may be waived) and assigns it to the discipline that owns the problem (artists fix characters, animators fix animations). The tester verifies the fix in several configurations, then the ticket is set to resolved, not deleted; related tickets get cross-linked. For huge combination spaces, run automated CPU matches overnight.
- **when:** From the first testable build to release.
- **pitfalls:** Programmers doubling as main testers; reports from outdated builds; deleting fixed tickets.
- **episodes:** 208;94;50

### TEAM-T15 · Overhead budget
- **what:** Schedule from effective output, not headcount.
- **how:** Assume about 70% effective output (ten people produce roughly seven people's work), more overhead as the team grows, less on a team of a few. Name the owners of workload management, outside communication and creative supervision. When a strong creator becomes a lead, backfill their production work. Expect big workflow changes to cost output (his remote-work estimate was about 30%).
- **when:** Scheduling, staffing, and whenever someone is promoted to lead.
- **pitfalls:** Planning as if headcount equals output.
- **episodes:** 88;66

### TEAM-T16 · Subteam showcases
- **what:** Regular presentations where each discipline or subteam shows everyone what it finished.
- **how:** Every few months (or each period), have graphics, sound, animation, effects, UI and other teams, or subteams like the Character Team, present finished work to the whole team. Book the time and venue even though aligning schedules is a hassle.
- **when:** Medium and large teams, or any team where people only see their own slice.
- **pitfalls:** Skipping them because they're hard to organize.
- **episodes:** 43;88

### TEAM-T17 · Audio feedback kit
- **what:** How to brief and correct sound and music when you can't draw it.
- **how:** State the sound's role in concrete terms ("this is a strong attack"), compare only to sounds everyone knows ("like a jet engine"), and link a real example. Use onomatopoeia only as an extra clue. For music, give one reference track and write which quality to keep and which signature element not to copy, and list two or three other directions so you don't fixate. When notes keep coming back reversed, play the original together and agree on terms. If you lack musical vocabulary, route notes through a sound director.
- **when:** Any sound or music request or review.
- **pitfalls:** Emails asking for more "clang"; assuming people know the sound you mean; fixating on one reference.
- **episodes:** 185;222;127;146

### TEAM-T18 · Shared reference screen
- **what:** One agreed display for judging color so everyone reviews the same image.
- **how:** Set up a monitor far enough away for everyone to see, calibrate other monitors to it by eye, and light review areas neutrally. Stop using any monitor that crushes shadows or flattens contrast for art checks.
- **when:** Art and lighting reviews, especially with remote or mixed hardware.
- **pitfalls:** Assuming everyone sees the same colors; tinted office lighting.
- **episodes:** 184

### TEAM-T19 · Workflow-change survey and central purchasing
- **what:** Find and fix shared problems fast after the team changes how it works.
- **how:** Shortly after the change, survey everyone on what is and isn't working. For equipment gaps, let anyone send a product link; one central budget buys it, ships it to their home, and they keep it, which skips approval chains across partner companies. Move rituals like daily test matches online.
- **when:** After going remote or any big change in tools or process.
- **pitfalls:** Letting each company run its own approvals; ignoring missing desks and chairs that will cause health problems.
- **episodes:** 66

### TEAM-T20 · Comic-panel proposal deck
- **what:** A proposal format that moves fast even when it's long.
- **how:** One screenshot or diagram and about two lines of text per slide, one topic per slide. Group slides into categories and subcategories and show where the audience is. Say a little about each slide instead of reading it. Cut graphs, research, Plan B and long passages about your grand vision.
- **when:** Pitching a game or a major feature to the team or to decision-makers.
- **pitfalls:** Walls of text read aloud; endless decks with no visible structure; confusing speed with short.
- **episodes:** 38;12;52

### TEAM-T21 · One-sentence acceptance goal
- **what:** A stopping rule for feedback rounds.
- **how:** Before reviewing an asset, write its goal in one sentence (for the dojo wall: it must look like wooden boards actually broke). Approve anything that meets it, even if it differs from your picture. If several rounds of feedback still don't reach the bar, put it in the game as-is and move on.
- **when:** Supervising any asset that keeps bouncing between rounds.
- **pitfalls:** Big swings each round that feel like changing direction; endless polishing of one asset while others wait.
- **episodes:** 224;185

### TEAM-T22 · Giveaway game
- **what:** Turn spare items into a small team event.
- **how:** List the day's items on the daily report page and let interested people sign up. Pick winners with a different quick game each time: a rock-paper-scissors tournament, a match in the game you're building, first to pull off a tricky move, or a secret rule such as whoever posted closest to 0 seconds.
- **when:** Whenever you have duplicates, unused hardware or event merchandise to pass on.
- **pitfalls:** Handing things out at random.
- **episodes:** 190

## Work ethic and creativity

### CRAFT-T01 · Same-day review pipeline
- **what:** A supervision routine that never leaves a teammate waiting overnight and still protects the lead's own work time.
- **how:** Requests arrive by email with a fixed subject ('Review Request') once the work is actually finished. Handle them first come, first served; gather materials, review, and explain any changes to everyone involved. Reply the same day, ideally right after the review you're on. Set a delivery cutoff with collaborators (Sakurai's channel editors had to deliver by 8 PM). Send feedback as bullet points; channel episodes took about six to eight rounds. Block a fixed period (mornings or after a cutoff) with no reviews.
- **when:** Any time you review other people's work, especially as a director or lead.
- **pitfalls:** Instant messaging and ticketing each have drawbacks for this, in his view; late deliveries that push reviews into the night.
- **episodes:** 177;261

### CRAFT-T02 · Problem-theme ideation drill
- **what:** A structured way to get ideas when none come.
- **how:** Write the problem as a one-line theme. List broad solution types, silly ones included. Next to each, note why it fails here. For each failure, write a variant that fixes the flaw (no sand, try dirt; bucket too small, try a bigger bucket). Prune only once the list is long.
- **when:** When you're stuck on a design decision or need a new mechanic.
- **pitfalls:** Starting from nothing with no defined theme; pruning too early.
- **episodes:** 237

### CRAFT-T03 · Something to run from
- **what:** Use an imagined pursuer or a short self-imposed limit to create urgency.
- **how:** Before starting a task, tell yourself you only have a few hours left and work as if it's true. Frame each milestone as something closing in on you rather than a finish line you'll reach eventually. Set the imagined deadline well ahead of the real one.
- **when:** Tasks you could 'finish whenever'.
- **pitfalls:** Waiting for the actual last day; effectiveness depends on the person and situation.
- **episodes:** 171

### CRAFT-T04 · The one-hour installment
- **what:** Keep a long-running output habit alive by keeping each piece small.
- **how:** Pick a recurring output that uses a skill your main job already trains (for a director, explaining ideas). Cap each installment at a short fixed time (his Famitsu columns took about an hour). Publish what you think is good without writing for an imagined audience or out of duty.
- **when:** Devlogs, columns, team reports, social posts, video series.
- **pitfalls:** Obligation and overthinking; letting each piece grow until the habit dies during crunch.
- **episodes:** 98;126

### CRAFT-T05 · Daily report with a screenshot
- **what:** A daily output habit that also keeps a team connected.
- **how:** Every workday, post one current screenshot at the top and three to five topics (news, what you're working on, checklists, issues, sometimes games you played or thoughts). Allow comments on each topic. Sakurai began during Brawl and the internal screenshot became his public Screenshot of the Day.
- **when:** When leading a team, especially as an outsider joining an existing team.
- **pitfalls:** Relying on mass emails, which often go unread.
- **episodes:** 123;43;126;261

### CRAFT-T06 · Internal-pressure drafting
- **what:** Write a proposal alone, under pressure, before anyone else sees it.
- **how:** Don't discuss or post about the idea until the first complete draft exists. Each time you want to talk about it, write that thought into the document. Once the draft is done, show it to colleagues for weak points and new ideas.
- **when:** New proposals and design documents created from scratch.
- **pitfalls:** Talking it through early; replacing your draft with a compiled group brainstorm.
- **episodes:** 144

### CRAFT-T07 · Hot-and-fast proposal writing
- **what:** Get a proposal or script written in one push while motivation is high.
- **how:** Sort ideas into main topics and subtopics and itemize the main points; then write the whole thing in one go with brief sentences and images backing the text. Cut graphs, research, the Plan B and long passages on your grand vision. For a large batch, write fast and accept small imperfections (Sakurai wrote his channel scripts in about a month, roughly 12 a day).
- **when:** Proposals, pitches, scripts, presentations.
- **pitfalls:** Letting the draft sit; agonizing over every line; padding.
- **episodes:** 52;261;12

### CRAFT-T08 · Exercise-bike play sessions
- **what:** Fold sustained aerobic exercise into time you already spend playing.
- **how:** Put an exercise bike (or stepper or treadmill) in front of the screen and pedal through play sessions; he has gone for two hours. Racing games suit it (think of pedaling as going faster); stealth games make you pedal slower. Ramp up gradually.
- **when:** When playing many games for research eats into exercise time.
- **pitfalls:** Overdoing it: he hurt his legs climbing around 500 stairs.
- **episodes:** 24

### CRAFT-T09 · Eye-strain workstation fix
- **what:** Change the environment first; treat other remedies as secondary.
- **how:** Measure eye-to-monitor distance. If it is under 60 cm, move the monitor back (Sakurai uses at least one meter) and get a larger monitor to compensate. Match brightness to the room. Prefer TV-distance play to handheld screens for long sessions. Blue-light glasses, eyedrops and supplements come after.
- **when:** Long dev and play sessions; any time your eyes tire quickly.
- **pitfalls:** Long sessions with the screen close to your eyes.
- **episodes:** 81

### CRAFT-T10 · Blind playthrough
- **what:** Study someone else's game with no outside influence.
- **how:** Pick one game. Skip its trailers, reviews, strategy guides and Let's Plays until you finish. If curious, play it rather than watching someone else. Open a guide only as a last resort when truly stuck. Form your verdict, then read what others think.
- **when:** Studying other developers' work; refreshing your own first-impression sense.
- **pitfalls:** Spoilers are hard to avoid today, especially for story-focused games.
- **episodes:** 199

### CRAFT-T11 · First-game career path
- **what:** A concrete route into making games for a living.
- **how:** Pick any accessible tool (Unity, Unreal Engine, C, JavaScript, visual programming or Maker software). Make a small game, imitating an existing one if that helps you finish. Let friends play it or release it free online, gather reactions, then improve it or apply the lessons. For applications, make a game with an original idea and send it even if you don't meet every requirement.
- **when:** Students, hobbyists and anyone trying to break in.
- **pitfalls:** An imitative showcase game; waiting until you meet every listed requirement.
- **episodes:** 34;118;196

### CRAFT-T12 · Echo-chamber feedback sort
- **what:** Separate useful criticism from noise without losing your direction.
- **how:** When an idea or review gets only praise, deliberately ask people outside that circle what is wrong with it. Sort what you hear into team concerns to adapt to and outside pressure that would make the game more ordinary. Check spreading online claims against data or actual matches.
- **when:** After reveals, reviews, community feedback or internal polls.
- **pitfalls:** Seeking out only agreement; sanding off deliberate quirks to please outside voices.
- **episodes:** 228;218;247

### CRAFT-T13 · Fixed-scope side project
- **what:** Run a big project next to your main job without it swallowing you.
- **how:** Decide the total run and per-edition size up front (PRESS START: ten annual concerts, 14 to 16 pieces each). For content projects, brainstorm one-line topics, sort them into categories with fixed counts, cut weak ones, and outline and write everything before production. Make a pilot of a few real pieces at final quality to prove you can deliver. Use one ID scheme for folders, files and a tracking sheet.
- **when:** Concerts, video series, columns, books or any recurring project beside development.
- **pitfalls:** Open-ended growth; once everything is fixed in advance you can't change course on audience requests.
- **episodes:** 116;261

### CRAFT-T14 · Negative-to-question reframe
- **what:** Turn defeatist thoughts into action.
- **how:** When you catch a defeatist line ('my work feels pointless'), don't say or post it. Rewrite it as a question ('what are some ways I can make it better?') and do one thing from the answer. Before a hard task, psych yourself up out loud and start.
- **when:** Slumps, setbacks, long crunches.
- **pitfalls:** Treating it as forced positivity that denies the feeling.
- **episodes:** 212

### CRAFT-T15 · Instant-access reference library
- **what:** Keep reference games and hardware ready to check a mechanic or capture footage in minutes.
- **how:** House current consoles together behind a video selector, give each its own switchable power outlet, plan ventilation for the hottest machines, and label every cable and selector input. Store media in standard cases by system and box up hardware your displays can't connect to.
- **when:** If you study or capture footage from many games.
- **pitfalls:** Letting the collection go unorganized, which only gets worse.
- **episodes:** 216

## Marketing and updates

### MKT-T01 · Reveal-video pipeline
- **what:** Sakurai's production order for Smash fighter reveals, so structure and length are settled before money is spent on CG.
- **how:** 1) Write a plot like a planning document: every cut, how characters behave and often when the music comes in. 2) Meet the production staff (CG studio) to agree details. 3) Storyboards, with frequent adjustment meetings from here on. 4) Animatics: a rough edit made by moving and stretching storyboard art; at this point structure and length are essentially set. 5) Previz weaves in CG. 6) Production: touched-up model revisions, backgrounds, effects and blur up to final quality. 7) Sound, built on the game's own sound effects. Gameplay segments are captured separately with debug and replay tools and brushed up over repeated checks.
- **when:** Any trailer with a pre-rendered or scripted segment.
- **pitfalls:** Starting expensive CG before the animatic has locked timing.
- **episodes:** 163

### MKT-T02 · Five-check shot pass
- **what:** Sakurai's most frequent notes on reveal footage, usable as a checklist.
- **how:** For each shot: zoom in and go bolder; tweak the angle of view to transform the layout; add interactions with other characters; balance serious and comical; sync to the music. Also remove depth-of-field focus pulls that make big things read as small.
- **when:** Polish pass on any trailer or showcase footage.
- **pitfalls:** Stopping at 'good enough'.
- **episodes:** 163;174

### MKT-T03 · Seconds-to-gameplay audit
- **what:** Check how quickly a trailer shows the actual game.
- **how:** Watch the trailer as a stranger and note the timestamp of the first real gameplay. Cut logos, long opening movies and character intros ahead of it; lead with what makes the game appealing; move story and world later. Acceptable alternatives to opening on raw gameplay: footage that clearly illustrates the systems, or a tech demo that truthfully shows how the game runs.
- **when:** Before publishing any trailer or store-page video.
- **pitfalls:** Teaser-only reveals for new IP or indie titles; exceptions he grants are for famous series or creators.
- **episodes:** 186;17

### MKT-T04 · Expectation-match check
- **what:** Make sure marketing paints the picture the game delivers.
- **how:** Lay the title, box art, store screenshots and trailer next to a recording of normal play. For each, ask what a buyer would expect and whether the game delivers it. Fix mismatches; keep surprises only if the overall picture still matches.
- **when:** Before store-page launch, box art sign-off and each trailer.
- **pitfalls:** Misleading for surprise; marketing that attracts the wrong audience.
- **episodes:** 101

### MKT-T05 · Dojo-style update cadence
- **what:** A frequent, predictable stream of official information written by the person who knows the game best.
- **how:** Publish one new detail on a fixed schedule (Brawl: every weekday, five a week). The director writes or reviews the text; the team helps with screenshots. Localize simultaneously if you want a global audience (Brawl: six languages with regional partners). Reply to comments where risk management allows.
- **when:** Pre-launch campaigns and ongoing post-launch communication.
- **pitfalls:** The director is the busiest person on the team, so delegate production where possible, though reviewing still takes time.
- **episodes:** 245;80

### MKT-T06 · Players' guide for a misunderstood game
- **what:** Explain an unusual game's depth directly to players.
- **how:** Write a plain document on how the game is played, its core game essence and unique features, and where its push and pull lies. If press won't run it, publish it yourself as a guide on an official site.
- **when:** When early reactions dismiss the game as shallow or misread what it is.
- **pitfalls:** Relying only on press coverage.
- **episodes:** 143;245

### MKT-T07 · Content-shaped official site
- **what:** A home base that mirrors the game's structure.
- **how:** List your game's content types (characters, modes, techniques, online, stages, music, events) and make each a section; add features the game needs, such as a music player; run a blog alongside the official social accounts; have someone who knows the design review layout and updates.
- **when:** Any game big enough to need one canonical information source.
- **pitfalls:** A one-size-fits-all template.
- **episodes:** 143

### MKT-T08 · Demo as a separate deliverable
- **what:** Plan a demo like a small second game.
- **how:** Decide early. Schedule its own management, updates, tuning and a near-final QA pass. Keep spoiler content in separable files; strip it and check the build for leftovers; retest, since removing data tends to break things. Gate a pre-launch release on balance; if it isn't balanced, consider a post-launch demo built from the finished game.
- **when:** Whenever a demo is being considered.
- **pitfalls:** Leaked spoiler data; an unbalanced demo creating negative word of mouth.
- **episodes:** 122

### MKT-T09 · Demo-play checklist
- **what:** Get the most quality footage out of a live or recorded play demo.
- **how:** List everything the footage must show (for a fighter: all special moves, the Final Smash and as many regular moves as possible), keep matches relatively close, tick items off while playing, and check the on-stage play setup in advance.
- **when:** Presentations, showcase streams, event demos.
- **pitfalls:** Demoing content you don't understand.
- **episodes:** 207

### MKT-T10 · Live-stakes performance
- **what:** Make live gameplay thrilling by keeping real failure on the table.
- **how:** Play at high difficulty with no restarts so the audience senses genuine risk (Sakurai used Intensity 9.0, an average weapon and zero defense). Pick a segment of known length so music or presentation can be planned around it, with cue points that follow the player's actual progress.
- **when:** Live events, concerts, stage demos.
- **pitfalls:** A Game Over really would end the show, so accept that risk deliberately.
- **episodes:** 227

### MKT-T11 · Post-launch triage
- **what:** Decide which updates are worth the team's time.
- **how:** Fix crashing and fatal bugs as a must. Treat balance and quality-of-life changes as optional, weighed against freeing the director and key staff for the next game. Prefer contained fixes (lower specific encounters, add an easier option) over core-system changes; re-run broad checks after each fix before release; watch player videos for rare bugs; use online and tournament data for balance.
- **when:** From launch through the end of support.
- **pitfalls:** Fixes that create new bugs; games-as-a-service titles may need a different approach.
- **episodes:** 231;141;136;220

### MKT-T12 · Competition-for-time list
- **what:** Position a game against everything that wants the same player's hours.
- **how:** Write down what your target player could spend the same time on: current releases, older and remastered games, subscription catalogs, phone and social content. Next to the list, write why they would pick your game instead.
- **when:** Concept and pitch stage, and again before setting marketing messages.
- **pitfalls:** Listing only this year's releases.
- **episodes:** 72;198;250

### MKT-T13 · Top-down target statement
- **what:** Pick an audience from the market, not from your own taste.
- **how:** Map the player segments that exist now (physical console buyers, single social game players, location-based, competitive shooter, VR, fitness, MMO, PC indie and so on), choose one deliberately, and describe it by experience level or need (e.g. 'beginners'). Decide whether you are aiming at the broad trunk or a niche branch.
- **when:** Before tuning difficulty and writing marketing copy.
- **pitfalls:** Demographic slices treated as hard lines.
- **episodes:** 112;226

### MKT-T14 · Footage capture tools
- **what:** Build tools so marketing footage can be shot, not caught.
- **how:** Add debug and replay features that let you pause, rotate characters, zoom and change the view angle, so footage can be re-shot from the best camera angle. Expect limits (in Smash, fighters couldn't be moved closer or farther and objects could vanish from hands).
- **when:** As early as possible in development.
- **pitfalls:** Capturing trailer gameplay only from live play.
- **episodes:** 163;195

### MKT-T15 · Announce the obvious early
- **what:** Head off speculation by announcing what people will notice anyway.
- **how:** If a partnership or development arrangement will be visible (for example, the director being seen at a partner studio), announce it as the project begins.
- **when:** Partnerships with external studios.
- **episodes:** 135

### MKT-T16 · Licensed release timing
- **what:** Fit a licensed game into the property's other media.
- **how:** Time the game's release with the property's comics, anime, figures and events, since fans see the game as one form of the property among many.
- **when:** Licensed and tie-in games.
- **pitfalls:** Judging a licensed game by fun alone.
- **episodes:** 188

## Tech and tools

### TECH-T01 · External parameter table with one-button conversion
- **what:** Designers tune numbers in a spreadsheet and see them in every running build at once.
- **how:** Programmers expose every tunable as a named parameter read from a table. An add-in converts the sheet and pushes it to all active dev kits in one press. Compare: original Smash and Melee needed ten minutes per conversion plus a reboot, with hand-edited text files.
- **when:** From the first playable build.
- **pitfalls:** If only one person can edit parameters, tuning bottlenecks on them.
- **episodes:** 46;68

### TECH-T02 · Bracket notation for tunables in specs
- **what:** Specs mark exactly what programmers must expose.
- **how:** Write each value as a plain-language phrase in brackets with O as the placeholder and units where they matter: Initiate movement at [Special startup X-velocity is O]. At the same time, decelerate by [Special X-deceleration is O/frame]. Team rule: anything in brackets becomes a parameter. Keep a separate list pairing each bracketed phrase with its value.
- **when:** Character- and move-specific specs.
- **pitfalls:** Works less well for global values like general attack power; handle those separately.
- **episodes:** 68

### TECH-T03 · Hidden fields and linked formulas
- **what:** Parameter sheets that resist mistakes.
- **how:** Collapse rarely adjusted parameters out of the default view. Express dependent values as formulas so relative balance holds, for example one move always 1/2 as strong as another.
- **when:** Once a single entity has many parameters, as every Smash attack does.
- **pitfalls:** Hidden values still need documentation so they aren't forgotten.
- **episodes:** 46

### TECH-T04 · Frame counting by ear
- **what:** Feel frame durations at 60 fps without tools.
- **how:** Count 1-and-2-and-3-and while clapping seconds: each beat is about 30 frames, half a beat about 15. Anchor references: Smash Attack input to release about 15 frames; Tekken standard kick startup about 15; Street Fighter II heavy punch startup a bit under half that. Write timings in specs as exact frame numbers and verify by feel after implementation.
- **when:** Writing and reviewing move and animation specs.
- **pitfalls:** Correct frame counts can still be broken by code or data errors; investigate when it feels off.
- **episodes:** 76;5

### TECH-T05 · One documented speed unit per project
- **what:** Every speed in specs and parameters has a unit the whole team shares.
- **how:** Use pixels per frame for 2D sprites (Mario's top speed in Mario Bros. is about 1 p/f on a 256-pixel-wide NES screen) or real-world units for 3D. Build characters and environments at real-world scale if you want to use real speeds as-is; use distance per frame (m/frame) where the game needs it. Judge speed relative to character size: Kirby is about a 20 cm square, so low km/h values are fast for him.
- **when:** Project start; whenever resolution or scale changes.
- **pitfalls:** The same pixel speed gives different results on screens with different pixel counts.
- **episodes:** 84

### TECH-T06 · Frame-drop versus slowdown check
- **what:** Make sure dropped frames never slow the game.
- **how:** Decouple logic timing from the display: force the frame rate down and confirm objects still cover the same distance per second. Test at other refresh rates (the PAL lesson: 50 Hz versions of interrupt-timed games ran at 5/6 speed). Find any timer tied to processing speed and lock it before final balance.
- **when:** Engine setup; every optimization pass.
- **pitfalls:** Optimization that speeds up a processing-tied game also raises its difficulty.
- **episodes:** 5;141;74

### TECH-T07 · Lag check before every session
- **what:** Verify the display and audio chain adds no delay.
- **how:** On the exact setup, play an instantly responsive 2D game (NES Kirby via Nintendo Switch Online) and press jump; the character must move the instant you press. Set Game Mode or Low Latency Mode or disable all picture processing. Convert listed display times to frames (16.67 ms = 1 frame at 60 fps). If hit sounds lag through a home theater, use an analog jack or low-latency audio.
- **when:** Before demos, playtests, events and footage capture.
- **pitfalls:** A 3D game is a poor lag test; someone else having played on the setup proves nothing.
- **episodes:** 8;156

### TECH-T08 · Final-hardware difficulty pass
- **what:** Catch timing changes between dev setup and retail units.
- **how:** After late optimization and before sign-off, play difficulty-critical content on production hardware. Compare timer rates (hunger, fatigue, growth), time limits and enemy speeds with design values.
- **when:** The last weeks before submission.
- **pitfalls:** If a fix is needed post-launch, keep it narrow (lower specific encounters, add an easier option).
- **episodes:** 141;92;74

### TECH-T09 · Debug mode essentials
- **what:** A daily-use debug mode that saves the team time.
- **how:** Commands for frequent setups (set every fighter to X% damage); all debug functions on button combinations of the normal controller; capture tools (pause, rotate characters, zoom, change camera angle); regular reorganizing and pruning; stripped from the release build.
- **when:** All of production.
- **pitfalls:** Missing features breed workarounds such as setting Fox's forward tilt to 100% damage.
- **episodes:** 195

### TECH-T10 · Isolation views
- **what:** Developer views that show exactly one thing.
- **how:** Per-part or whole-model translucency and hiding (for capes, clothes, swaying parts); an on/off toggle for every display element; collision surfaces highlighted in red; slow motion and background-off for effects.
- **when:** Animation, stage and effect reviews, including reviews by people outside the team.
- **pitfalls:** Scaling or deleting model parts to see an animation changes what you're reviewing.
- **episodes:** 217;30;79

### TECH-T11 · Replay plus free debug camera
- **what:** Re-shoot any moment of play from the best angle.
- **how:** Record matches as replays and play them back with a free camera, so trailer and screenshot shots can be framed and re-taken.
- **when:** Marketing capture, screenshots, and analyzing play.
- **pitfalls:** Know the tool's limits (Smash's debug capture couldn't move fighters nearer or farther; objects could vanish from hands).
- **episodes:** 163;195

### TECH-T12 · Test matrix estimate and hot-spot list
- **what:** See where full coverage is impossible and where to aim.
- **how:** Multiply characters by opponents by moves by items by player counts by stages and hazards by progression modifiers (89 fighters alone give about 8,000 pairings). Then test first: one entity controlling another (grabs and throws, especially on a target mid special move); scene-changing supers (restrain, release, no leaks between scenes); extreme and V-shaped terrain; parameter, growth and unlock systems; AI mode conflicts.
- **when:** Test planning, and again when systems are added.
- **pitfalls:** A game of dozens of hours may need thousands to tens of thousands of test hours; prioritize, don't brute-force.
- **episodes:** 136;221

### TECH-T13 · Bug ticket pipeline with severity classes
- **what:** A bug flow where nothing gets lost.
- **how:** Latest daily build only. Testers work from design docs and checklists in subteams. File only with repro steps. Manager assigns a class (S: hangs or possible save corruption; A: blocks progression; lower: sometimes waived) and an owner by discipline (art, animation, code). Tester verifies the fix in several configurations; ticket set to resolved, not deleted. Cross-link or merge related tickets; rebalance overloaded people.
- **when:** From the first external test onward.
- **pitfalls:** Programmers as primary testers waste time; reports from old builds mislead.
- **episodes:** 208;94;50

### TECH-T14 · Machine and worldwide soak testing
- **what:** Let computers and distant teams find what humans miss.
- **how:** Run CPU-versus-CPU matches all night to surface bugs. Near the end of the project, have testers in Japan, North America and Europe pass save data back and forth.
- **when:** Games with near-limitless combinations or open worlds.
- **pitfalls:** Automated play finds crashes and hangs better than feel problems.
- **episodes:** 208

### TECH-T15 · Post-launch triage
- **what:** Decide what an update must and need not do.
- **how:** Must fix: crashing and fatal bugs. Optional: balance and quality-of-life changes, weighed against tying up the director and key staff. Prefer contained fixes; Ultimate lowered Challenger fight difficulty and added Very Easy to World of Light rather than change core CPU code. Treat player videos and reports as a major test pass. Regression-test before every patch.
- **when:** After release.
- **pitfalls:** Updates and DLC stretch the schedule (Smash for 3DS/Wii U wrapped about a year and a half after release).
- **episodes:** 231;141;136;135

### TECH-T16 · Sort-friendly label scheme
- **what:** Names that group and order themselves.
- **how:** Category, then modifiers, then numbers: Attack1 (neutral), Attack11/12/13 (combo steps), Attack4 (smash), AttackS4 (side smash), AttackAirF (forward aerial), SpecialHi (up special); directions N, Hi, S, Lw. Sort the folder and check the order. Use English.
- **when:** Project start.
- **pitfalls:** Keep player-facing names separate: players see Up Special because they input the direction first.
- **episodes:** 22

### TECH-T17 · Lock, merge and recover workflow
- **what:** Many people safely editing one project.
- **how:** All files on a server; lock to edit, upload to unlock. Allow concurrent edits on huge shared files with auto-merge and a named person resolving leftovers. Recover vanished edits from version history.
- **when:** Any team bigger than one.
- **pitfalls:** Reversions still happen often; don't assume the tool prevents lost work.
- **episodes:** 172

### TECH-T18 · Demo build hygiene
- **what:** A public demo that leaks nothing and breaks nothing.
- **how:** Keep spoiler content in separable files; build the demo from a stripped set; audit for leftover data; run near-full QA after removal; maintain it as a second version with its own tuning and updates.
- **when:** Any pre-release demo.
- **pitfalls:** Removing data greatly raises the chance something breaks.
- **episodes:** 122

### TECH-T19 · Worst-case performance budget
- **what:** Hold the frame rate in the heaviest scene.
- **how:** Pick the target at project start. Profile every stage or level with the maximum number of characters and effects (Ultimate: eight fighters at 60 fps on Switch). Let the weakest platform set shared limits and cut features it can't run (Ice Climbers on 3DS).
- **when:** From pre-production.
- **pitfalls:** Peripherals cost processing too: a twin-stick add-on took about 5% processor load in Kid Icarus: Uprising.
- **episodes:** 99;135;125;5

### TECH-T20 · Cheap rendering toolkit
- **what:** More on screen for less processing.
- **how:** Billboards for flashes, particles and simple objects, or a billboard body with 3D parts attached (King Bob-omb); orbit the camera to check. Particle rules: short lifetimes with higher speed, several particles packed into one billboard, staggered spawn timing and positions, fewer and larger billboards. Lock finger joints and swap pre-made patterns. Put detail where the camera looks; LOD only when worth the labor. Do color and light variation in the asset rather than heavy shaders.
- **when:** Busy scenes or modest hardware.
- **pitfalls:** Billboards look odd when the camera circles them; misused Y-axis billboards look too skinny.
- **episodes:** 153;244;210;44;10

### TECH-T21 · Memory-saving art tricks
- **what:** Fit more content into tiny memory.
- **how:** Share halves between look-alike characters, flip one image on both axes for motion, cap limb frames (three leg and two head patterns), reuse parts across variants. Store sprites as two 1-bit layers for three colors plus transparency; compress runs (0x8 instead of eight zeros). Replace per-frame collision with hand-authored paths matched to terrain when CPU is scarce.
- **when:** Tight memory or retro-style formats.
- **pitfalls:** Hand-authored patterns are labor-heavy and cost ROM; use them when processing is the scarcer resource.
- **episodes:** 100;183;244

### TECH-T22 · Data-driven CPU player
- **what:** An AI that reads data instead of the screen.
- **how:** Precompute per-character reach, attack timing and movement range; feed the AI the opponent's attack data, position and status. Add a reaction delay. Use behavior modes that switch with match state, and test where they conflict. Bias high levels toward offense; cap perfect defense.
- **when:** Fighting and action games with CPU opponents.
- **pitfalls:** Contradictions grow as mechanics and tracked criteria multiply.
- **episodes:** 221

### TECH-T23 · Stick dead zone and gesture sensitivity per controller
- **what:** Inputs that register the same way on every pad.
- **how:** Configure the lowest detected stick tilt for each console or controller. Expose a sensitivity setting for flick-to-dash style gestures.
- **when:** Every new platform or controller.
- **pitfalls:** An analog-dependent game can't be made fully playable on a 0-or-1 D-pad.
- **episodes:** 19;154;175

### TECH-T24 · Clock-seeded random numbers
- **what:** Randomness players can't farm by resetting.
- **how:** Factor the system's internal clock or another indefinite value into the random seed.
- **when:** Any random outcome players could gain from forcing.
- **pitfalls:** Fixed seeds after a reset let players replay identical results.
- **episodes:** 189

### TECH-T25 · Early compression test on capped media
- **what:** Find out how much fits before cutting content.
- **how:** Test compression on the largest asset sets (such as music) early in production.
- **when:** Cartridges or any capped download size.
- **pitfalls:** Sakurai feared moving Ultimate's music to free DLC until compression fit it in a quarter of Smash for's space.
- **episodes:** 160

## Case studies

### CASE-T01 · Lead-and-Helper co-op camera
- **what:** Make simultaneous co-op work in a jump-heavy scrolling game.
- **how:** Make one player the lead whom the camera follows; let the supporting player go off-camera; if the supporter drifts too far off-screen, warp them back to the lead (Kirby Super Star's 'Space Jump').
- **when:** Local co-op in side-scrollers where equal camera weight would drag players around.
- **pitfalls:** Characters whose abilities move at very different speeds make equal-weight scrolling even harder.
- **episodes:** 20

### CASE-T02 · Rebalance a movement ability through context
- **what:** Tame an over-strong ability without removing it.
- **how:** Shrink the playable vertical space (Kirby's Adventure enlarged the bottom info panel, which also made characters read bigger and gave room for ability art), confine key fights to one screen, and lower the ability's speed.
- **when:** When a screen-size or platform change makes a signature move too strong.
- **pitfalls:** Cutting the move outright and losing what made the character appealing.
- **episodes:** 11

### CASE-T03 · Charge drift
- **what:** Turn a risky maneuver into squeeze and release.
- **how:** Braking starts a charge, the charge builds through the drift, and releasing at the end gives a burst of speed. Pay the reward after the curve is cleared, not during it. Reject rewards with no tangible benefit (a score bonus) or that snowball (permanent power-ups).
- **when:** Racing or movement systems built around a risky action.
- **pitfalls:** Simply making drifting faster.
- **episodes:** 57;59

### CASE-T04 · Parameter-only variety
- **what:** Create many distinct vehicles or characters from one behavior.
- **how:** Write one shared behavior and a small number of types (Air Ride: star versus bike), then express every vehicle's character as parameters. Rework code so changing a few numbers changes the feel, and have the designer write all the parameters and specs.
- **when:** Rosters of vehicles, characters or stages.
- **pitfalls:** Separate code paths per variant; parameters only one person can edit.
- **episodes:** 59;135;74

### CASE-T05 · Memory-saving art and authored motion
- **what:** Fit more game into tiny memory and processing budgets.
- **how:** Build look-alike variants from one shared half, animate simple objects by flipping one frame on both axes, cap limb frames (Poppy Bros. Sr.: three leg and two head patterns), mirror a big boss and place it as background, and hand-author enemy paths that line up with the terrain instead of running collision checks. Give the richest art to scenes where one major character is on screen alone.
- **when:** Severe memory or CPU limits.
- **pitfalls:** Hand-authored paths are wasteful of authoring time; only worth it when processing is the bottleneck.
- **episodes:** 100

### CASE-T06 · Size targets for the screen and the audience
- **what:** Make targets readable and hittable.
- **how:** On small handheld screens, scale standard enemies well up (Uprising: four or five times Pit's size). For beginner-focused games, make bosses big enough that slightly off-target attacks still connect. When the weakest screen sets limits, slow launch speeds so action stays readable.
- **when:** Handheld shooters, beginner games, multi-platform releases.
- **pitfalls:** Tiny distant targets that make players focus on a small part of the screen.
- **episodes:** 125;42;100;135

### CASE-T07 · Flick input
- **what:** Get a stronger action from timing rather than extra buttons.
- **how:** Read how quickly the analog stick reaches the edge of its range: a fast flick triggers a dash, a smash attack or a strong jump, while tilt amount scales walking speed. Offer a flick-sensitivity option to reduce accidental dashes. Uprising reused flicks for dashing and dodging.
- **when:** Analog-stick action games.
- **pitfalls:** Accidental dashes for players with heavy thumbs.
- **episodes:** 27;154;125

### CASE-T08 · Context-sensitive attack button
- **what:** Keep controls simple while offering ranged and melee attacks.
- **how:** Use one attack button and let the game choose ranged fire or melee based on the equipped weapon and distance to the enemy; combine dashes with normal, charged and rapid fire for shot variety.
- **when:** Shooters with both melee and ranged attacks.
- **pitfalls:** Ambiguous choices if the distance threshold isn't readable.
- **episodes:** 125

### CASE-T09 · Wager difficulty (Fiend's Cauldron)
- **what:** Let players bet resources on difficulty for bigger rewards.
- **how:** Start at a default intensity (Uprising: 2.0). Spending currency raises it for tougher enemies and better rewards; failing spills some of the bet and lowers intensity on continue; spending can also lower it, down to 0.0, nearly invincible. Make clear to newcomers that lowering is possible.
- **when:** Single-player games that want to reward strong play.
- **pitfalls:** Uprising didn't make lowering intensity clear enough to beginners, and the game felt too hardcore.
- **episodes:** 41

### CASE-T10 · Fixed-length segments for music sync
- **what:** Time music to exact gameplay beats.
- **how:** Use segments with a known length (Uprising's air battles last five minutes) so music can hit specific beats and moods, and write the scenario on the development side so story beats match. A mid-boss can shift the clear time, which a live orchestra had to follow at PRESS START 2012.
- **when:** On-rails or scripted segments with strong music.
- **pitfalls:** Free-roaming sections where length varies can't be synced the same way.
- **episodes:** 125;227

### CASE-T11 · Separate parameter sets per mode
- **what:** Keep characters playable when they move between very different modes.
- **how:** Give each character its own jump and movement parameters for a mode whose demands differ from the core (Brawl's side-scrolling Subspace Emissary versus Versus), and budget time to tune both sets.
- **when:** Spin-off modes that reuse the main roster.
- **pitfalls:** Doubling the tuning workload for every character.
- **episodes:** 108

### CASE-T12 · Overpowered earns less
- **what:** Let players self-select difficulty without trivializing rewards.
- **how:** Allow players to brute-force content with strong loadouts as a chosen difficulty, but reduce the rewards when they are overpowered.
- **when:** Collection or RPG-like modes with equippable power.
- **pitfalls:** Players may feel punished if the reduction isn't visible.
- **episodes:** 160

### CASE-T13 · Strength layer for party modes only
- **what:** Power party modes and customization with variable strength while protecting competitive play.
- **how:** Build one parameter layer that can boost or alter character strength (stat boosts, equipment, alternate special moves) and use it for party modes, customization and trainable AI; keep it out of level-playing-field modes.
- **when:** Games serving both casual party play and serious competition.
- **pitfalls:** Variable strength leaking into competitive modes.
- **episodes:** 135

### CASE-T14 · Narrow post-launch fix
- **what:** Correct a launch problem without re-testing the whole game.
- **how:** When the root cause is unknown or the core is risky to touch, adjust the specific places players feel it (Ultimate lowered Challenger fight difficulty and added Very Easy to World of Light) instead of changing core functions.
- **when:** Patching a shipped game.
- **pitfalls:** A narrow fix treats symptoms; the cause may remain.
- **episodes:** 141

### CASE-T15 · Gamified unlocks
- **what:** Make unlocking characters a reward moment.
- **how:** Have each new character arrive as a challenger the player must beat to unlock them, and pace those encounters so they don't become frustrating.
- **when:** Games with a roster to unlock.
- **pitfalls:** Challengers arriving too often, or too hard for the player's level.
- **episodes:** 151

### CASE-T16 · Explain-the-depth site
- **what:** Bridge the gap when people misjudge a new concept.
- **how:** Turn your own documentation of how to play and why the game is deep into a public players' guide. Update it on a fixed, frequent schedule (Brawl's Dojo posted every weekday) and plan localization from the start (six languages for Brawl).
- **when:** New concepts that look shallow or strange from the outside.
- **pitfalls:** Letting updates lapse once the habit has formed.
- **episodes:** 143;245
