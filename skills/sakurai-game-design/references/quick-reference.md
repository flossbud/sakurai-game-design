# Quick reference: every principle

Compact list of every principle by area, most important first. Use `scripts/search.py` to get full rows (why, anti-pattern, example, episodes).

## Game essence (`--area essence`)

- **ESS-01** [CRITICAL] Put risk and reward side by side, in the right amounts: For each core interaction, map how risk and reward change with distance, position and timing, and make sure the spot where the player can score is also a spot where they are exposed. Attach fresh risk to rewards, like a kicked shell that pays points but can bounce back. Tie effectiveness to closeness, as Darius does by capping on-screen shots so firing rate rises as you close in. *(eps 6;134;148;155;157;159;193)*
- **ESS-02** [CRITICAL] Give every action a downside that opposes its benefit: List every verb with its risk and its reward. Where a downside is missing, add one: edge jumps that risk falling, dashes that cost control or stamina, long-range weapons with less ammo, strong attacks with longer recovery, flight that comes with falling or a requirement to fly. If you keep a risk-erasing option, counterweight it (limited ammo, enemies that cover the new angles). *(eps 6;65;77;82;134;193)*
- **ESS-03** [CRITICAL] Make the riskiest play pay the most: Rank your options by how much risk they demand (startup, exposure, accumulated danger, stake) and make the payoff climb with it. Give long-startup moves more damage and knockback, make multi-line clears require a tall stack, pay escalating points for chaining risky actions, and pay out more for higher wagered difficulty. *(eps 41;86;148;155;159;193;238)*
- **ESS-04** [CRITICAL] Squeeze players with stress, then release it into growth: For every section, name the source of stress, the moment of release, the growth reward and the progress it unlocks, and fix any missing step. Use squeeze-and-release in moment-to-moment play too, such as holding a charge through a risky drift and releasing it for a burst. *(eps 9;59;71;213)*
- **ESS-05** [CRITICAL] Understand why something is fun before you copy or rebuild it: Deconstruct (pick the fun part), analyze (state its risk and reward in one sentence), then rebuild (find a new verb that delivers the same payoff). Check whether your idea changes the concept or only renames the default action. When adapting or porting, carry the proven core over and spend effort on what the new format adds. *(eps 9;57;74;86;92;96;128;134;193;219)*
- **ESS-06** [HIGH] Make every rule earn its place by creating a new decision: Build up from a minimal core one mechanic at a time and write down the new decision each creates; cut any that create none. At each project's start, list the genre 'givens' you inherited and re-justify each. In one-button designs, build on timing, reaction or hold-versus-release rather than mashing. *(eps 27;51;82;148)*
- **ESS-07** [HIGH] Let reads and clever plays, not raw stats, decide contests: Give both sides counters and information: whiff punishes, guard-throw-attack cycles, specialties that can be read, signals that can be feinted. Tune so positioning, timing and reads can overcome stat or size advantages. *(eps 27;51;82;92;178)*
- **ESS-08** [HIGH] Offer choices with real upsides and real downsides: Give every weapon, unit, character and route a clear upside and a clear downside on different axes (coverage vs. shot count, cost vs. power, range vs. ammo, safe-and-long vs. risky shortcut). Build counter cycles. Show the effect (attack range, spread) instead of only numbers. *(eps 15;64;134;182;218;230)*
- **ESS-09** [HIGH] Place the best rewards beside the biggest hazards: Put power-ups, valuable enemies and shortcuts next to deadly geometry. Put ground, water and structures near flight paths and tie movement powers to the environment (launch off cliffs and ramps). Scale dungeon rewards with depth and make defeat cost the spoils so retreating is a real choice. *(eps 15;64;65;134)*
- **ESS-10** [HIGH] Let players choose how much risk to take: Offer optional risk with optional reward: press-on-or-retreat dungeons, escalating combo scores, wagers on difficulty, safe versus risky routes, partial cover. Map each system for ways to cut losses, raise gains, and trade a big loss for a bigger gain. *(eps 6;15;41;48;64;155)*
- **ESS-11** [HIGH] Let skilled players turn last-moment danger into big payoffs: Make parries trigger only at the moment of impact and pay heavily; give dodges a brief window where other actions are harder; add last-chance mechanics that let players survive at the edge; make reversals strongest at point-blank. *(eps 6;86;125;154;159;193)*
- **ESS-12** [HIGH] Give enemies a purpose beyond being defeated: Give players a simple way to use on-screen enemies (inhale, grab, spit) and give each enemy type something distinct to take. Give enemies cues that hint at weaknesses and reward exploiting them with more than damage, such as a combo setup. Keep weakness systems simple for casual audiences. *(eps 4;11;15)*
- **ESS-13** [HIGH] Make bosses demand a strategy, and decide how authored it is: For each boss decide up front: one authored solution, an ideal strategy found by trial and error, or many viable approaches. If authored, treat it as a tutorial in disguise; if free-form, add push and pull on purpose. Reward success clearly, and let aggressive positioning end the fight faster. *(eps 105;134;157;219)*
- **ESS-14** [HIGH] Plan rewards early and make players earn them: As soon as the bones of the game work, list what every enemy, mission and activity grants, when it arrives and where it is used. Gate characters, equipment and collectibles behind play, gamify unlocks (incoming challengers), and give casual players a grid of goals to work toward. *(eps 59;75;151;163;233)*
- **ESS-15** [HIGH] Turn rewards into growth the player chooses how to use: Pair every currency with desirable things to buy, including at least one expensive goal worth saving for. Show progress to the next reward (current vs. needed). Price crafting so the output is clearly worth more than its materials. Let players choose which skill or item to invest in. *(eps 9;23;71;75;151;203)*
- **ESS-16** [HIGH] Make losing part of the fun: Keep respawn and rematch times short, make the moment of defeat entertaining in itself, and write down how each death motivates a retry, aiming for 'I'll get you next time!'. Expect mid-match kills to raise the scorer's tension rather than relieve it. *(eps 97;117)*
- **ESS-17** [HIGH] Name your game's main source of fun; it may not be game essence: Name the main source of fun and put the polish budget there (fine-tune control feel if control is the fun). Audit agon, alea, mimicry and ilinx. Then layer risk and reward where it fits: fall deaths and dangerous gathering in crafting games, strict timing in rhythm games, a work-earn-upgrade loop in task games. *(eps 26;45;134;152;178;182;188;203)*
- **ESS-18** [MEDIUM] Pay off risk tangibly, after the risky act, without snowballing: Grant a useful, temporary benefit once the risky action is completed (a speed burst at the curve's exit), not throughout it. Avoid permanent stacking advantages in competitive play. If players can overpower themselves, reduce their rewards. *(eps 57;59;151;160)*
- **ESS-19** [MEDIUM] Raise the stakes as play goes on: Increase speed or pressure steadily across a session, and tie enemy speed or aggression to how many threats remain or to the player's own progress, so the order of clearing becomes a choice. *(eps 86;155)*
- **ESS-20** [MEDIUM] Leave room for players to beat the system within the rules: When testers find a balance-breaking trick, ask whether it stays inside the game's rules and whether finding it is fun before patching it. Consider a deliberate within-the-rules shortcut or a rare, lucrative enemy with high HP or a tendency to flee. Keep blocking anything that amounts to rewriting game parameters. *(eps 6;48;238)*
- **ESS-21** [MEDIUM] Give every contest a stake that people care about: Let spectators pick a winner before a match, show standing that moves with performance, keep both players' progress visible in races, and keep real stakes (no restarts) when showing play live. *(eps 120;170;227;241)*
- **ESS-22** [MEDIUM] Praise good play explicitly and give scores a meaning: Add crowd cheers for big launches and KOs and a gasp for near-misses, allies who comment on feats, generous labeled bonuses, and celebratory effects aimed at the scorer. Present scores in a unit that fits the fantasy and count gains up on screen. *(eps 59;95;200)*
- **ESS-23** [MEDIUM] Vary each session so players adapt instead of converging: Add a mode built for variety (a sandbox with differing power-ups and events), vary probabilities instead of fixing results where repetition goes stale, and where draws are random let players choose among two or three, each with a reason to matter. *(eps 59;120;128;135;178;189)*
- **ESS-24** [MEDIUM] Side with what's fun as a game over realism: When realism and fun conflict, choose fun, and make risky actions rewarding even if they wouldn't be in real life. Borrow realism for readable information (mimicry), not for the rules. *(eps 57;89;178)*

## Balance and difficulty (`--area balance`)

- **BAL-01** [CRITICAL] Pick the target player first, then set how much risk to allow: Write down the target player in terms of experience ('beginners', not 'children'), decide how much push and pull they want, then set the risk dials to match. If you go hardcore, make it an explicit goal rather than the default. *(eps 4;6;41;108;125;148;213;226)*
- **BAL-02** [CRITICAL] Be kind to beginners: neutralize threats early and forgive small slips: Give beginners ways to stop threats while the risk is still low, a forgiving health pool, an escape from the steepest penalties, big targets that near-misses still hit, and an opening window where deaths cost little. Keep some penalty for real failure. In competitive play, let newcomers act and have fun before they get flattened. *(eps 4;100;148;156;157;242)*
- **BAL-03** [CRITICAL] Never let a player get permanently stuck: Let players change difficulty at any time without losing progress. After repeated failures in one spot, offer an easier setting or a skip that counts as cleared, worded so it doesn't humiliate. Provide a floor that is nearly guaranteed to win. If a shipped game proves too hard, patch in an easier option. Teach newcomers explicitly how to turn difficulty down. *(eps 41;55;141;213)*
- **BAL-04** [CRITICAL] Don't tune difficulty to your team; handicap yourself, then go easier: Tune while handicapped: play one-handed, with lower health or stricter conditions, and make sure you can still clear it. Set difficulty a good deal below what feels right to the team, then lower it once more. Bring in people who have never played and use each one fresh only once; watch children play rather than asking them questions. *(eps 128;148;248;256)*
- **BAL-05** [CRITICAL] Get players back in control within seconds of failing: Measure death-to-control time and cut it: a flashy, impactful failure effect of about a second, short loads, no non-interactive screens (if text must remain, let button presses advance it). Make sure enemies can't hit players the moment they respawn, but don't make respawning so free that the game feels mindless. *(eps 97;117)*
- **BAL-06** [CRITICAL] Keep every option's peaks and valleys; never average them out: When something is too strong, first exaggerate its weaknesses instead of cutting its signature strength, and don't soften weaknesses in the same patch. Reject proposals that make a character more average. Push interchangeable options apart. This applies to move sets in non-competitive games too. *(eps 19;218;220;247)*
- **BAL-07** [HIGH] Fix balance with the narrowest change that keeps the signature intact: List the move's parameters, mark the defining one, and change a secondary one instead: lengthen the vulnerable follow-through or shrink the hitbox. For an overpowered ability, try shrinking the play space, confining key fights to one screen or lowering its speed before removing it. Post-launch, adjust specific encounters or add an easier option rather than rewriting core AI. *(eps 11;77;141;247)*
- **BAL-08** [HIGH] Scale every penalty and reward to how good or bad the action was: For every reward, penalty and damage value ask how good or bad the triggering action really was. Give irreversible choices an explicit recovery path and cost. Check whether a death that costs the player their build still leaves a realistic way back. Keep the mapping loose enough to surprise: polish out only what feels unreasonable. *(eps 4;113;117;156;238)*
- **BAL-09** [HIGH] Shape difficulty like a mountain trail, not a steady ramp: Plot player power against enemy difficulty across the game and reshape it into deliberate peaks (bosses that demand a strategy) and flats (unexpectedly empty areas, spots to rack up gains). Don't level-scale enemies to the player. Check every growth stage for dips. Playtest the endgame for spikes. *(eps 92;105;213)*
- **BAL-10** [HIGH] If failure means repetition, give players a reason to push on: For each likely failure point, list what players will replay on the way back, then shorten it or vary it (randomized equipment, items, events). Make the next area, story beat or reward visible or hinted. If you borrow permadeath or Souls-like systems, write down your own reason to push on. *(eps 117;128;189)*
- **BAL-11** [HIGH] Layer optional depth over a simple core both audiences can use: Build a minimal core that can finish the game or compete, add depth on top, and verify a core-only playthrough works. Offer a one-button version of special inputs and give the full version a modest bonus (a bit more damage or invincibility). Keep novice extras optional and skippable. *(eps 11;27;159;169;226)*
- **BAL-12** [HIGH] Size complexity to your audience and lifespan; hold back what they can't feel: Decide whether the game is for casual sessions or hundreds of matches and size its depth to match. Before adding a rule, check your target players could still grasp the game. Keep inputs easy enough to feel the push and pull, and lower speed and input density for casual audiences or laggy online play. Keep weakness and combo systems simple in casual games. *(eps 15;41;82;108;148)*
- **BAL-13** [HIGH] Tune risk through explicit dials, not a vague difficulty knob: List your risk dials (player hitbox size, partial cover, threat-neutralizing range, hits the player can take, escape tools, control inertia, game speed, target size, enemy durability, how freely players can leave) and set each deliberately. When you lower risk in one layer, decide whether to add it back in another. *(eps 4;6;15;20;100;148)*
- **BAL-14** [HIGH] Decide on purpose how much your game evens out skill: Choose early: casual (everyone takes turns winning) or skill-reflecting. For casual play, give trailing players speed boosts or stronger items. For skill-reflecting play, leave core stats alone and route catch-up through chaos: make power-ups harder for the leader to get, charge comeback meters faster for losers. Keep variable-strength systems in party modes, out of level-playing-field matches. For home play, tolerate quirks players can house-rule. *(eps 135;189;202)*
- **BAL-15** [HIGH] Never let luck alone decide a player's loss: Give players ways to respond to or control random events, keep randomness out of strict skill contests, prototype strategy combat without random evasion first, and keep random elements that still demand skill. Seed random numbers with an unpredictable value such as the system clock so resets can't force results. *(eps 120;189;221;230)*
- **BAL-16** [HIGH] Let difficulty be a choice with stakes, not a humiliation: Let players wager currency to raise difficulty for proportionally better rewards and pay to lower it; on failure, ease off automatically and take part of the stake. Give in-world levers that push difficulty back. If players can overpower themselves, treat it as a self-chosen difficulty and pay fewer rewards. Lock difficulty-lowering assists behind an unlock step so nobody flips them by accident. *(eps 41;55;156;160;213)*
- **BAL-17** [HIGH] Check balance with testers and data, but let feel decide: Staff dedicated testers, use online and tournament results where you can, and test mirror matches for built-in side advantages. Look at multiplayer matches as well as top-tournament usage. Make final calls on feel, and step in as director mainly when an option's individuality is threatened. *(eps 135;218;220;230;247)*
- **BAL-18** [MEDIUM] Make CPU opponents human-paced, aggressive and beatable: Add a reaction delay, let status effects like reversed controls briefly confuse CPUs before they adapt, bias high levels toward offense, and cap perfect shields and dodges. Test for contradictions between AI behavior modes. *(eps 221)*
- **BAL-19** [MEDIUM] Show rank so most players feel they are succeeding: Show standing as a number that rises with skill, measured against the whole population. Hide the identity and score of the #1 player from general players. Give top players an elite tier that matches them with each other. Tune matchmaking range by region and time of day, widening only as needed. *(eps 170)*
- **BAL-20** [MEDIUM] Re-check difficulty after late performance changes, on real hardware: Find any gameplay timing tied to processing speed, freeze it before final tuning, and replay difficulty-critical content after every optimization pass and on the actual retail hardware. Take it seriously when several people independently report the same feel problem. *(eps 74;92;141)*
- **BAL-21** [MEDIUM] Check that co-op doesn't make the game harder than solo: Playtest co-op for power-ups budgeted for one player, players blocking each other, and a camera or scroll point that strands the trailing player. Consider a lead-and-helper structure where an off-screen partner warps back. *(eps 20;157)*
- **BAL-22** [MEDIUM] Give mismatched players handicap tools, and use them yourself: Expose difficulty and resources (lives, health, aggression) as separate per-player settings, offer asymmetric fail conditions, include an automatic handicap option, and ease off when you play newcomers. *(eps 155;157;202;242)*

## Planning, structure and pacing (`--area planning`)

- **PLAN-01** [CRITICAL] Fix one clear concept before production, then steer by it: Before anyone builds content, write the concept as one statement of appeal (for example 'the fun of sending opponents flying'), and do not start production until it is that clear. Use the gap before full staffing to finish the design documents and win stakeholder buy-in. When doubt sets in, check the decision against the concept. Amplify emergent fun that heads the same way instead of switching direction. *(eps 187;29;132;160;113;261)*
- **PLAN-02** [CRITICAL] Prove the core fun in a bare prototype before full production: Build only the core rules first: Smash's prototype had no special moves, dodges or items. Mock up key characters at their intended on-screen size with complete movesets. For a non-game or content project, finish two or three representative pieces at final quality as a go/no-go test, then show them to partners and rights holders. Pitch moving mechanics with animated mock-ups, not static text. *(eps 27;100;261;29;74;34)*
- **PLAN-03** [CRITICAL] Hand players control and the core fun within the first minutes: Time a first boot: a newcomer should be playing, and should grasp the core fun, within three minutes. Open on a climactic scene the player controls and explain how things got there later (a slice from later in the story, or a battle as other characters). Show early that the enemy means business. Let repeated presses of confirm lead straight into the main game. If the game must open on everyday life, keep it short and hand over control early. *(eps 17;235;113;125;240)*
- **PLAN-04** [CRITICAL] Measure and maximize the time the player is in control: Time a session in three buckets: in control, not in control (cutscenes, loading, menus, transitions, forced animations) and empty time with nothing to do, then set targets to shrink the last two. Keep transitions but make them quick. Hide unavoidable loads in presentation or show a simple on-theme indicator. Cut the gap between 'Ugh, I blew it!' and regaining control: a punchy failure effect of about a second, then control back, with no enemies waiting on the respawn. Skip the trip home once an errand is done. *(eps 73;250;117;246;78;64;180)*
- **PLAN-05** [HIGH] Plan to trim: cut what you can't finish, lock what you can't lose: Weigh candidate concepts partly by estimated development time. Keep a visible list of what the whole game still needs, not just each person's tasks. Decide early which features are must-ship (a hands-on tutorial, for example) and which are trim candidates. When a target such as a stable 60 fps is non-negotiable, pare content until it holds. Re-weigh remaining cost against realistic return before entering the expensive final stretch. *(eps 258;27;110;20;135;160;173)*
- **PLAN-06** [HIGH] Treat play time as a cost; build fun first and let length follow: Do not plan or market around an hour count. Write down the core fun experiences and the systems that reinforce them first, and size content after. Prefer dense, varied content to padding, and give a solo mode an appropriate length. If failure sends players back, plan what they will see on each retry so repetition still pays. *(eps 250;20;72;198;128;108;113)*
- **PLAN-07** [HIGH] Teach by letting them play, and only what they don't already know: Teach each action where progression first needs it: an early real obstacle that requires it, a small enclosed area that can only be left by using a new movement skill, short prompts along the opening path. Build a mode that doubles as training, tailored per character. Skip genre conventions (at most show the attack-button icon when the first enemy appears), but test onboarding with genre newcomers. Make prompts read the current button mapping, and lock the hands-on tutorial into the schedule. *(eps 17;85;173;175;242;219)*
- **PLAN-08** [HIGH] Open with a constrained slice of the core, then add controls: Begin with a restricted form of the core action, such as an on-rails segment, a single-screen fight, or the basic verb alone, and add free movement, dodging and depth systems afterward. Check that a novice can make progress with only the simple core. Consider a no-penalty 'trial time' for first-timers. *(eps 125;11;4;157;85)*
- **PLAN-09** [HIGH] Name your audience and hook in one line before designing: Write one line that names the target player or the signature mechanic, the way each of his works is captioned ('tailor-made for newcomers'; 'Rack up damage, then smash foes off the screen!'). Define the audience by experience or need, note who already serves them, and then tune risk to match. *(eps 2;4;148;226;112;7;125)*
- **PLAN-10** [HIGH] Offer, and pitch, something players can't get anywhere else: Open the proposal by describing your game's own core experience without naming another game. List the reasons a player would pick yours over the original, many rather than one, and check again near the end of development. Form the concept first and use market data only to check its risks. Save 'it's like X' for sequels and informal chats with people who share the reference. *(eps 72;161;196;226;198;40)*
- **PLAN-11** [HIGH] Question every inherited "given" at project kickoff: At kickoff, list the conventions inherited from the genre or your last game (jump physics, enemies to defeat, blocks that vanish, how bosses are beaten) and decide whether each still earns its place. For every new idea, ask whether it changes what the player does or only what it is called. Keep de facto control standards unless you have a deliberate reason to break them. *(eps 82;74;86;71;9;125;219)*
- **PLAN-12** [HIGH] Spec enough to start; expect the fun to surface while building: Fix the concept and core rules, write enough spec to start, and settle details as implementation reveals them. Schedule polish as fun-making work, not cleanup. When a problem appears, the person who spots it reports it at once with everything they know; lay out every option and decide quickly. *(eps 29;52;187;164;158;261)*
- **PLAN-13** [HIGH] Write specs nobody can misread: units, frames, bracketed parameters: Keep tunable numbers out of code. In specs, wrap each tunable value in a plain-language bracket with a placeholder, for example '[Special startup X-velocity is O]', under the rule that anything in brackets becomes a parameter, and keep a list pairing each phrase with its value. Pick one unit of speed for the project and include it in every parameter name. Give timings in frames. Keep specs in a collapsible outline (character, then moves, then each move's spec). *(eps 68;84;76;137;14;46)*
- **PLAN-14** [HIGH] Write proposals hot and fast, one image and idea per page: Itemize the outline, then write the whole proposal in one push without discussing it; consult others once the draft exists. Give each page one screenshot or diagram and about two lines of text, grouped into categories and subcategories with visible section markers. Cut graphs, research, grand-vision passages and the plan B. Talk around each slide instead of reading it. Judge each page's information, not the page count. If it is rejected, rework how it shows the appeal instead of blaming the reviewers. *(eps 38;52;12;144;74;261)*
- **PLAN-15** [HIGH] Commit to one ideal spec; leave Plan B out: Before sending a proposal or spec, delete every alternative and keep the option you judge best overall, weighing ease of implementation and appeal yourself. Then invite team feedback and trim for cost together when concrete problems surface, falling back to an alternative only when they justify it. *(eps 109;12;52;21)*
- **PLAN-16** [HIGH] Plan rewards at the planning stage, and fit their type to the concept: Once the bones of the game exist, list every reward type and roughly when it arrives, and add systems before production if the list is thin. Make sure saved-up currency has appealing things to buy. If stat growth would undermine the concept, reward with unlocks, collectibles, options and objectives. Let rewards help with the next challenge and offer choices. Don't lock content many players want from the start, and don't make shareable movies the payoff. *(eps 151;75;9;163)*
- **PLAN-17** [HIGH] Keep the next objective visible and within reach: Show progress as current versus required (EXP now and EXP to the next level) and make every action move it. Make key purchases few, expensive and powerful. Use a goal grid that reveals its neighbors' conditions as each panel clears. At several points in a playthrough, ask what the player is working toward; if the answer is unclear or out of reach, add a nearer objective. If there is no ending, place the goal so far away players can't see it. *(eps 23;151;59;64;203;128)*
- **PLAN-18** [HIGH] Shape progression as peaks and flats, not a steady incline: Plot player power and challenge across the whole game and reshape it into peaks (bosses that demand a strategy and pay off with a clear reward) and flats (an unexpectedly empty area inside a dense zone, a spot to rack up gains, a short scene as a breather). Vary the scenery along the way. In open or multi-path games, don't scale every enemy with the player. *(eps 105;9;71;233;15)*
- **PLAN-19** [HIGH] Write story around play: the protagonist's view, the player's victories: Start with gameplay, not story, and brief players while they play (in-play dialogue, with an option to turn it off). Trim long backstory about allies and villains. Never let a cutscene undo a fight the player just won; if a companion must leave, protect the balance and items the player invested. Tie story beats to the challenge ahead. Break these rules only on purpose. *(eps 113;26;125;64;235)*
- **PLAN-20** [HIGH] Keep cutscenes lean and purposeful, and never make them the reward: Review every cutscene: keep deliberate breathers and beats that play cannot carry, and shorten or cut the ones that are just downtime. Treat showcase movies as marketing everyone can watch. Test any quick-time event with players very carefully before committing to it. *(eps 73;233;163;17;113)*
- **PLAN-21** [HIGH] Let players skip, pause and speed through anything non-interactive: Make every cutscene, logo and the credits skippable from the first playthrough, all at once if possible. Make cutscenes pausable. Offer line-by-line skip and fast-forward, or let voiced lines play while the player keeps playing. Have any button press during a cutscene bring up a skip prompt. *(eps 233;180;211;104)*
- **PLAN-22** [HIGH] Collect every hard limit at kickoff and design inside it: At kickoff, list every guideline and hard limit (ratings, platform rules, regional rules on depicting gambling, storage size, processing headroom on the weakest target) and check planned visuals and features against them. Let the weakest platform set shared limits. Budget several adjustment rounds for region-sensitive features. If you write guidelines yourself, state their purpose and allow justified exceptions. *(eps 139;120;135;160;125;100)*
- **PLAN-23** [HIGH] Budget the hidden costs each feature drags in: Before approving a feature, estimate its testing, code and maintenance cost. Schedule programmer time for each unique mechanic, reserve people for post-launch fixes, and plan a demo as a small separate game that needs QA. When volume matters, choose lightweight formats (an image plus a gameplay effect rather than a model and written description). *(eps 136;206;122;87;231;151;108;135)*
- **PLAN-24** [MEDIUM] Deliver volume as short, distinct chunks with their own rules: Split content into self-contained modes or segments, picked from a hub, each with a rule no other has. If two end up playing the same, cut or change one. Use fixed-length segments where you want music or events to hit exact beats, and give competitive sessions short, concrete goals. *(eps 20;135;125;227;241)*
- **PLAN-25** [MEDIUM] Give every character and option an identity in a word or two: Define a neutral baseline for your rules, then push each character's parameters well away from it and give some a one-of-a-kind mechanic, with programming budgeted for it. Write a one- or two-word identity for every character, enemy and option; if you can't, push further. Give each option a clear trade-off and let players test it right away. *(eps 206;182;108;74;218)*
- **PLAN-26** [MEDIUM] Spend a sequel's head start on purpose, and make it clearly more fun: At sequel kickoff, list what carries over (team, tools, assets, hardware architecture) and decide where the saved effort goes. Write how the sequel is more fun and more impactful than the last game, and list concrete changes for every element. Re-examine inherited rules, and if the tempo changes, retune every parameter. *(eps 164;40;160;82;135)*

## Game feel (`--area feel`)

- **FEEL-01** [CRITICAL] Make every button press visibly register the moment it happens: On the first frame after an attack input, jump to a clearly different pose instead of easing out of idle. Give every accepted press an unambiguous signal (pose, sound, highlight). In menus accept confirm from more than one button; during cutscenes let any press bring up a skip prompt. Shape hit sounds so they reach a strong level almost instantly. Count the frames from press to first visible change for every action and treat that number as a design value. *(eps 19;36;49;104;124;167)*
- **FEEL-02** [CRITICAL] Stop the action at every big moment: hits, boss defeats and deaths: Freeze attacker and target briefly when a hit lands. On a boss defeat, freeze and flash the screen before the defeat sequence. When the player takes damage or loses a life, pause for an instant so the point of impact is visible. For the biggest moments (finishing blows, boss kills) layer a full stop with screen shake, a flash and slow motion. Compare builds with and without the stop. *(eps 3;47;36;62;142;225;131)*
- **FEEL-03** [CRITICAL] Hunt down input lag across the whole display and audio chain: Before any playtest, demo, capture or event, press jump in a game that responds instantly (a 2D NES game, not a 3D one) on that exact setup and confirm the character moves the moment you press. Use Game Mode or Low Latency Mode, or turn off every picture enhancement. Check sound separately and use an analog or low-latency audio path if hit sounds arrive late. Convert latency specs to frames: 16.67 ms is one frame at 60 fps. Rule out display lag before retuning any timing. *(eps 8;156;49;108;19;252)*
- **FEEL-04** [CRITICAL] Make attacks that look like they connect actually connect: Make collision wider than the visible weapon in fast games and make sure each attack's range crosses the median line where opponents stand. During hit stop, shake only the rendered model. Give every hit but the last of a multi-hit move fixed knockback. Test strings against every reaction stance so the target's top node doesn't drift. When a hit feels off, adjust the hitbox and the animation together. Check reach in every direction and in ground and aerial versions. *(eps 225;165;47;145;142;62;253)*
- **FEEL-05** [CRITICAL] Keep every wait and transition hasty, but don't cut it out entirely: Time every fade, black screen and non-interactive stretch, then trim each one to 'a hasty speed' that still reads as a transition. Hide unavoidable loading inside an in-world moment or a themed wipe; if you can't, show a simple, world-styled loading indicator straight away. Don't time presentation to development-build load times, which will change. Let any press during a cutscene bring up a skip, and let cutscenes pause. *(eps 246;73;78;180;104)*
- **FEEL-06** [CRITICAL] Make failure hit hard for about a second, then hand back control: Measure the time from failure to control. Keep the failure effect flashy and to 'a second or so' (genre-dependent), then return control as soon as possible. Trim loads and non-interactive screens; if a step must remain, make it player-advanced. Don't respawn players into enemy fire. Keep some real cost so respawning never feels mindless. *(eps 117;3;73)*
- **FEEL-07** [HIGH] Specify, feel and verify every timing in frames: Assume a target rate (at 60 fps, 60 frames is one second). Train by clapping '1-and-2-and-3-and': about 30 frames per beat, 15 per half beat. Anchor on known moves (about 15 frames from Smash Attack input to release, about 15 for a standard Tekken kick's startup, a bit under half that for a Street Fighter II heavy punch) and estimate from the 0/15/30 anchors. Write specs as frame numbers (attack start frame, total frames) and speeds per frame. Lock processing speed and frame timing before final tuning. *(eps 5;76;36;14;68;84;74)*
- **FEEL-08** [HIGH] Sell hit stop with shake, scaling and blending, not a bare freeze: Shake the victim more and the attacker less, moving only the rendered model. Shake grounded characters horizontally only and airborne ones in all directions, starting big and decaying over the precomputed stop length. Compute stop length from attack strength, then apply a per-move multiplier; lengthen it for lightning and shorten it in multiplayer. Increase shake when the camera is pulled back. Blend the victim into the damage pose over about four frames. Keep a sword attacker's animation creeping forward very slowly (per-attack toggle). Let hit marks keep animating and let the hit sound's main body finish during the stop. *(eps 47;131;142;167;179)*
- **FEEL-09** [HIGH] Front-load the hit and make strong attacks pay for it in recovery: Lay out each attack so the hit registers about 1/4 to 1/3 of the way in (about 1/5 for fast moves), with standard attacks finishing in under a second. Scale recovery with power; even Smash Attacks with large openings almost never exceed one second. When a move is too strong, lengthen its follow-through or shrink its hitbox rather than cutting what defines it. *(eps 77;36;238;247;49)*
- **FEEL-10** [HIGH] Return control when players expect it, using cancel frames: Mark a cancel window in the latter part of each recovery. When an input arrives during it, blend from the current pose into the new action. Playtest whether the window lines up with when players try to act, and keep the recovery pose visibly held up to that point. *(eps 36;77;194)*
- **FEEL-11** [HIGH] Shape the jump's rise, fall and air control to your game's goal: Build the jump from an initial upward velocity plus downward acceleration added every frame, with a maximum fall speed. Use separate rise and fall gravity. Decide air control on purpose. For slams, make the descent faster than the ascent. If jump and up-attacks share an input, keep a short, uniform pre-jump windup. For forgiving escapes, use a very strong burst then heavy gravity for a few frames before normal gravity. Ask each project whether you need a jump at all. *(eps 37;193;65;61)*
- **FEEL-12** [HIGH] Sell big impacts with screen shake tuned to the camera: Author a few distinct presets (Smash uses large, medium, small, extra-small) with different axis behavior, not scaled copies. Start strong and decay with a little randomness. Shake the camera's look-at point as much as the camera or more. Scale amplitude by camera distance and field of view, and retune scenes with unusual FOV. Reserve it for impacts that deserve it, and pair it with a brief full-screen flash only for the heaviest ones. *(eps 103;3;56;47;223)*
- **FEEL-13** [HIGH] Tune on-screen response so each character has its own weight: Decide each character's intended weight, then tune speed, inertia and jump physics to it and compare characters with identical inputs. Run them on one shared behavior and express differences as parameter sets. Reserve schedule time just to sense and fine-tune movement. Set analog tilt sensitivity per console. *(eps 19;26;118;124;96;148;59;108)*
- **FEEL-14** [HIGH] Frame the camera for the gameplay, not just to follow the character: Track the center point less (or not at all) on the Y axis during jumps. Let the character drift off center during dodges and drifts. In side-scrollers show more space ahead. In co-op scrollers make one player the camera's lead and warp stragglers back. When a character is knocked flying, stop scrolling, then gently recenter once they settle. Treat camera inertia as a tunable value. *(eps 61;20;100;103;125;42)*
- **FEEL-15** [HIGH] Keep controls simple and physical, and no busier than the game needs: Map moves to direction plus button and use input timing (a quick flick) for stronger variants. Scale walk speed by tilt and trigger dashes with a fast flick instead of a double-tap or held button. Try the core action on one button before adding more. Make the dominant action's button the most prominent only when actions are unequal. On new hardware, list what the device uniquely does and adopt only the uses that fit. Lower input density for casual audiences or laggy online play. *(eps 27;51;154;125;108;147;169)*
- **FEEL-16** [HIGH] Never let a deliberate input feel canceled or misread: Decide ledge behavior per action: keep short stepping attacks and forward dodges on the platform, let fast rushes carry over, stop long rushes late in the move, and use speed thresholds for rolling moves. For walking, consider requiring a push toward the edge or steering along near-parallel ledges. Keep a short, uniform windup where two inputs share a direction. Expose flick sensitivity as a setting. Playtest ledges and edge cases by feel. *(eps 102;37;154;104)*
- **FEEL-17** [HIGH] Build knockback as a tunable payoff with per-attack parameters: Compute knockback from attack damage and the target's accumulated damage. Give each attack a free launch angle, an optional modifier toward the attacker's direction of travel, a multiplier, a guaranteed minimum kept in check by scaling, and an optional fixed value. Let target weight and fall speed shape the arc. Cap launch speed for readability on the smallest target screen. *(eps 145;178;238;135;160;100;248)*
- **FEEL-18** [HIGH] Choose your frame rate on purpose and protect it: Pick the target rate early by weighing control smoothness against the rendering budget. If the game is timing-heavy, hold a stable 60 and pare content and effects rather than dropping to 30. Keep game speed independent of how many frames are drawn; a variable frame rate is not slowdown. Check high refresh rates against displays that can show them. *(eps 5;135;156;99;96)*
- **FEEL-19** [MEDIUM] Use genre-standard controls, keep series rules stable, and allow remapping: Map core actions to the genre's standard buttons unless you have a deliberate reason not to. Keep rotation rules, previews and similar fundamentals identical across a series. Ship a remapping screen: a drawn controller, pick the button with the cursor then the action, let several buttons share an action, and save named profiles. Expose stick and flick sensitivity. Remap awkward legacy schemes when re-releasing old games. *(eps 175;139;156;197;55;154;19)*
- **FEEL-20** [MEDIUM] Retune every feel parameter when tempo, mode or hardware changes: When the game's speed changes, budget time to rebuild movement and combat values rather than porting them. Give characters a separate movement parameter set for very different modes. Let the weakest target screen set shared speed limits. Redo tuning if performance changes late. *(eps 160;108;135;74;5;84)*
- **FEEL-21** [MEDIUM] State speeds in shared units and judge them against body size: Pick one unit per project (pixels per frame for sprites; km/h or distance per frame for real-scale 3D), document it and put it in parameter names. Build at real-world scale if you want real speeds. Judge speeds against the character's own size. Practice estimating speeds by eye before checking the number. *(eps 84;68;5)*
- **FEEL-22** [MEDIUM] Treat the screen as a small window and spend it on play: Before choosing camera and field of view, decide what must fit on screen. Prefer views that leave more room for play. If targets look tiny, scale them up. Weigh VR's blur and head-turning costs, and favor it where gaze and heading differ. *(eps 42;125;61)*
- **FEEL-23** [MEDIUM] Offer comfort toggles instead of stripping presentation for everyone: List inertia-heavy camera effects (footstep bob, vehicle-frame sway, blur) and give each an option. Lock cockpit or vehicle frames in place in a comfort mode. Offer a blur-strength setting. Cap shake and flash so the screen stays readable. Recruit testers who actually get sick. *(eps 129;192;103;223)*
- **FEEL-24** [MEDIUM] Tune feel by hand, with exposed parameters and fast iteration: Keep every speed, timing and force in parameters outside the code, name them in plain language with units, and push edits to builds in one step. Build debug commands for the setups you test most (set damage percentage, slow motion, hide background). Vary one value at a time and note how it feels. *(eps 118;46;68;59;195;79;102)*

## Animation (`--area animation`)

- **ANIM-01** [CRITICAL] Snap to a big lead-in pose on the frame the button is pressed: Skip easing frames out of standby: make the first frame after input a drastically different windup pose. For charge moves, hold that pose and shake in place while the button is held. Light, spammable moves can have no lead-in at all. If a windup reads as slow, have the character pull directly into the right pose rather than lengthening the ease. *(eps 36;49;150;253)*
- **ANIM-02** [CRITICAL] Animate where and when the attack's collision actually is: Give animators each attack's active frames and hitbox placement, and review attacks with collision displayed. Make the motion cross the median line where opponents stand and stay in the gameplay plane rather than angling toward or away from the camera. When a hit feels wrong, fix the arc or pose and the hitbox shape or timing together. Watch for stance changes on hit that move the target's top node. *(eps 225;165;62;142;253;50)*
- **ANIM-03** [HIGH] Make the hit stop frame the attack's strongest, clearest pose: Treat the contact frame as a key pose: include it in reference photos and review it on its own. On sword swings, start collision partway into the swing once the pose is strong, and let hit stop kick in after the blade has swung out a bit. Check the pose on both the left and right of the screen. *(eps 62;225;36;179;142)*
- **ANIM-04** [HIGH] Exaggerate past "OK": too much is just right: Push twists, reach and extension past natural limits, until the rig's weighting nearly breaks. Treat motion capture as a starting point and make it bigger. Make a sword swing more than 'just a sword swing'. Scale exaggeration to the character's on-screen size and camera distance. *(eps 28;91;166;236;62;150;142;50)*
- **ANIM-05** [HIGH] Reach key poses fast; don't let interpolation water them down: Scrub interpolated attacks and cut in-between time so the key poses arrive quickly. Pull the windup further back, hold the anticipation relatively long, then pass through the key poses in as little as one frame each. Give poses more energy than the reference. Judge snappiness by eye in game, not by frame count. *(eps 150;194;36;49)*
- **ANIM-06** [HIGH] Make the follow-through big, held and unlike standby: Keep the motion going with big movements that carry the strike's momentum before returning to standby. Hold a pose clearly different from standby. Give it its own trajectory instead of reversing the attack. Give landings a full follow-through, and place follow-throughs where they read from the game's camera. *(eps 77;36;150;253)*
- **ANIM-07** [HIGH] Make the first damage frame an extreme pain pose: Author the hit-stop frame of each damage animation as an exaggerated, asymmetric pain pose, and segue into it with a quick blend from the pose at impact rather than a snap. Choose high, middle or low reactions by the nearest impact point and small, medium or large by strength, with a separate knockback set. Twist bodies in knockback so arms and legs read. Enter the damage animation immediately, even from special attacks. *(eps 142;47;165;166;253)*
- **ANIM-08** [HIGH] Pose for a clear, asymmetric silhouette from the game's camera: Make all four limbs readable from the game's main view. Break symmetry unless it's deliberately comic. Twist the torso instead of bending it sideways; give torso, hips and legs different lines. Check poses on both sides of the screen and filled in solid black. Correct swing animations until the blade trail reads from the gameplay camera. *(eps 62;142;106;243;253;179;225;28)*
- **ANIM-09** [HIGH] Specify each animation's frames and key poses before animators start: Keep an animation list: every animation described in text with its length and when the attack occurs; mark loops like idle and walk 'Loop' or 'Any'. For each attack specify the standby pose, the attack pose, the attack start frame and the total frames until it can be canceled. Add photographed figure poses for key poses and source-game screenshots where relevant. Treat the spec as a first step; animators fill the gaps. *(eps 14;36;76;225)*
- **ANIM-10** [HIGH] Judge animation in motion, at gameplay size and camera: Review attacks at full speed, at real in-game size and from the gameplay camera. Turn down corrections for distortions that only show in freeze-frames if the move reads in motion. For detailed checks use slow motion, hidden backgrounds and dev-mode transparency for capes and clothing. *(eps 28;91;166;179;217;79)*
- **ANIM-11** [MEDIUM] Keep blends short; use them for cancels, segues and directional poses: When a cancel arrives, blend from the current pose into the new animation. Segue into damage poses with a quick blend (about four frames in Smash). Keep blend durations short and check them for distortion. Blend a fixed center stance with poses around a circle for directional moves. In full 3D, layer upper and lower body, place feet on terrain and let slopes affect turns. *(eps 194;47;142)*
- **ANIM-12** [MEDIUM] Squash and stretch while keeping volume constant: Add squash to landings, crouches and bounces and compare with and without. When squashing on one axis, widen the others. On 3D models, sculpt the squashed shape (an oval-track profile, a blend shape) instead of scaling one axis. Use shape animation for cartoon squeezes a skeleton can't do. *(eps 121;232)*
- **ANIM-13** [MEDIUM] Move bodies like bodies: two axes, linked joints, bent wrists: Layer a dip (X) or tilt (Z) onto every Y-axis turn of heads, wrists and torsos. Move the shoulder and collarbone with a twisting wrist. Bend wrists rather than keeping them in line with the arm. Pose anything swung with the forearm in a diagonal grip. Plan head turns with the eye line, and reduce the head turn once the eyes carry part of the look. *(eps 251;253;106;210)*
- **ANIM-14** [MEDIUM] Give mechanical motion personality: off-center pivots, wobble and recoil: Move the pivot of thrown or spinning objects and swung weapons slightly off the geometric center or grip. Layer a small looping offset onto straight-flying projectiles. Snap guns or cannons back on each shot. Hand-tune rotation timing (offset the two axes, add an occasional counter-motion) instead of trusting generated curves. Make quick mock-ups during review to show the alternative. *(eps 255;156;251;150)*
- **ANIM-15** [MEDIUM] Build rigs that allow exaggeration from the start: Decide early whether the style needs exaggeration. Rig shoulders to move forward and back and the upper and lower body to rotate independently. Budget extra joints or an extended skeleton for limbs that bend past straight, watch for IK flipping, and hire or brief animators with cartoon sensibilities. Where processing is tight, lock finger joints and swap preset finger patterns. *(eps 236;28;210)*
- **ANIM-16** [MEDIUM] Keep faces toward the viewer in side-view games: Mirror the animation set when a character changes facing instead of turning the model around. Angle runs and similar moves diagonally toward the camera. Plan for flipped handedness and quick item hand-swaps; skip mirroring for asymmetric characters such as sword fighters. Make emblems and lettering mirror-safe. Try placing the mouth on the side of the face in profile. *(eps 181;232)*
- **ANIM-17** [MEDIUM] Push facial expressions a step past done, shaped by anatomy: Once an expression feels done, make it one step bigger and judge it at in-game size. Show eye white above and below the iris on wide eyes, offset upper and lower teeth, and size mouth shapes to the jaw. Retouch key expressions before modeling. If the character only blinks in the source, carry emotion through body movement. *(eps 166;165;253;232)*
- **ANIM-18** [MEDIUM] Give movement weight, and give fast motions enough frames to register: Add frames to secondary motions that vanish before they register. For speeds a walk cycle can't support, use bounding or sliding locomotion. Shift weight forward in walks to improve weight and silhouette. Keep feet planted on landing. Add squash on landings. *(eps 84;165;253;121)*
- **ANIM-19** [MEDIUM] Honor the source's feeling, not its literal frames: Keep a source character's signature poses and quirks, but change what the new medium needs: a different first frame for momentum, a new follow-through path instead of reverse playback. Put source screenshots next to your version and compare feel, not just shapes. Keep source limitations (a face that only blinks) and compensate elsewhere. *(eps 150;165;166;28;243)*
- **ANIM-20** [MEDIUM] Check every variant: ground and air, every direction, both facings: Play ground and aerial versions of every special side by side, comparing poses and reach. Test attacks aimed in each direction. Review poses on both screen sides. Run multi-hit strings against every reaction stance. *(eps 253;62;142;181)*
- **ANIM-21** [MEDIUM] Find and communicate poses with a physical figure: Keep a posable figure at your desk and practice posing it, including zooming it around ('bundodo'). For specs, pose it, photograph it, cut out the silhouette and raise the contrast. In reviews, show a posed figure or a retouched frame rather than describing the change. *(eps 106;14;62;253)*

## Visuals, characters and effects (`--area visuals`)

- **VIS-01** [CRITICAL] Make everything players must read stand out from decoration: Highlight every collision surface in a debug view and check it reads at a glance in the normal view. Push non-colliding scenery back with aerial perspective and weaker contrast, then repair the overall look. Check that platforms, projectiles, costumes and fighters in shadow or vignetting stay visible, and that the floor doesn't overpower projectiles. Desaturate screenshots to see what actually stands out. Give dangerous attacks a visual tell players can react to. *(eps 30;165;253;214;157;152)*
- **VIS-02** [CRITICAL] Keep characters visible inside their own effects: Review each big effect with the character inside it at gameplay camera angles. Leave gaps around the body. Make the parts of a surrounding effect that sit between camera and character lower than the parts behind it. Add an outline to character models. Cut layered effects or background atmosphere that bury fighters, and make scattering elements like hit stars fly far enough not to hide behind bodies. *(eps 35;131;253;165)*
- **VIS-03** [CRITICAL] Never let color alone carry gameplay meaning: Run screenshots of every color-coded element through a color-blindness simulation filter (Photoshop includes one). Where hues merge, separate them by brightness, not just hue, without making them so dark they vanish into the background. Also check whole screens in black and white. Keep each screen to one dominant color theme rather than a mishmash. *(eps 214;133;223)*
- **VIS-04** [CRITICAL] Give every character a silhouette that reads even in solid black: Fill each character and key pose in solid black and compare the whole cast side by side. Exaggerate signature features (hair, ribbons, gloves, head size, limb thickness). Check key poses from the game's main viewpoint, with all four limbs readable and on both sides of the screen. Brighten extremities such as hands, feet or socks so movement reads. Sharpen shapes that have gone soft. *(eps 243;62;142;106;165;253;158)*
- **VIS-05** [HIGH] Give effects contrast with crisp shapes and dark accents, not glow alone: Build each effect around solid polygons or simple images as the focal point, with additive glow as support. Mix dark elements (black accents, a dark backdrop behind a hit mark) into bright effects. Test every effect on your brightest and darkest backgrounds. When processing is tight, mixing white and black pixels makes an effect show on any color. *(eps 16;131;253;56;244)*
- **VIS-06** [HIGH] Fit each hit mark to its hit: size, place, layers and duration: Scale size and flashiness by attack strength. Spawn the mark at the real contact point and check placement in game. Layer a main flash, a shockwave behind the target, an upward spray and material extras such as sparks for machines, without clutter. Keep durations short, shortest for rapid hits. Let the mark keep animating during hitstop while the characters freeze. Adapt any borrowed effect to your own camera distance and hit frequency. *(eps 131;244;253;179)*
- **VIS-07** [HIGH] Flash for impact and status, with internal contrast and fixed color meanings: Add shadows or dark elements into hit flashes so the character keeps internal contrast. Give each status one flash color or treatment and keep it consistent. Use rim lighting to signal states like invulnerability. Scale flash strength with camera distance so close-ups don't flash most of the screen. Keep flashes to a single brief moment and full-screen flashes mild. Check them on real LCDs, whose response time changes how they look. *(eps 223;3;56)*
- **VIS-08** [HIGH] Treat effects as essential and study them frame by frame: Add debug slow motion, a background-off toggle and on/off toggles for each display element. Record your effects and reference games' effects and step through them to see shapes, timing and layering. A/B a key scene with effects off. Give swings, punches and kicks trails or blur. Add celebratory flourishes aimed at the player who scored. *(eps 79;179;95;217)*
- **VIS-09** [HIGH] Let each character's look express its essence and its mechanics: Write a short core-essence statement per character and share it with everyone on that character. Make effects and trails carry the move's character: an emphasized edge where a sword's tip is stronger, a frenzied trail for a heavy blade, no fade-out for a blade that emits light. Show state changes on the character's body. Let poses express personality. Where a system has hidden rules, consider an effect that shows them. *(eps 243;206;179;131;115;20;223)*
- **VIS-10** [HIGH] Prefer expressive exaggeration over literal realism: Exaggerate signature features and expressions past where they feel done, judged at in-game size. Try manga conventions such as a mouth on the side of the face in profile, animated by swapping cels. Use shape animation for squash and stretch through gaps. Review models at the angles players see, and question default anatomy that reads less expressively. *(eps 232;91;166;243;28)*
- **VIS-11** [HIGH] Size characters and targets for the screen they're played on: Decide how much of the screen gameplay and targets should occupy before fixing camera and field of view. Scale enemies up if they look tiny. Make bosses in beginner games big enough that slightly off-target attacks still hit. When characters are small on screen, push their exaggeration and carry their appeal elsewhere, such as HUD portraits. *(eps 42;125;100;254;91)*
- **VIS-12** [HIGH] Unify mixed art styles around one visual concept: Before production, have your strongest artist make a few image boards (world, scenery, characters, items) and build every model and the lighting from them. When mixing styles, meet in the middle: slightly desaturate pure primaries and make them respond to light, add material texture to flat cartoon characters and ease texture on realistic ones, tweak proportions where matchups look silly, give everyone the same refinement pass, and ground stylized features in how they'd work if the character really existed. Apply the same check to animation, effects and audio. Vet a new technique before adopting it mid-project. *(eps 130;138;100;20)*
- **VIS-13** [HIGH] Draw the light an asset reflects, not just the asset: Make lighting part of an asset's definition of done. Light groups of repeated assets with colored light, brighten the spots that would catch more light, vary hue slightly across copies, and add atmosphere that does more than flat fog. Enrich flat backgrounds with contrast and hue-shifted light. Let stylized colors respond to scene lighting. Check that character lighting on each stage isn't too dark. *(eps 10;90;138;253;215)*
- **VIS-14** [HIGH] Judge visuals on the final composited frame at gameplay distance: Approve art in the game with all post-processing on, not in a model viewer. Review scale and lighting on full screenshots. Check assets both up close and from the gameplay camera. Line up mixed characters in game rather than against their source art. Step back from details regularly. On high-end projects, give one person responsibility for post-processing and final output. *(eps 215;174;224;138;205;63)*
- **VIS-15** [HIGH] Spend detail where players actually look: Rank assets by how much they're seen: the area in front of the camera and the stage surface first, off-screen foundations last. Budget each model by its on-screen size, raised for models also shown up close elsewhere. Put environment detail where cautious play makes players look, and where the camera actually reveals background. Build LOD only when the gain is worth the labor. Build stage art for the worst-case load. *(eps 44;152;165;99)*
- **VIS-16** [HIGH] Raise graphics and gameplay together, because graphics multiply fun: Build environment art alongside the mechanics and match its density to how closely play makes players look. Treat storefront banners and key art as critical. When readability and beauty conflict, keep both and give gameplay the slight edge. *(eps 152;30;99)*
- **VIS-17** [HIGH] Direct art by showing, and approve whatever meets the goal: Send a quick paintover, retouched screenshot, mouse sketch, posed figure or density reference beside the original, with one line on what to change and why. Make notes concrete: which axis, which spacing, which repetition. Flag tiny flaws the moment you see them. State each asset's core goal in one sentence and approve anything that meets it. If several rounds don't converge, ship it and move on. Keep physical models of the characters for artists. *(eps 90;165;224;253;201;223;216;106;255)*
- **VIS-18** [MEDIUM] Stage explosions as flash, blast and smoke, then let them finish: Open with an instant flash whose light rays reach well past the blast radius, mixed with black elements, plus a shockwave and a flare. Follow with a crisp roar of flames from the center with sparks. Keep the hitbox active for only part of the blast. Let black smoke take over as the flames wane, and end with flickering sparkles. Add a screen flash or shake only where it suits that explosion. Make ground variants whose blast spreads upward and whose shockwave follows the floor. *(eps 56;156;253;205)*
- **VIS-19** [MEDIUM] Sell scale through materials, saturation, atmosphere and density, not shape: Fix materials before shape: enlarge texture loops and painted detail, lower saturation on realistic assets, shrink gloss area and strength, match normal-map detail to real size, add dirt and mixed hues to big mechanical or monotone surfaces. Lower saturation and add haze with distance, so the nearest objects are the most saturated. Avoid depth-of-field blur. Give large effects more loops or smaller particles. Use placement and surroundings to establish size. *(eps 63;174;201;205)*
- **VIS-20** [MEDIUM] Honor the source's feeling instead of copying it literally: Get original production material and put source screenshots next to your version. Compare palette, saturation, relative brightness of costume parts, eye shape and signature poses. Combine the classic design with selected newer details. Stay close where the source shows a lot and invent where it shows little. Don't invent facial range the source lacks; carry emotion through movement instead. Get the rights holder's permission for changes. *(eps 165;243;253;166;168;188;138)*
- **VIS-21** [MEDIUM] Protect players' eyes: cap glare, flashing, blur and depth of field: Raise contrast on glowing areas like magma but cap their brightness. Keep full-screen flashes brief and mild, scaled down when the camera is close. Use pre-drawn blur textures on spinning parts, keep artificial blur subtle and offer a strength option. Remove depth of field and focus pulls except where truly needed. Offer toggles for camera embellishments such as footstep shake. *(eps 90;223;192;174;129)*
- **VIS-22** [MEDIUM] Check color on matched screens and on the displays players use: Set up one shared reference screen everyone can see and calibrate monitors by eye against it. Retire monitors that crush shadows or flatten contrast. Light art areas neutrally. Check flashes and dark scenes on real LCD TVs. When porting or imitating retro art, judge it as it looked on a CRT. *(eps 184;252;223)*
- **VIS-23** [MEDIUM] Make imaginary objects feel real through weight, light, use, placement and air: For each prop decide texture, light response, weight, use and placement, not just shape. Add environmental wind so cloth and foliage flutter, and small gusts around explosions. Make broken things irregular (no perpendicular cuts, uneven spacing, no copy-pasted fragments) and keep debris out of the space characters move through. Do a top-down plausibility sweep: windmills turning with the wind, debris spread naturally, light shafts through broken ceilings, motion that isn't excessive. *(eps 205;165;224;178)*
- **VIS-24** [MEDIUM] Give each weapon its own trail, and fix the swing until it reads: Build trails as a polygon ribbon swept behind the blade like a folding fan, with a translucent, fading tail. For swings that finish in one frame, show a single flat trail image and let hitstop hold it on screen. Check every swing from the gameplay camera and adjust the animation rather than using plain horizontal slashes. Design each trail for its weapon. Extend a similar blur to punches and kicks. *(eps 179;165;192)*
- **VIS-25** [MEDIUM] Get the most out of every particle: Keep particle lifetimes short and compensate with higher speed. Pack several particles into one billboard and draw billboards somewhat large. Stagger spawn timing across emitters and offset positions so particles don't overlap. Build flashes and particles from camera-facing billboards with sparks on top. Orbit the camera around billboarded objects and switch to models where they look odd. Use Y-axis billboards carefully. When processing is scarce, use hand-placed animation frames or random picks from preset patterns. *(eps 244;153;201;10)*
- **VIS-26** [LOW] On small, low-color canvases, give every pixel and color a job: Draw round or symmetric sprites at odd dimensions. See how far an outline plus one highlight carries a material before adding colors. Spend extra colors on anti-aliasing against the real background or on reflections. Mix light and dark pixels so small effects show on any background. Design emblems and shared parts that survive mirroring. When imitating retro art, remember its dithering was made for CRTs. *(eps 115;244;181;100;183;252)*

## Audio (`--area audio`)

- **AUD-01** [CRITICAL] Mix every sound by how relevant it is to the player: List every sound and rank it by relevance, then set volumes in that order. Smash's order, loudest first: KO; hits given or taken, and perfect shields; special-move activations and voices, with explosions between hits and specials and louder the bigger they are; dodges, shield blocks and ledge grabs; attack swings without impact and dashes; landings; jumps; walking; ambience lowest of all. Within a tier, scale by strength. Balance voices carefully, because clear speech cuts through everything. When voices or channels are limited, decide in advance which part yields. *(eps 32;204;107;239)*
- **AUD-02** [CRITICAL] Make impact sounds hit instantly and clear out fast: Shape hit sounds with a short attack that reaches a strong level almost immediately (not literally starting at peak volume). Trim reverb tails. Cut rapid jab sounds to the shortest length that still reads as consecutive hits. Time heavy hits so the main body of the sound plays during hitstop and is over when action resumes. Review in the busiest mix, not one sound at a time. Make sure the punch survives speakers that drop bass, and that the playback chain isn't delaying it. *(eps 167;8;32;54)*
- **AUD-03** [HIGH] Treat audio as essential, never as bolt-on polish: Schedule and budget music and sound alongside graphics from the start. List audio changes for every character or major asset at kickoff, as you do for art and moves. Construct every sound deliberately, even with limited technology. Use what new hardware makes possible to do something the old one couldn't. Tie presentation to music, for example by starting play as a stage's musical intro ends. Benchmark against the sounds you remember from games you loved. *(eps 257;4;40;96;160)*
- **AUD-04** [HIGH] Answer every input and every feat with sound: Give every accepted press a clear signal, sound included. Keep input-feedback sounds audible even at low mix priority. Add crowd reactions tied to skill: cheers for big launches and KOs, a subtle 'oh!' for near-misses like a barely caught ledge. Let spectators clap along to victory music. Consider vibration matched to the frequency of impact sounds. *(eps 104;32;95;55)*
- **AUD-05** [HIGH] Exaggerate action sounds; keep material and ambient sounds realistic: Decide each sound category's job: selling impact (exaggerate) or conveying material, weight and place (keep realistic). A/B real recorded hits against exaggerated ones. Give simple-graphics games distinct material sounds. Layer realistic ambience even under cartoon art. Set the overall exaggeration level against how realistic your visuals are, and unify audio across mixed sources the way you unify art. *(eps 89;204;178;138)*
- **AUD-06** [HIGH] Never leave dead silence; keep ambience quiet but alive: Mix ambience at the lowest priority. Stop in every area and just listen, and add layers wherever it's silent. Give realistic visuals a soundscape (wind in leaves, feet on dirt, clinking armor), each layer at an appropriate level. In simulations, call a few simple off-screen sounds to suggest activity. Check ambience on good speakers. Put extra effort in for first-person and over-the-shoulder games. Keep music from standing out too much over detailed visuals. *(eps 204;69;26)*
- **AUD-07** [HIGH] Finalize sound on cheap TVs and several setups, not studio speakers: Compare important sounds on headphones, PC speakers and an inexpensive TV before approving them. Watch sounds whose punch lives in the bass. Check good speakers for ambience quality. Keep the same reference gear all project. Don't tune so hard for weak speakers that you lose your creative vision. Check audio latency separately from video. Check every audio feature you rely on exists on each hardware variant you ship. *(eps 54;204;8;239)*
- **AUD-08** [HIGH] Never make sound the only carrier of critical information: Give every audio warning a visual tell players can react to, and treat sound as a reinforcing layer for critical events. Where it helps, offer audio and vibration cues as accessibility options, such as a sound near a spot you can jump to or an item you can pick up, and combat cues. *(eps 157;257;54;55)*
- **AUD-09** [HIGH] Build the soundtrack on a few memorable themes: Write one main theme early and plan where its arrangements appear across the game. Cut or merge tracks that have no melody players could hum. When arranging, keep the main melody intact so that humming the arrangement is humming the original. *(eps 69;127)*
- **AUD-10** [HIGH] Match music tempo and mood to the action and the player's feeling: Count BPM by clapping along (one clap per second is 60 BPM, two is 120) and note a target per scene. For fast battles, start from roughly 130 to 170 BPM, with near-200 tracks as rare exceptions. Set tempo by your game's actual speed, not its genre. Write one line per scene on what the player should feel and check the music supports it. Consider tying tempo to game state if you can afford it. *(eps 25;69)*
- **AUD-11** [HIGH] Brief sound work with concrete roles, shared references and examples: Decide what feedback and feeling each sound must give before requesting it. Describe its role concretely ('strong attack'). Compare only to universally known sounds ('a jet engine') and link an example for anything specific. Use onomatopoeia only as an extra clue next to a concrete description. Give one reference track with what to keep and what not to copy. Route musical feedback through a sound director. Agree on terms like 'main melody' by playing the reference together. *(eps 185;222;127;146)*
- **AUD-12** [HIGH] Approve the sound that serves the game, not the one in your head: Judge drafts by whether they fit the game and the scene, not by how closely they match your idea or the reference. List two or three other directions before locking a reference. Balance multi-environment fixes against your creative vision. If an existing song fits the theme exactly, consider licensing it as-is. *(eps 185;222;167;54)*
- **AUD-13** [MEDIUM] Shape music structure around gameplay timing: Brief composers with gameplay-driven rules: an intro that covers any start countdown (or music that starts after it), big dynamic shifts for a dynamic game, a build to a climax at the end, a length that fits the typical match with only a little loop, and a loop point before or after the intro. Use fixed-length segments to sync music to beats. Plan cue points where music waits for or follows variable-length events. Start play as a stage's musical intro ends. *(eps 127;125;227;4)*
- **AUD-14** [MEDIUM] Ground characters with audible, varied, material-aware footsteps and landings: Make footsteps clearly audible. Give each character unique footsteps. Match landing and walking sounds to footwear or costume and to the floor material. Record several variations of repeated sounds and rotate them. Make landings louder than jumps. *(eps 32;89;69)*
- **AUD-15** [MEDIUM] Script every vocalization; keep battle voices short, sparse and non-competing: List every grunt and line (attacks, jumps, damage reactions, special and super moves, reveal lines). Bring reference audio from the original game and prefer the original or current series actor. Keep combat lines short and infrequent, and check how loud or feral voices overlap. Brief and warm up actors before they enter the booth. Record banter-heavy casts together when schedules allow. Budget heavy editing time after recording. Where you can, deliver dialogue as voice during play so players needn't stop. *(eps 107;32;211;261)*
- **AUD-16** [MEDIUM] When arranging beloved music, treat the original as players heard it as right: Keep the main melody intact. Decide which melody is 'main' by what players heard and remember from play. Preserve odd sections. Mirror structure the hardware forced, such as an instrumental where the original lost its vocals. Keep separate language versions distinct. Recreate how a sound came out of the original cabinet, not just the raw file. Review for mistranscriptions of the original score. *(eps 146;127;54)*
- **AUD-17** [LOW] When voices or channels are scarce, plan what yields and fit timbre to role: Decide in advance which music part drops when a sound effect needs a voice. Give crisp tones to melodies, mellow tones to bass and noise-like sounds to hits and explosions. Test compression on big audio sets early, before cutting content. Check every hardware variant or region supports the audio features you rely on, or provide a fallback. *(eps 239;160;96)*
- **AUD-18** [LOW] Let players reach and choose the music they love: Where your game celebrates many sources, offer several tracks per world and let players choose. Don't lock music many players want behind progression. Give options such as menu-music frequency. *(eps 108;151)*
- **AUD-19** [LOW] Showcase game music live with the performance at the center: Gather far more candidate songs than you have slots and narrow them down by listening together. Use game footage only occasionally. Size each edition so it fits beside development work. If gameplay joins the performance, keep real stakes and plan cue points for variable timing. *(eps 116;227;40)*

## UI and clarity (`--area ui`)

- **UI-01** [CRITICAL] Let players skip and pause anything they are not controlling: Let one press skip a whole non-interactive sequence (company logos, opening movie, intermissions, cutscenes, credits), including on a brand-new save. Show a skip prompt the moment any button is pressed during a cutscene. Let players pause in the middle of any cutscene. If you write company or platform logo guidelines, make skippable the default. The only accepted exception for pausing is an online game that keeps advancing. *(eps 180;233;104;73;250)*
- **UI-02** [CRITICAL] Let players read and advance text at their own speed: Make one press finish the current text box instantly and the next press advance it. In voiced cutscenes add a skip that jumps to the start of the next line as well as a skip for the whole scene, or a fast-forward. Where possible, deliver dialogue as voiced lines during gameplay so players never stop to wait. Playtest text pacing with fast readers. *(eps 211;233;180;125)*
- **UI-03** [CRITICAL] Ship in-game button remapping in every game, however simple: Build a key config screen that draws the controller graphically. Let players select a button with the cursor, then pick its action from a list. Allow several buttons to share one action, and let each be reassigned separately. Save layouts under player profile names chosen before a match, stored on the controller where hardware allows. Where actions can go on the directional buttons, confirm a remap with a long press so short presses still navigate. Show each action's default button, split maps by context (aiming, swimming, menus), and make sure no essential action can end up unbound. Keep genre-standard defaults. *(eps 197;55;175;156;85)*
- **UI-04** [CRITICAL] Answer every button press, and accept confirm on several buttons: In menus, accept confirm from the main button plus another such as a shoulder button. In games with one or two actions, put each action on several buttons instead of leaving most dead. During cutscenes, respond to any press with a skip prompt. Give every accepted press a clear sound, highlight or animation. For each state (menus, gameplay, cutscenes), list which buttons do nothing and give them a sensible reaction unless silence is deliberate. *(eps 104;197)*
- **UI-05** [CRITICAL] Make the game playable without reading a single word: Put a picture or character on every major menu icon. Give each section a color. Set the default cursor on every screen from the title onward so pressing confirm repeatedly starts the main mode, tutorial included if there is one. Switch the game to a language you cannot read and try to navigate it; fix any screen where you get lost. *(eps 240;133;147)*
- **UI-06** [CRITICAL] Size and lay out text for every language and the real screen: Leave room in every text area for each target language's glyph size and string length. Read every screen, in every language, on the actual target display, including the handheld itself. Mock up labels in your longest language before locking a layout. Keep the interface clean so text has room to grow, and offer a font-size setting where space allows, along with subtitle size options. *(eps 33;147;55;111)*
- **UI-07** [HIGH] Write help text and names in plain words, never the item's name: Show a one-line description of the highlighted item, for example at the bottom of the screen. Rewrite it without the item's own words: Options becomes Adjust a variety of game settings. Cut excess words, translate meaning rather than words, and reread as someone who does not know the term. Name modes by what the player does (Play Solo, not 1P Mode), drop jargon like config or key repeat, and name moves in input order (Up Special), even if internal labels differ. *(eps 229;22)*
- **UI-08** [HIGH] Offer comfort and accessibility options, grouped, previewed and safe: Add a dedicated Accessibility menu that also links relevant options from other menus, with presets that switch groups on at once. Put difficulty-lowering options behind an extra unlock step. Include subtitle size, background, speaker names with colors and speaker direction; a screen reader for all text; colorblind and high-contrast modes; a magnifier; footstep head-bob off, locked vehicle frames and a center dot for motion sickness; a blur-strength slider; flick sensitivity; a cutscene-vibration toggle; and difficulty changes at any time without restarting. *(eps 55;129;192;154;213;214)*
- **UI-09** [HIGH] Make choosing fast by cutting steps to the most common choices: Count the inputs to the most frequent choices and reduce them. Support D-pad navigation with crisp one-step movement and a distinct, clicky feel per step. Add shortcuts as optional accelerators layered over a simple default path. When a platform adds a wheel, touch or gyro, prototype using it to jump through lists, but adopt only uses that fit. In equipment screens, offer a one-press equip-strongest. *(eps 169;147;182;240;154)*
- **UI-10** [HIGH] Size menu elements by how often players use them: Rank options by expected use; make the top ones the largest tiles or icons and shrink rarely used ones. Make a game's dominant action the most prominent. Keep a plain, equal list when options really are equal. Mock icon labels in the longest target language early. *(eps 147;18;44)*
- **UI-11** [HIGH] Settle clarity versus style per game, and organize menus like drawers: Decide early where the UI sits between clarity and style from the game's content and setting. Group related items and plan a window hierarchy proportional to the amount of content. Review each menu for visual guidance, element size, window hierarchy, brightness and saturation, and typeface. Then improve usability wherever it does not break the chosen style. *(eps 18;111;133;254)*
- **UI-12** [HIGH] Keep the HUD informative without crowding the play area: Let tracking elements hide themselves based on distance and situation. Turn HUD panels semi-transparent when a character moves behind them. Keep essential information out of edges a display may crop. Offer HUD size with a preview, and HUD hiding for players who want immersion. Make HUD space earn its place. *(eps 254;252;11;55)*
- **UI-13** [HIGH] Put navigation information where the player is already looking: In playtests, note where eyes go; if they mostly watch the mini-map, move the needed information into the main view. Try a translucent full-screen map players can keep up while moving, with one button for a map-only view. Add in-world guidance such as corner-sharpness markers, shape terrain to lead players, design how a 3D map shows height, and offer optional aids like destination arrows, ledge vibration and audio cues near jump or pickup spots. *(eps 119;55;42)*
- **UI-14** [HIGH] Show loading honestly and in your world's style, never timed to dev builds: Build one minimal, animated indicator styled after your world that can appear anywhere for any duration, and show it immediately when a load cannot be disguised. Use load-masking transitions only if they still work when final loads are shorter, and never put an icon on top of one. Time every fade and black screen and cut each to the fastest version that still does not feel abrupt; keep a short transition rather than none. *(eps 78;246;73;117)*
- **UI-15** [HIGH] Never let color alone carry information players need: Run every color-coded element through a color-blindness filter (Photoshop includes one). Where hues merge, separate them by brightness, without making them so dark they disappear into the background. Back color up with shape, icon or position, and offer colorblind or high-contrast modes. *(eps 214;55;133)*
- **UI-17** [HIGH] Treat fonts, frames and icons as worldbuilding, but keep them legible: Mock up one representative screen in several styles (slick, cute, horror, flashy, digital, regional or historical) and pick the one that fits the world and audience. Budget time for a custom font if nothing fits. Theme backgrounds, frames, icons, loading indicators and wipes, not just the typeface. Check every themed font and frame for legibility at real screen size before locking it. *(eps 111;78;18;246;133)*
- **UI-18** [HIGH] Show what an option will do before the player commits: Draw the effect on screen, such as a weapon's attack range, instead of only a stat. Show pros and cons side by side. Give visual settings a live preview, show a dark and a bright reference scene on brightness or HDR screens, and let players change them mid-game to compare. Let players test a build immediately, ideally swapping on the fly. Put a short looping clip beside each new skill's description. *(eps 182;55;85)*
- **UI-20** [HIGH] Keep the next goal and the distance to it on screen: Display current versus required values for the next reward on the main screen and update them on every gain. Consider a checklist where clearing an objective reveals the neighbors' conditions. At several points in a playthrough, ask what the player is working toward right now; if it is unclear, show it. *(eps 23;59;200)*
- **UI-16** [MEDIUM] Color-code menu sections, one dominant color per screen, fit to tone: Give each top-level menu its own color and theme all its submenus in it. Put large images on panels so players learn options by picture. Keep one dominant color theme per screen; large character art is the exception, since the images become memorable. Set saturation to fit the game's tone and audience. *(eps 133;18;240)*
- **UI-19** [MEDIUM] Make large inventories quick to sort, tell apart and equip: Add sorting and categories, make every weapon, part and icon clearly distinct by sight, and include a one-press equip-strongest option, which he calls essential. In libraries or launchers, let players make folders. *(eps 182;18;58)*
- **UI-21** [MEDIUM] Make HUD elements react to play and carry character appeal: Put character portraits in the HUD posed toward the player; animate their backgrounds, for example with upward UV scrolling. Swap meters per ability or mode and visibly break them when a vehicle or character is destroyed. Show icons for hard-to-track facts such as opponents hit or defeated. Animate score gains as a running tally. *(eps 254;200;11)*
- **UI-22** [MEDIUM] Present scores and ranks so improvement feels meaningful to most players: Choose a unit that fits the fantasy and the direction that suits it (lower is better for an age). Show standing as a number that rises as players improve, measured against the whole player population. Do not show general players the world number one. Consider an elite tier that rewards top players and keeps them matching each other. *(eps 200;170)*
- **UI-23** [MEDIUM] Use text effects for emotion, and let translators restyle them: Support inline markup for size, color, animation and font on individual words. Store the tags in the localizable script so translators can add, move or remove emphasis without a programmer. Offer several speech-bubble styles and a few clear emotion symbols. *(eps 93)*

## Directing and teams (`--area team`)

- **TEAM-01** [CRITICAL] Give one director the final say; never settle design by majority vote: Name one person who holds the whole-game vision and makes the content calls. If you split the role (co-leads on a jam team, say), write down exactly who decides what. When the team is split, the director decides and explains the reasoning. Polls are fine as input or out of curiosity, but the decision and any later adjustments stay with the director. If the person with the vision believes in an unpopular idea, build it and judge it in the game. *(eps 158;234;50;191;70;228)*
- **TEAM-02** [CRITICAL] Hold the concept steady and steer with small, frequent corrections: Before production, write the concept as one sentence of appeal (his examples: the fun of sending opponents flying, the thrill of sprinting down a straight path) and don't start full production until it's that clear. When fatigue or doubt sets in, check decisions against the concept instead of changing course. Correct individual calls as soon as you see they're wrong, amplify emergent fun that still heads toward the concept, and before scrapping a part, count the months of work and the morale you'd lose. Keep restating the concept to the team. *(eps 187;191;29;60;109;224)*
- **TEAM-03** [CRITICAL] Raise problems the moment you see them, with facts and a fix: Make it a team rule with no exceptions: tell someone who can act, right away, with everything you know, and don't waste time arguing about how to bring it up. Bring the problem and at least one possible fix as a set. Flag being stuck well before the deadline with an honest status, and treat that report as a baton pass. If the team decides to keep things as they are, ask for their reasons and accept them. *(eps 29;60;258;191;158;249)*
- **TEAM-04** [CRITICAL] Decide on the spot instead of putting decisions off until later: Make sure someone with the authority to decide is in each decision meeting, and push them to decide before it ends. If you're the decider, make the call as soon as a teammate brings you the issue; if information is missing, research it right away. Put every option on the table, pick the one that works in the short and long term, and leave leeway to correct it. *(eps 191;29;60;177)*
- **TEAM-05** [HIGH] Show, don't tell: give visual feedback as quick retouches and sketches: For any visual note, spend 5-10 minutes on a rough paintover, a mouse sketch, a photo of a posed figure or a diagram, and send it side by side with the original plus one line on what to change. Label it as an example of the direction; the artist still does the release-quality work. Save retouches for what words can't carry, because nobody can retouch everything. *(eps 90;165;224;253;14;100)*
- **TEAM-06** [HIGH] Make every note concrete enough to act on without guessing: Name the exact change: the axis, the spacing, the repetition, the frame. For sound, state the role ("this is a strong attack"), compare only to sounds everyone knows ("like a jet engine") and attach a link to a real example. With a reference track, say which quality to keep and which signature element not to copy. When revisions keep contradicting your notes, play the reference together and check you mean the same thing by the key terms. If you lack a craft's vocabulary, route notes through someone who has it. Send notes as bullet points. *(eps 224;185;222;146;127;261;165)*
- **TEAM-07** [HIGH] Give feedback to everyone involved at once, in the open: When reviewing a feature, gather everyone who touches it (planner, programmers, modelers, animators, effects, sound, and their leads), show the build on a big screen, use a mic if the room needs it, take questions at the end, and let anyone sit in. Hold group reviews within each discipline. When a review needs changes, explain them to everyone involved, not only the person who asked. Keep these sessions speedy, or the extra meeting time stops paying off. *(eps 114;14;177;43)*
- **TEAM-08** [HIGH] Answer every review request the same day it arrives: Use one predictable channel (his is an email titled "Review Request", sent once the production work is finished), handle requests first come, first served, and reply the same day, ideally right after the review you're on. Set a delivery cutoff so reviews don't start late at night (his was 8 PM). Block a fixed daily period, such as mornings, with no reviews so your own work gets done. *(eps 177;261;253)*
- **TEAM-09** [HIGH] Write specs teammates can build from without guessing: For animation, give an animation list with each move's length and when the attack becomes active, posed-figure photos for key poses, and four values per attack: standby pose, attack pose, attack start frame, and total frames until it can be canceled. Write timing in frames, not "quick", and give every speed a unit. Put each tunable value in plain-language brackets ("[Special startup X-velocity is O]") so programmers know what to expose. Script every grunt before a voice session. Tell artists and animators each piece's gameplay role (which surfaces have collision, when a hit lands). Tidy your private outline before it goes on the shared server. *(eps 14;36;50;68;84;76;137;107;22;225;30)*
- **TEAM-10** [HIGH] Start specs from the player's ideal, then negotiate feasibility with programmers: Choose the one option you judge best and delete the Plan B from proposals. Flag every choice made for development convenience and keep it only if players won't suffer for it. Bring programmers in to balance the ideal against what's technically feasible, take the team's suggestions with an open mind, and fall back to an alternative only when real problems appear. *(eps 21;109;12;52)*
- **TEAM-11** [HIGH] Make sure everyone knows the goal and their piece's role in it: Share the project's objectives with every role, not only the leads, and mix professions in subteams with the director involved in each. Tell artists which elements have collision, tell animators when and where collision is active, and give everyone working on a character a short statement of its core essence. Keep a visible picture of everything still needed to ship. *(eps 50;187;243;30;225;110)*
- **TEAM-12** [HIGH] Show the whole team the whole game, every day: Post a daily report: one current screenshot at the top, then 3-5 topics (news, what you're working on, checklists, issues to fix, and sometimes a game you played or current events), with comments open. Put key announcements there instead of in mass email, and invite teammates to post what they finished. Every few months, or every period, have each discipline or subteam present its finished work to everyone, even though scheduling it is a hassle. *(eps 43;123;88;126;261;190)*
- **TEAM-13** [HIGH] Playtest with fresh outsiders and children, and watch instead of asking: With permission, invite staff members' children and their friends, make it feel like a fun session with snacks, stand back and note where they struggle or lose interest. Rotate in new testers who start from the very beginning, treating each one as a one-time first impression. Recruit testers prone to motion sickness to check camera comfort. Share the reactions with the team. *(eps 248;256;129;34;88)*
- **TEAM-14** [HIGH] Delegate execution to specialists and testers; keep the director supervising: Build tools and pipelines so a few planners can enter and tune data; staff dedicated play testers and trust their analysis; give specialties such as post-processing a clear owner. Let composers pick pieces they're attached to, and otherwise assign by strength. Keep the director playing the build and supervising instead of doing data entry. *(eps 135;220;46;88;127;215;90;245;261;50)*
- **TEAM-15** [HIGH] Step in personally when delegated core work stalls: Let leads own their work, but watch the core: if the central gameplay still isn't working after a long stretch, step back in, write fresh specs and rebuild the logic on the finished assets. When tuning stops giving the results you expect, have strong programmers hunt for code bugs, since no parameter can fix broken code. When someone reports they're stuck, take the baton and reassign, re-scope or change approach. *(eps 59;259;258)*
- **TEAM-16** [HIGH] As director, own the trimming when plans slip: When plans slip, anticipate the damage and offload the heavy or flammable cargo: drop or scale back the riskiest work and reset the course. Cut detail no player will see, but allow harmless extras that cost no schedule or performance. Set hard requirements early and pare content until they hold. Before the costliest late phase, check whether the project can actually be finished. Thank the team at the end, because finishing usually means painful cuts. *(eps 258;44;135;110;87)*
- **TEAM-17** [HIGH] Protect individuality: intervene when tuning sands distinctiveness down: Delegate balance analysis, but have the director step in mainly when a proposed change threatens a character's or the game's individuality. Tell the tuning team to keep characters unique by amplifying shortcomings to offset advantages. Listen to the team's concerns and adapt to them, but separate those from pressure that would only make the game more ordinary. *(eps 220;218;228;70;158)*
- **TEAM-18** [HIGH] Judge work by its purpose, not your mental image, and know when to stop: State each asset's goal in one sentence (for the dojo wall: it must look like wooden boards actually broke) and approve anything that meets it. Approve sounds that fit the game even when they differ from what you imagined. Flag flaws as soon as you see them, but if several rounds still don't reach the bar, put it in the game as-is and spend your time elsewhere. Aim for first-try approvals by being clear about what you want from the start. *(eps 224;185;222;90;14;253)*
- **TEAM-19** [HIGH] Lead by request and respect, never by rank: Phrase direction as requests to the people who'll do the work. Address everyone politely in name and tone, including brand-new and much younger hires. Treat disagreements as normal between allies. Raise issues as constructive feedback about the work, never as complaints about a person behind their back. Don't act subservient either, and leave every team on good terms. *(eps 260;249;162;158;253)*
- **TEAM-20** [MEDIUM] Meet trouble with calm analysis and shared responsibility, not blame: When something goes wrong, analyze the cause and propose a fix before reacting. In post-release reviews, look across the whole chain instead of naming a culprit. Treat friction between roles as a question of mutual support: what can you do so the other side can do their best work? *(eps 259;162;224;60)*
- **TEAM-21** [MEDIUM] Give everyone an open, answered channel for opinions and requests: Set up an opinion board where anyone can post about how development is going, and have the director reply with their thinking or forward items to the right team. Survey everyone soon after a big workflow change and fix the common problems first. Make time for one-on-ones. On the listening end, be flexible and decide quickly. *(eps 60;123;66;259;228)*
- **TEAM-22** [MEDIUM] Separate the director's content role from the producer's business role: Write down who owns content and concept and who owns budget, staffing and publicity. On a tiny team one person can wear both hats, but know which hat a decision belongs to. Build enough trust that differences of opinion don't turn into standoffs. If you go independent, estimate how much company management would eat your creative time before you hire. *(eps 234;258;53;245;162)*
- **TEAM-23** [MEDIUM] Budget for overhead: ten people produce about seven people's work: Plan at roughly 70% effective output from headcount, with more overhead as the team grows. Name who handles workload management, external communication and quality supervision. Backfill a promoted lead's production work. Set up shared tools and rules early. On a team of just a few people, expect closer to full output. *(eps 88;66;53;114)*
- **TEAM-24** [MEDIUM] Give every task and bug a ticket with one owner and a status: Split each content piece into tickets per discipline (spec, model and rig, animation, programming, effects, sound effects, balancing), each with one owner, a status and a deadline a lead reviews, and leave slack between dependent tasks. For bugs: test only on the latest daily build, file a ticket once the bug can be reproduced, grade severity (Class S for hangs and save corruption, Class A for progression blockers), route it to the discipline that owns the problem, have the tester verify the fix in several configurations, and mark it resolved instead of deleting it. *(eps 94;208;50;110)*
- **TEAM-25** [MEDIUM] Present ideas fast: core idea first, no justifications or alternatives: Open with the core idea and drop justifications, alternatives and Plan B. Use one image and about two lines of text per slide, group slides into categories so people know where they are, and talk around the slides instead of reading them. Explain even the basics simply. Describe what's new rather than "X, but with Y". *(eps 12;38;1;52;161)*
- **TEAM-26** [LOW] Build a warm team: thank, celebrate and play together: Turn giveaways into quick games (list items on the daily page, let people sign up, pick winners by rock-paper-scissors, a match in your own game or a secret rule). Keep snacks in the review room. Thank the staff explicitly at master-up. When forming a core team, favor people who love the game or genre. *(eps 190;87;248;43;114;132)*
- **TEAM-27** [LOW] Line up the next plan before the team disbands: During the tail end of a project, draft the follow-up proposal, including feature lists and per-character adjustments. Use any gap before a new team assembles to write design documents and win over key stakeholders. *(eps 160;132;261)*

## Work ethic and creativity (`--area craft`)

- **CRAFT-01** [CRITICAL] Make every call for the player, not for the developers' convenience: When a spec or compromise exists to make development easier, ask whether players would accept it; keep it only if it is a sensible efficiency trade they won't notice, otherwise look for alternatives together with the programmers. When you feel lost or unmotivated, ask how the decision in front of you entertains the player and let that decide. Remember that real people will buy what you make. *(eps 7;21;67)*
- **CRAFT-02** [HIGH] Treat each project as a professional assignment, not a hobby: At kickoff, write down the genre, goal and audience the project must serve and design to that brief. On tasks that aren't to your taste, find what makes them interesting and do them well anyway. Give a sequel the same full effort as an original, and practice working in styles outside your specialty so you can match a set style. *(eps 7;130;162;164;261)*
- **CRAFT-03** [HIGH] Refuse easy-way-out compromises: the hardship fades, the game endures: Before taking a shortcut, ask whether you'll regret it once the game ships. Set up an efficient workflow first, then put the effort you save into what players will actually experience. Decide which special touches will set the game apart and commit to them. *(eps 67;40;164)*
- **CRAFT-04** [HIGH] Start now: motivation arrives after your hands are moving: Take the first physical step at once: open the document and type a line, open the canvas and draw. Cut off social media, video sites and games before a work block. Psych yourself up out loud and start. Save planning tricks such as visualizing tasks for after you've begun. If you want to make games, sit down and make one with whatever tool you have. *(eps 83;212;118)*
- **CRAFT-05** [HIGH] Manufacture urgency, and favor speed over endlessly polishing drafts: Give each task a short self-imposed time limit, set well ahead of the real deadline, and frame milestones as something closing in on you. Once the outline and main points are itemized, write the whole draft in one push. Accept small imperfections in proposals, scripts and presentations and live with the results. *(eps 171;52;261;12)*
- **CRAFT-06** [HIGH] Take a lot in: play many current games to the end, reflectively: Play as many different current games as you can rather than one title at length, and try to reach each ending. While playing or watching, note what works and why. Choose the input form that suits you (film works too if you reflect on it). Before claiming old games were better, replay one next to a recent game. *(eps 126;45;220;198)*
- **CRAFT-07** [HIGH] Put a lot out: keep a small, steady, low-pressure output habit: Commit to a recurring output such as a devlog, column, daily team report or daily screenshot. Time-box each installment to a short fixed slot. Publish what you think is good instead of writing for an imagined audience. Before starting a side project, fix its total run and the size of each edition so it fits beside your main work. *(eps 126;98;123;116;83;261)*
- **CRAFT-08** [HIGH] Clear every review the same day, and protect a block for your own work: Take review requests through one channel with a fixed subject line and handle them first come, first served. Reply the same day, ideally as soon as the current review is done, and explain any changes to everyone involved. Set a delivery cutoff so reviews never start late at night, and reserve a fixed period (mornings, or after a cutoff) with no reviews at all for your own tasks. *(eps 177;261;29)*
- **CRAFT-09** [HIGH] Define the problem's theme, then list many ideas before pruning: Write the problem as a simple one-line theme. List broad solution types and note why each fails in this context. For every failure, look for a variant that fixes its flaw. Keep thinking actively instead of waiting for inspiration, and prune only once the list is long. *(eps 237;140)*
- **CRAFT-10** [HIGH] Ask why other games work, then rebuild instead of mimicking: Before borrowing a mechanic, write down why it works in its source and whether that reason applies to your game. Deconstruct the fun into its parts (usually risk and reward), analyze it, and rebuild it as your own mechanic. If you start from an homage, list many reasons a player would pick yours over the original, not just one. *(eps 9;57;196;72)*
- **CRAFT-11** [HIGH] Train a keen personal eye, and put your own take into the game: When reviewing your build or someone else's game, write down the small details most people would overlook and what you would change. For each major decision, state your personal take and how it differs from the genre's usual answer, and keep the difference on purpose. Practice explaining the idea until teammates can repeat it back. *(eps 70;253;228)*
- **CRAFT-12** [HIGH] Notice echo chambers and seek opinions from outside your circle: When an idea gets nothing but praise, ask people outside that circle what is wrong with it. Before acting on reviews or social reactions, check whether you are only seeing the voices that agree. Form your own verdict on a game before reading what others think. When online claims spread, check them against data and actual play. *(eps 228;218;247;199)*
- **CRAFT-13** [HIGH] Keep your deliberate direction; don't let outside voices sand it off: Sort feedback into team concerns you should adapt to and outside pressure that would only make the game more ordinary, and protect the deliberate quirks. Let the person who holds the big picture make creative calls instead of counting votes, and use polls only as input. Back the call with reasons and with everything the team knows. *(eps 228;158;247;218)*
- **CRAFT-14** [HIGH] Steer by your concept when fatigue and doubt set in: Write the concept as one clear statement before production and don't move forward until it's that clear. When doubting, check decisions against it, and amplify emergent fun only if it heads toward the concept. In proposals, commit to the ideal spec instead of hedging with alternatives, then course-correct with the team. *(eps 187;109;7)*
- **CRAFT-15** [HIGH] Treat a rejection as your job to show the appeal better: When a pitch is rejected, rework how it demonstrates the fun instead of blaming the reviewers. Describe the game on its own terms rather than by comparison. If press or partners dismiss the game, explain it to players yourself. When something underperforms, review the whole chain instead of hunting for one culprit. *(eps 52;143;245;161;162)*
- **CRAFT-16** [HIGH] Never fight or badmouth coworkers: the industry is small: Raise problems directly as constructive feedback, ideally with a proposed fix. If a situation doesn't suit you, step back instead of escalating. Finish or hand off your work before leaving. Never speak ill of anyone. Follow a partner company's rules. Meet trouble with calm analysis, and treat directions to others as requests, not orders. *(eps 249;162;260;259;60)*
- **CRAFT-17** [HIGH] Break in by finishing your own game and letting originality show: Start small in whatever tool you can get and finish it. Get people to play it (friends, free online release, or a sale if you're confident), find out what they think, then improve it or carry the lessons forward. Send an original game with your applications even if you don't meet every listed requirement, and enter open proposal calls. *(eps 34;118;196;149)*
- **CRAFT-18** [MEDIUM] Keep a new idea sealed until its first draft is written: Whenever you want to tell someone about the idea, write it into the document instead. Hold back work-in-progress posts while it takes shape. Consult colleagues for weak points and new ideas only once the draft is finished. Picture how people will react to keep yourself going. *(eps 144;52)*
- **CRAFT-19** [MEDIUM] Turn negative thoughts into questions about how to improve: Stop voicing or posting defeatist lines about your work. Rewrite each one as a question and act on the answer: 'my work feels pointless' becomes 'what are some ways I can make it better?' *(eps 212;83)*
- **CRAFT-20** [MEDIUM] Protect your body: exercise a little and fix your workstation: Do at least a little regular exercise, for example pedaling an exercise bike through play sessions. Keep about a meter between your eyes and the monitor (move it back if it is under 60 cm, and use a larger screen to compensate) and match brightness to the room. Make sure everyone has a proper desk and chair. Ramp up gradually and stop if something hurts. *(eps 24;81;66;261)*
- **CRAFT-21** [MEDIUM] Give one project all of you, and keep overhead off creative time: Before taking a demanding lead role, decline or finish competing commitments. Focus on one directing job at a time. Before going independent, estimate how much of your time company management would eat. Keep a daily block that belongs only to your own tasks. *(eps 132;53;177)*
- **CRAFT-22** [MEDIUM] Picture the real people who will play, and let that fuel you: While writing and developing, picture specific reactions to specific parts of the game. Mentally present your current build to customers and note what you would change. During hard stretches, share outside players' reactions with the team. *(eps 144;67;207;248;124)*
- **CRAFT-23** [MEDIUM] Respect the labor behind every game, and judge it by its own goals: Evaluate a game against what it set out to do for its intended players. Approach other developers' games with gratitude for the struggle behind them. Frame awards as celebration, not a race. Don't write off creators who disappear from view, and give yourself the same freedom to start something new. *(eps 13;87;198;176;31;110)*
- **CRAFT-24** [MEDIUM] Study your genre's roots, then carve a new path: Play landmark games of your genre and note which mechanic each introduced and what it cost. Then aim your own design at something those games didn't do instead of retracing them. *(eps 155;156;157;209;45)*
- **CRAFT-25** [MEDIUM] Don't let convenient tools or obvious answers fence in your ideas: Regularly ask which 'impossible' ideas are only the limits of a tool or convention. Before adopting the standard solution (a stock rig, a raw leaderboard, a template site), consider at least one alternative built for your game. *(eps 236;170;143)*
- **CRAFT-26** [MEDIUM] Use advice to sharpen your own judgment, not to replace it: Test each lesson against your own build, keep what fits, and keep making and playing games to build the judgment the advice depends on. *(eps 1;261)*

## Marketing and updates (`--area marketing`)

- **MKT-01** [CRITICAL] Treat making the game known as seriously as making it good: From early in development, plan what the dev team itself will contribute to promotion (reveal videos, an official site, a demo, presentations) and budget the time for it. Ask the publisher for the marketing and operations plan so you can suggest ideas while they can still be acted on. Think about how each piece of news will reach people scrolling social feeds. *(eps 80;245;122;234;31;152)*
- **MKT-02** [CRITICAL] Make every marketing image match how the game actually feels: Compare your title, cover, screenshots, trailer and demo with actual moment-to-moment play and fix anything that sets the wrong expectation. Surprises are fine as long as the overall picture matches. Go indirect or subversive only if it still captures the heart of the game. A tech demo is acceptable only if it truthfully shows how the game will run. *(eps 101;186;122)*
- **MKT-03** [CRITICAL] Get to real gameplay as fast as possible in every trailer: Treat a gameplay trailer as a required deliverable, including on your store page. Cut opening logos, long movies and character intros; lead with what makes the game appealing and save story and world for later. If you don't open on raw gameplay, open on footage that clearly illustrates the systems or an honest tech demo. Watch it as a stranger and trim whatever delays the game. *(eps 186;17;12)*
- **MKT-04** [CRITICAL] Ship in the best shape possible: patches can't win back players who left: Set the release quality bar as if no update will ever come. Lock content early enough to play a near-final build, and check difficulty on the actual retail hardware after late optimization. After launch, treat player videos and reports as your biggest test pass. *(eps 231;136;141;87)*
- **MKT-05** [HIGH] Choose your audience from a top-down market view, not your own taste: Map the current player segments and decide deliberately which one the game serves. Define the target by experience level or need rather than age or gender. For broad reach, use orthodox, broadly appealing design; for a niche, commit fully to that branch's values. Set the risk level of your rules to fit the audience you chose. *(eps 112;226;148;7)*
- **MKT-06** [HIGH] Don't let market research generate your concept: Form the concept first and use data to check its risks, not to pick it. When research says a genre is booming, ask how crowded it will be when you release and what you offer that the others don't. Keep graphs and research out of the pitch; spend that space on what is new. *(eps 226;52;158;72)*
- **MKT-07** [HIGH] Count every game, old or new, as competition for players' time: List what your target player could spend the same hours on (current releases, older games, subscription catalogs, phone and social media) and write down why they would pick yours. Measure against the best games in the genre. Offer something fresh that can't be found elsewhere, and lead with fun rather than an hour count. *(eps 72;198;250;31)*
- **MKT-08** [HIGH] When a trailer shot feels good enough, zoom in and go bolder: Run every trailer shot through five checks: zoom in and go bolder; tweak the angle of view to transform the layout; add interactions between characters; balance serious and comic beats; sync to the music. Remove depth-of-field focus shifts unless a shot truly needs one. Polish faces and highlights that will be seen up close. *(eps 163;174;165)*
- **MKT-09** [HIGH] Explain an unusual game's essence to players yourself: Write a plain explanation of how the game plays, its core essence and where its push and pull lies, and publish it directly to players as a guide if the press won't run it. Have the person who knows the game best write it or at least review it, and delegate production tasks like screenshots. *(eps 245;143;27;234)*
- **MKT-10** [HIGH] Post updates on a fixed, frequent schedule fans can make a habit: Release one new detail on a set cadence (Brawl's Dojo posted every weekday). Have the director write or review each post and the team help with production tasks. Review every official social and website update before it goes out. Shape news for people scrolling social feeds, and keep the announcements off your personal account. *(eps 245;80;123;143)*
- **MKT-11** [HIGH] Keep one official home base, shaped around your game's content: Maintain one canonical site alongside a blog and official social accounts. Build its sections from your actual content (characters, modes, techniques, online play, stages, music, events) and add features the game needs. Have someone who knows the design review the layout and every update. *(eps 143;80)*
- **MKT-12** [HIGH] Make storefront art strong enough to earn the click: Treat banner, capsule and cover art as critical assets, not afterthoughts, and test whether they make people want to click. Keep them honest to the game (see MKT-02). *(eps 152;101)*
- **MKT-13** [MEDIUM] Triage post-launch work: fatal bugs first, the rest is a bonus: Rank post-launch work by severity. Plan people and time for fixes that slip past master-up. Prefer narrow, contained fixes over changes to core systems, and re-run broad checks before releasing each patch. Use online and tournament data as reference for balance. Budget DLC as a real schedule extension. *(eps 231;141;136;135;160;220;87)*
- **MKT-14** [MEDIUM] Budget a demo as its own small game, and release it only when balanced: Decide early whether you'll make a demo and schedule it as its own deliverable with near-final QA. Keep spoiler content in separable files and check the demo build for leftovers. If the pre-release build isn't balanced, consider a post-launch demo made from the finished game and accept a smaller splash. *(eps 122;101)*
- **MKT-15** [MEDIUM] Build assets and tools so they double as marketing material: Produce key art and renders at a quality and format marketing can reuse. Make touched-up revisions of game models for pre-rendered CG and build trailer sound from the game's own sound effects. Give the team replay and debug-camera tools (pause, rotate characters, zoom, change angle) for capturing footage and screenshots. *(eps 80;163;195)*
- **MKT-16** [MEDIUM] Demo by playing: show every key feature in a close match: Before a demo, write a checklist of what the footage must show (for a fighter: every special move, the super move and as many regular moves as possible) and keep matches relatively close. Learn the content thoroughly, check the stage setup, and when playing live keep real stakes such as high difficulty and no restarts. *(eps 207;227)*
- **MKT-17** [MEDIUM] Present your game on its own terms, not as "X but with Y": Rewrite your pitch or announcement so it describes the core experience without naming another game, and check that people can still picture it. Sum the game up in one line naming its audience or its hook. Keep 'it's like X' for informal talk with people you know share the reference. *(eps 161;2;72)*
- **MKT-18** [MEDIUM] Make a sequel's reveal prove it beats its predecessor: Write in one line how the sequel is more fun and more impactful than the last game, and make the reveal show exactly that. If a returning player would think 'I've already played this', expand the system or the content before announcing. *(eps 164;160;40)*
- **MKT-19** [MEDIUM] Hear fans through deliberate channels, but keep the plan yours: Collect wishes with structured tools, such as a survey that discounts repeat votes, at the planning stage. Answer persistent requests with dedicated content. Decide in advance which parts of the plan are open to audience input and which are fixed, and read reactions with the echo-chamber caution in CRAFT-12. *(eps 160;39;245;261;228)*
- **MKT-20** [MEDIUM] Plan from the start to reach a global audience in their languages: If you want a global audience, plan localization of updates and videos from the start, line up regional partners, and write scripts or copy early so translators work from final text. *(eps 245;261)*
- **MKT-21** [MEDIUM] Don't lock expensive movies behind progress; they'll be shared anyway: Assume any in-game movie will be uploaded at or before launch. Turn showcase cinematics into marketing everyone can watch, and reward progression with things that keep their value after someone uploads them. *(eps 163)*
- **MKT-22** [LOW] Pick a title that shortens into something memorable: While naming, list the short forms players are likely to use and favor names that shorten well. Choose words for the tone they imply. *(eps 101;259)*

## Tech and tools (`--area tech`)

- **TECH-01** [CRITICAL] Keep every tunable number out of the code: Have programmers read every value that might need tuning (attack power, speeds, decelerations, timings) from a parameter table kept outside the code that planners can edit at any time. Build characters or vehicles as parameter sets on one shared behavior, with only a few true types in code. When a mode needs different physics, give it its own parameter set. When the game's tempo changes, retune every parameter instead of porting old values. *(eps 46;68;59;108;135;160)*
- **TECH-04** [CRITICAL] Keep game speed independent of frame rate and processing speed: Drive game logic from a clock that does not depend on how many frames are drawn or how fast the processor runs. When diagnosing performance, tell frame drops (less redrawing) from true slowdown (the game itself running slower). Find every timing that still depends on processing speed (enemy speeds, time limits, decay timers). Lock processing speed and frame timing before final balance, and redo tuning if they change. *(eps 5;141;74;92)*
- **TECH-05** [CRITICAL] Set the frame-rate target early and cut content until it holds: Choose the target at the start of the project, per game. Budget for the worst-case scene (the maximum number of characters and effects) rather than the average. When one platform is weaker, let it set the shared limits, and cut or simplify features rather than lower the target. Profile every stage or level at maximum load. *(eps 5;135;99;156;125)*
- **TECH-08** [CRITICAL] Verify the final build on real retail hardware before sign-off: Before sign-off, play the finished game on production units, not just dev kits. Compare timer-driven systems (hunger, fatigue, growth), time limits and enemy speeds against design values. Schedule a final difficulty pass after late optimization lands. When several team members independently report the same feel problem, treat it as real even without a cause. *(eps 141;92;74)*
- **TECH-09** [CRITICAL] Remove input and display lag from every test, demo and capture setup: Before any demo, playtest, event or capture, press jump in an instantly responsive 2D game on that exact setup and confirm the character moves the moment you press. Switch TVs to Game Mode or Low Latency Mode, or turn off every picture-enhancing setting. Check audio separately and try an analog connection or low-latency audio. Convert listed display times to frames. Check yourself instead of trusting that someone else did, and rule out display lag before retuning feel. *(eps 8;156;252;19)*
- **TECH-14** [CRITICAL] Aim testing at the places where systems fight over control: Estimate the test matrix early so you know where to prioritize. Put the first and heaviest passes on grabs, throws and restraints, especially on a target mid special move; on scene-changing supers (restrain, release, nothing leaks between scenes); on extreme and V-shaped terrain; and on every parameter, growth or unlock system. List the conditions each AI behavior mode responds to and test cases where two modes disagree. Let machines help: run CPU-versus-CPU battles overnight, and near the end pass save data between regions for worldwide testing. *(eps 136;208;221;231)*
- **TECH-17** [CRITICAL] Ship as if no patch will come; treat updates as a bonus: Set release quality as though no update will ever ship. Lock content early enough to spend time on near-final builds. After launch, fix crashing and fatal bugs as a must, and weigh balance and quality-of-life updates as optional work against freeing the director and key staff for the next game. Watch player videos and reports as your biggest test pass. Budget the schedule for updates and DLC, which stretch it. *(eps 231;136;135;141)*
- **TECH-02** [HIGH] Make the edit-and-test tuning loop a single button press: Build a conversion tool, for example a spreadsheet add-in, that pushes edited parameters to every active dev kit or test build in one press, without a reboot. Give planners and other team members access so a small tuning team can work while the director reviews. Back them with play testers who keep playing the current build. *(eps 46;135;220)*
- **TECH-03** [HIGH] Make parameters unmistakable with plain-language names, units and formulas: In specs, write each tunable as a bracketed plain-language phrase with a placeholder value, such as [Special startup X-velocity is O] and [Special X-deceleration is O/frame], under the rule that anything in brackets becomes a parameter, and keep a list pairing each phrase with its value. State a unit for every speed (pixels per frame for sprites, km/h or m/frame for real-scale 3D) and every time (frames at your target rate). Hide rarely adjusted fields in the editor, and link dependent values with formulas, such as one move at 1/2 another's power. *(eps 68;46;84;76;5)*
- **TECH-06** [HIGH] Spend processing where players look, and cut what nobody sees: Rank assets by how much they are actually seen and spend polygons and texture detail in that order: in front of the camera and on playable surfaces first. Set model budgets by on-screen size, raised for models also shown up close elsewhere. Build LOD only when the gain is worth the labor. Prefer cheap light and color work in the asset over heavy per-object shaders. Lock finger joints and swap a few pre-made poses. Use billboards for flashes, particles and simple objects. Allow playful details only when they meet deadlines and cost no performance. *(eps 44;10;210;244;153;40)*
- **TECH-10** [HIGH] Calibrate for every controller, platform and regional hardware variant: Set and test the stick dead zone per platform or controller. Expose sensitivity for gesture inputs like flicks. Before promising support for an input device, check whether your mechanics need analog values. List each target's audio, input and button conventions and provide fallbacks. Recheck raw pixel speeds on every resolution. Account for peripheral costs: a twin-stick add-on for 3DS added about 5% processor load, too much for a game already at the limit. *(eps 19;154;175;239;197;84;125)*
- **TECH-11** [HIGH] Build debug commands for the test setups you use most: Add direct commands for frequent setups, such as setting every character to a chosen damage percentage. Put debug functions on button combinations of the normal gameplay controller. Include capture tools (pause, rotate characters, zoom, change camera angle). Regularly reorganize and prune the debug menu, and strip it from the release build. *(eps 195)*
- **TECH-12** [HIGH] Add view toggles that isolate exactly what you are checking: Add a dev-mode option to make any selected model part, or the whole model, translucent or hidden. Add an on/off toggle for every display element. Add a view that highlights every collision surface (his example marks them in red). Add slow-motion and background-off toggles for effect review. Make these views available to reviewers outside the team. *(eps 217;30;79)*
- **TECH-15** [HIGH] Run bugs through tickets with repro steps, severity and the right owner: Require everyone to test on the latest daily build. Have testers work from design documents and checklists in subteams by game area. File a ticket only once the bug can be reproduced. A manager assigns a class (S for hangs or possible save corruption, A for progression blockers, lower classes sometimes waived on purpose) and routes it to the discipline that owns it. After a fix the tester confirms it in a variety of configurations, then marks it resolved rather than deleting it. Cross-reference or merge related tickets and rebalance overloaded people. Track all other work as tickets with one owner, a status and a deadline too. *(eps 208;94;50)*
- **TECH-16** [HIGH] Regression-test every fix, and keep late fixes narrow: Pair each fix with a regression pass over related systems before releasing a patch, and verify it in several configurations. For late or post-launch problems, prefer contained changes, such as lowering specific encounters or adding an easier option, over rewriting core AI or global difficulty code. *(eps 136;141;208;231)*
- **TECH-18** [HIGH] Fix the code before you tune the numbers: When tuning stops responding the way you expect, stop adjusting and have strong programmers hunt the underlying bugs. Play each move against its spec; when it feels off, inspect the code and data before touching the numbers. *(eps 259;76)*
- **TECH-19** [HIGH] Name files so they sort sensibly and read at a glance: Define the scheme at project start: broad category first, then modifiers, then numbers for strength or sequence. Sort a folder by name and check related files group in a sensible order; if not, reorder the name parts. Use English when other or overseas teams may join. Test names on someone new to the project. Use one ID scheme across folders, files and tracking spreadsheets. *(eps 22;261)*
- **TECH-20** [HIGH] Keep every file on a server with locking, merging and history: Put every file under a management tool on a server. Lock a file to edit it, upload when done to unlock. For large files several people need at once, allow simultaneous edits, let the tool merge, and name someone to resolve leftover conflicts by hand. When work goes missing, check version history first, since the tool backs up every edit. *(eps 172)*
- **TECH-07** [MEDIUM] Turn hard memory and processing limits into design tricks: Decide which resource is scarce. If memory is, share and flip art, cap animation frames, store sprites as stacked bitplanes and compress runs. If processing is, trade memory for it with hand-authored paths or frame animations instead of simulation. On size-limited media, test compression on your biggest asset sets early before cutting content. When space or time is tight, use presets or placeholder art so the budget goes into behavior. *(eps 100;96;118;183;244;160)*
- **TECH-13** [MEDIUM] Reuse debug tools for footage, screenshots and player-facing aids: Build replay playback with a free debug camera so trailer and screenshot shots can be re-framed from recorded play. Review debug overlays for ones that would teach players, weigh whether they reveal too much, and ship the clear ones in a training mode. *(eps 163;195;50)*
- **TECH-21** [MEDIUM] Treat a pre-release demo as a second build with its own QA: Decide early and schedule the demo as its own deliverable with its own tuning, updates and near-full QA. Keep spoiler content in separable files so it can be stripped cleanly. Audit the demo build for leftovers, then retest thoroughly. Release a pre-launch demo only when it is balanced enough to represent the game; otherwise consider a post-launch demo and accept the smaller splash. *(eps 122)*
- **TECH-22** [MEDIUM] Build CPU players from data and give them human reflexes: Convert each character's reach, attack timing and movement range into data the AI can query, and feed it the opponent's attack data, position and status. Add a reaction delay. Organize behavior into modes that switch with match state and test cases where two modes conflict. Tune high levels toward offense rather than waiting or perfect defense, and cap perfect blocks and dodges. *(eps 221;141)*
- **TECH-23** [LOW] Seed randomness with an unpredictable value: Factor the system's internal clock or another indefinite value into random number generation. *(eps 189)*
- **TECH-24** [LOW] Match real-time online players within their local region: For synchronous games, matchmake within the local region, not just the country. Consider lowering input density where online play will lag. Tune matchmaking range against population, especially in low-population regions and hours. *(eps 108;170)*
- **TECH-25** [LOW] Keep reference hardware, cables and displays organized and calibrated: Keep reference consoles connected through a selector, label every cable, adapter and selector input, give each console a switchable outlet and ventilation. Calibrate team monitors by eye against one shared reference screen and light review areas neutrally. Verify the display chain for lag before tests. *(eps 216;184;8)*

## Case studies (`--area case-study`)

- **CASE-01** [CRITICAL] Set the game's risk by its target player, not by your own skill: Write down the target player before tuning any risk. For newcomers, soften the harshest penalties while keeping some (flight over pits, but falling still costs a life). For experts, let them raise the risk themselves. Re-check risk whenever the platform or audience shifts. *(eps 4;148;108;125;41;213;226)*
- **CASE-02** [HIGH] Layer optional depth on a core that a novice can finish: Play through using only the core verbs and confirm the game can be finished that way. Put depth systems and audience-specific extras where players choose them: a map entrance, a rule toggle, an optional mode. Make sure the depth layer gives each encounter something to look forward to. *(eps 11;27;226;51;4)*
- **CASE-03** [HIGH] Build the concept as the answer to a named problem: Write the pain point of the genre or the brief in one sentence, then design a core rule that removes it. Test the answer: does it change what the player actually does, or only what the action is called? If it only renames, keep going. *(eps 27;74;86;20;125;57)*
- **CASE-04** [HIGH] Let the platform's unique strength and the market gap shape the concept: List what the target hardware does that others can't and which audiences competitors already serve, then aim the concept at the gap. If shipping on several platforms, give each at least one mode built on its own strength while sharing mechanics, animation and balance. Skip peripheral functions that don't serve the game. *(eps 125;135;154;40;108)*
- **CASE-05** [HIGH] Choose the concept your situation can finish, and uniquely enables: Estimate each candidate concept's development time. Inventory what carries over (team, tools, assets, hardware architecture) and look for the bold idea that inventory makes cheap. Pick the concept that fits both the schedule and that one-time opportunity. *(eps 27;59;11;160;164)*
- **CASE-06** [HIGH] Change a feature's context before cutting it: When a signature mechanic breaks balance or a request seems impossible, list the context levers (screen space, arena bounds, speed, camera, player roles, HUD) and try them before cutting the feature. *(eps 11;20;4)*
- **CASE-07** [HIGH] Build variety as rule-changing parameters on one shared core: Implement one shared behavior, then express each vehicle, stage, character or weapon as a parameter set that changes how it plays (size, gravity, speed, field shape, piece types) plus its own audio. Keep strength-varying layers out of modes that need a level playing field. *(eps 59;74;135;125;206)*
- **CASE-08** [HIGH] Tune and test on the final build and the real hardware: Lock processing speed and frame timing before final balance tuning. After any optimization, replay difficulty and timer-driven systems on the retail unit. When a launch problem is too risky to fix at its core, ship a narrow, contained fix. *(eps 74;92;141;135)*
- **CASE-09** [HIGH] Give each sequel one headline concept its new situation makes possible: Name the sequel's headline in one line and tie it to what is new (hardware, audience, team continuity). Then list every element of the game (characters, stages, graphics, effects, animation, sound, play feel, extras) and set an upgrade target for each. *(eps 40;108;135;160;164;132)*
- **CASE-12** [HIGH] Earn replay value with varied, fun play, not punishing difficulty: Add at least one mode built for variety rather than perfection (a sandbox, random power-ups and events) so players don't converge on one method. Make failure cheap and the next attempt worth seeing. *(eps 4;59;100;128;189)*
- **CASE-10** [MEDIUM] Treat hard limits as design fuel: When memory, processing or screen size is scarce, write down the limit and ask which design answers it (reused and mirrored art, authored patterns instead of simulation, bigger targets, slower motion) before cutting content. *(eps 100;125;135;160;40;42)*
- **CASE-11** [MEDIUM] When a mode is out of budget, rebuild it from strengths you already have: Inventory the assets and systems you already have and design the missing mode around them. Pick lightweight formats when you want volume. Let players over-power themselves as a chosen difficulty, but give fewer rewards when they do. *(eps 160;135;151;40)*
- **CASE-13** [MEDIUM] When adapting, keep the proven core and spend effort on the new format: Identify the proven loop of the source and keep it. Put design effort into what the new format adds. Stay faithful where fans care, invent where the source is silent, and redesign characters to fit their new role. *(eps 92;188;27;100;108)*
- **CASE-14** [MEDIUM] When delegated core work stalls, step in with fresh specs: Set a checkpoint for the core gameplay. If it still doesn't work, have the person with the vision rewrite the specs and rebuild the logic on the existing assets, briefing every discipline together so instructions aren't distorted in relay. *(eps 59;259;114)*
- **CASE-15** [MEDIUM] If people don't get the concept, explain its depth yourself: Write a plain explanation of how to play and why the game is deep, in the words of the person who knows it best, and publish it on a regular schedule where players will find it. *(eps 27;143;245)*
- **CASE-17** [MEDIUM] For a fixed-scope side project, script it all and pilot it first: When the content can be known up front, plan and write all of it before production, pilot a few pieces at final quality, and track everything in one spreadsheet that holds the release order and progress. *(eps 261)*
- **CASE-16** [LOW] Check a new technique fits the team and the game before adopting it: Before switching to a trendy technique mid-project, confirm the team knows it and that it suits how your levels are built. If you go ahead anyway, treat it as skill-building and budget for the learning. *(eps 20)*
