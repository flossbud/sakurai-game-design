# 238 · How Good Were the Player’s Actions?

**Category:** Game Essence · [Video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IGpEp0R1_xY) · [Transcript](../transcripts/238-how-good-were-the-players-actions.md)

## In one sentence
Set difficulty, rewards and penalties by asking how good or bad the player's action really was, so consequences feel earned, while leaving room for deliberate surprises.

## Key ideas
- When deciding difficulty, rewards or penalties, base your thinking on how good the player's actions truly were.
- Consider choices the player can't undo. If they did something really bad, a big penalty can be fun. But can they never redeem themselves? If they can, how much effort does it take? Looking at it from "how bad was that action?" rather than only from the scenario makes you rethink things large and small.
- Success and failure at any task should each have a fitting consequence.
- In Smash Bros., neutral attacks are quick and leave little opening, while smash attacks take time and leave you vulnerable. Landing a smash is more commendable, so it gets the damage and knockback that feat deserves.
- In score-based games, you usually earn more points for stronger, harder-to-beat enemies. Asking how good the action was also makes it easier to design location-based score or damage, or breaking enemy parts to weaken them.
- Players are happiest when their behavior is rewarded correctly. But push this too far and the game becomes predictable and loses excitement, so only polish away the parts that feel unreasonable.
- Breaking convention is fine: an unusually lucrative enemy made hard to beat (high HP, or a tendency to flee) becomes a special objective players strive for.

## Do this
- For every reward, penalty and damage value, ask "how good or bad was the action that triggered this?" and scale it to match.
- Give slow, risky moves (long startup, big opening) more damage and knockback than quick, safe ones.
- For irreversible bad choices, decide explicitly whether the player can recover and how much effort it takes.
- Award points by enemy strength and difficulty to defeat, and consider weak-point bonuses or breakable parts that weaken enemies.
- Add an occasional rule-breaking, lucrative enemy that is hard to beat (high HP or flees) as a special objective.
- Don't make the reward mapping so strict that results become predictable; fix only the consequences that feel unreasonable.

## Avoid
- Permanent penalties with no path to redemption, decided only by scenario logic.
- Rationalizing every reward until the game becomes predictable.

## Examples from the episode
- **Smash Bros. — neutral vs. smash attacks:** the quick, safe neutral attack versus the slow, vulnerable smash attack with more damage and knockback.
- **Score-based games:** more points for stronger enemies, location-based damage or score, and breaking enemy parts to weaken them.
- **A lucrative enemy:** high reward with high HP or a tendency to flee, which makes it a special objective.
