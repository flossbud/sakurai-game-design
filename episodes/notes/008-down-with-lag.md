# 008 · Down with Lag!

**Category:** Grab Bag · [Video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gjoKKyskco4) · [Transcript](../transcripts/008-down-with-lag.md)

## In one sentence
Your game's responsiveness is only as good as the player's display and audio setup, so learn to recognize input lag and test and demo on screens set to Game Mode or Low Latency Mode with extra processing turned off.

## Key ideas
- Even a game with great response time can be ruined by input delay, or "lag." Some lag is unavoidable on LCD displays, though how much varies. When we play, we're seeing images the console created in the past. If that delay is too long it ruins the experience, so it should be kept to a minimum.
- At E3 2018, at Nintendo of America's Treehouse: Live event, the Smash Ultimate setup had so much lag that "It ruined the feel of the controls." He pointed it out and they fixed it. The setup crew must have tested the game, yet nobody noticed (the recording setup probably contributed). His conclusion: "many people don't recognize lag when they see it."
- To test: use a game that responds the instant you press a button. 3D games aren't good for this; an NES game, such as Kirby on Nintendo Switch Online, works well. Press jump. If Kirby doesn't jump the instant you press it, your TV settings may be the culprit.
- Fixes: switch the TV's picture setting to "Low Latency Mode" or "Game Mode" (he checks for these whenever he buys a TV), and turn off any extra options the signal passes through.
- Sometimes only the sound is late. Through a home theater system, impact sounds can arrive late. Try an analog connection like a standard audio jack, or low-latency audio settings. Mismatched equipment may need research and experimenting.
- Screens have two kinds of delay. Response time is how long a pixel takes to change color, and most monitors handle it fine now. Display latency is the tricky one: it comes from post-processing that improves picture quality, which Game Mode or Low Latency Mode bypasses. Without those modes, turn off every picture-enhancing setting.
- TV specs sometimes list display time in milliseconds; lower is better. At 60 fps, 16.67 ms of delay equals one frame.

## Do this
- Before any demo, playtest or event, press jump in an instantly responsive 2D game on that exact setup and confirm the character moves the moment you press. Fix the display chain if it doesn't.
- Set test and demo TVs to Game Mode or Low Latency Mode. If neither exists, turn off every picture-enhancing setting.
- Check audio separately. If hit sounds lag through a home theater system, switch to an analog audio connection or a low-latency audio setting.
- Convert display latency specs to frames: at 60 fps, every 16.67 ms is one frame of delay.
- Check for lag yourself instead of trusting that someone else already tested.

## Avoid
- Assuming a setup is fine because someone has already played on it.
- Leaving TV post-processing on for gameplay.
- Judging lag with a 3D game. Use a 2D game that responds the instant you press a button.

## Examples from the episode
- **Super Smash Bros. Ultimate — E3 2018 Treehouse: Live:** heavy lag ruined the control feel until he pointed it out and it was fixed.
- **Kirby (NES, via Nintendo Switch Online) — jump test:** an instantly responsive game to check whether the display is adding delay.
