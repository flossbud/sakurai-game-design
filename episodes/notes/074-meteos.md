# 074 · Meteos

**Category:** Game Concepts · [Video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=snHecpmPeJs) · [Transcript](../transcripts/074-meteos.md)

## In one sentence
You can invent a fresh concept even in a genre you're bad at: identify its game essence (risk and reward), change the core action rather than just its wording, and make each stage or character change the actual rules.

## Key ideas
- After going freelance he reasoned that one title takes years and yields just one more game, which is not an efficient way to contribute. So he spent a period supervising and consulting, uncredited, because putting his name on those games would have been unfair to the teams that made them.
- Tetsuya Mizuguchi (formerly of Sega) asked him for a falling-block puzzle game, a genre already seen as a bit outdated. He found it a fascinating challenge even though he is really bad at such games, especially Puyo Puyo (he also supervised Kirby's Avalanche).
- His method was to work out the principles of the genre's game essence. The blocks are the risk, and the higher they stack, the greater the risk. Careful planning turns that accumulated risk into a huge reward all at once. The genre is simple, so the link is easy to see.
- He then asked whether the player really needs to *erase* blocks. Bombs that destroy everything already existed, and calling it "vaporized" or "demolished" wouldn't help: the core concept had to change. His answer was that matched blocks "blast off" into the air. Vertical and horizontal matches ignite, which allows tricks like secondary ignitions and air docking, and the idea was absurd enough to make a splash.
- His proposal used PowerPoint animations to explain the idea easily, and it came with a background story: Meteos rain down on your planet, you launch them back in an interplanetary war, and if you lose, your homeworld is doomed.
- The DS was a coincidence. The game was designed for directional buttons, but the touch screen made it feel much snappier.
- Unlike most puzzle games, where picking a character changes little, each Meteos planet changes block shapes, field width, launch characteristics, music and sound effects. Geolyte is laid-back. Boggob gains height with each jump. On Megadom blocks stay suspended longer. Mekks blocks fly straight at set speeds. Forte has strong vertical ignitions. Hevendor blocks vanish when matched. Jeljel launches max out with repeat ignitions. Brabbit moves very slowly. Players had favorites, and collecting Meteos types (which appear at different rates per planet) unlocks new planets.
- Regrets: processing load kept him from the balance he envisioned, and the game shipped with bugs. He tuned every planet personally, but processing speeds changed late in development, which made the game harder than he intended. Later versions (Xbox Live, Disney, flip phones) were made without him. He learned of them from gaming magazines.

## Do this
- Before designing in any genre, write its risk and reward in one sentence (for example "stack height is risk; clearing many at once is reward"). Then brainstorm other ways to discharge that risk besides the genre's default action.
- Test a new idea by asking whether it changes the core concept or only renames the usual action. If it only renames, keep going.
- Make stages or characters change the rules themselves (field size, physics, speeds, piece shapes, audio), not just the art, so each one plays differently and players pick favorites.
- Pitch mechanics with animated mock-ups (even PowerPoint animations) rather than static text, so readers see the idea in motion.
- Lock processing speed and frame timing before final balance tuning, and redo the tuning if performance changes late.

## Avoid
- "Novelty" that only renames the genre's standard action ("vaporized," "demolished") without changing the concept.
- Changing processing speed late in development after balance has been tuned.

## Examples from the episode
- **Meteos — launching blocks:** matched blocks ignite and blast off instead of disappearing, enabling secondary ignitions and air docking.
- **Meteos — planets:** each planet has distinct physics, field width, block shapes and audio (Geolyte, Boggob, Megadom, Mekks, Forte, Hevendor, Jeljel, Brabbit).
- **Meteos — difficulty:** a late change in processing speed made the game harder than his per-planet tuning intended.
- **Puyo Puyo / Kirby's Avalanche:** a genre he's bad at, yet he could still design for it by understanding its essence.
