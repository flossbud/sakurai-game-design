# 226 · Targeting and Market Research

**Category:** Marketing · [Video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Y0sf-emY5oE) · [Transcript](../transcripts/226-targeting-and-market-research.md)

## In one sentence
Don't let targeting and market research generate your new game ideas: they only describe paths others have already taken, and following them leads to average games in crowded markets.

## Key ideas
- Targeting means being specific about who you'll sell to, usually by age, gender and tastes: focusing on audiences that strongly support certain products and making up for weaker ones. Market research means studying trends in those audiences. Sakurai notes that Nintendo has remarkably precise data on its titles.
- He doesn't object to either, and they work well in some industries, but for coming up with new game ideas he recommends not taking them too seriously.
- The data is built on what came before, so it can't show what comes next. Audience boundaries are blurry: some older people like cute things, and some kids prefer intense content.
- The original Kirby was designed mainly for beginners, never "for children." Beginners can be children or adults, men or women. It still has "oomph", so advanced players enjoy how it controls too. You can't sum up its audience in one word.
- Market research shows the safe main road most people have taken. Keep following it and your work becomes more average and mediocre, and sells less. Around the NES-to-SNES transition, research showed RPGs selling well, so many Japanese companies made Dragon Quest or Final Fantasy-like games and all dove into an overcrowded market.
- Innovators stand alone because they walk paths nobody has walked, yet they shape the landscape and open new roads for others. Business needs won't always lead there, and Sakurai hopes the people deciding strategy take that to heart.

## Do this
- Define your target by experience level or need (e.g., "beginners") rather than by narrow demographic slices like age or gender.
- Form the concept first, then use market data to check its risks. Don't use the data to pick the concept.
- When research says a genre is selling, ask how crowded that genre will be when you release and what you offer that the others don't.
- Even when designing for beginners, give the controls enough depth that advanced players enjoy them too.

## Avoid
- Copying whatever genre the data says is selling, as in the flood of Dragon Quest and Final Fantasy-style games in the NES-to-SNES era.
- Treating demographic lines as hard boundaries.

## Examples from the episode
- **Kirby (the original game) — target audience:** made for "beginners" of any age or gender, but with enough oomph that advanced players enjoy the controls.
- **NES-to-SNES era Japanese RPGs:** research-driven imitations of Dragon Quest and Final Fantasy crowded into the same market.

## Quote
> Targeting and market research data is built on everything that's come before. They don't show the path to what comes next. (01:33)
