# 173 · Teaching Players How to Play

**Category:** Design Specifics · [Video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Icl-jMlz1iY) · [Transcript](../transcripts/173-teaching-players-how-to-play.md)

## In one sentence
Teach your controls by letting players play the real game while they learn, not through manual-style text or a separate training room, and protect that hands-on tutorial in the schedule.

## Key ideas
- Game controls are complex and hard to teach. Most genres reuse familiar control schemes so experienced players know how to play right away, but game mechanics won't evolve if we always lean on those conventions.
- Whether or not your controls are unique, a tutorial is essential to break down the rules. But nobody wants a hand-holding tutorial, and the worst option is making players read long paragraphs like an instruction manual.
- Better, but still something to avoid, is an inorganic training area, because players will think "Get on with it already! Give me the real game!"
- A fairly common approach is posting text along the path in the opening stage so players read it and then try it right away. It works for simple controls but doesn't suit every genre.
- Smash Bros. only includes a non-interactive video. That isn't what Sakurai wants: hands-on tutorials were planned many times and couldn't be implemented in the end. "Break the Targets!" in the first Smash Bros. was built to help players master the controls, which is why it differs for each character.
- His rule is that you must let players play while you teach them. The catch is that he also doesn't want players to feel restricted, which makes it a real conundrum.
- A hands-on mode for teaching techniques in Smash Bros. Ultimate was cut for schedule reasons, and he regrets that "probably more than anything else".

## Do this
- Replace text-heavy tutorial screens with short prompts placed inside the opening stage, right where the player will use that action next.
- Teach controls inside real gameplay instead of in a detached practice area.
- Build a mode that doubles as control training, for example a per-character challenge built around each character's own moves, as "Break the Targets!" was.
- Treat the hands-on tutorial as a must-ship feature in the schedule, not an extra that can be dropped late.
- Playtest the tutorial for any point where players feel restricted or held back from the real game, and loosen it there.

## Avoid
- Long paragraphs of instructions in the style of a manual, which Sakurai calls the worst thing you can do.
- A separate, inorganic training area that keeps players away from the real game.
- Relying on text along the path for complex controls in genres where it doesn't fit.

## Examples from the episode
- **Smash Bros. series — tutorials:** only a non-interactive video sequence teaches the controls, even though hands-on ways to learn were planned many times.
- **Super Smash Bros. (first game) — "Break the Targets!":** designed to help players master the controls, so each character gets a different layout.
- **Smash Bros. Ultimate — cut tutorial mode:** a hands-on mode to teach techniques was dropped for schedule reasons, and it is one of his biggest regrets.

## Quote
> At any rate, I'd say it's vital that you let players play the game as you teach them. (01:23)
