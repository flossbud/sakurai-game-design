# 125 · Kid Icarus: Uprising

**Category:** Game Concepts · [Video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=p-uwRfckTs4) · [Transcript](../transcripts/125-kid-icarus-uprising.md)

## In one sentence
Build your concept from what the hardware uniquely offers and what the market lacks, then shape every system (opening, enemy size, controls, story delivery) around how and where the game will actually be played.

## Key ideas
- After Smash Bros. Brawl, President Iwata asked Sakurai to consult on the unannounced 3DS's specs from an outside perspective and to make an exclusive game for it, with no genre specified. Concept from the gap: Touch! Generations titles already covered casual players on the DS, so he went the opposite way, a rich, meaty game for hardcore players. Glasses-free stereoscopic 3D meant depth had to be part of the fun, so he chose a third-person shooter: a rail shooter en route to each battlefield, then TPS on the ground. He felt existing FPS/TPS games were too stuck in by-the-book theory, and wanted to present TPS in a new way, the way Smash was a response to fighting games (for example, flick gestures on the touch screen).
- The Kid Icarus motif came later, at the end of the proposal. Iwata encouraged using a Nintendo character if it fit the concept. The series had had almost no entries since 1986 and there was demand for one, especially outside Japan, and Sakurai had just redesigned Pit and Palutena for Brawl. Pit can't fly, so Palutena grants him the Power of Flight for only five minutes, after which he fights on land. Project Sora was set up as a one-time company to be closed when the game was done.
- The game begins with a rail-shooter air battle (the route to the battlefield) because starting with TPS would force players to learn dodging, aiming and firing all at once, too much for beginners. He also wanted simple shooting with quick scenery changes. Because air battles have a known length, music could be timed to the exact beats and moods of each scene.
- In FPS/TPS games you often focus on a tiny part of the screen to hit distant targets, which suits a handheld poorly, so enemies were made many times larger than normal: even standard enemies are four or five times Pit's size.
- Controls: flicking the Circle Pad dashes or dodges (an idea carried over from Smash). Combining dashes with normal, charged and rapid fire gives many kinds of shots. Dodging just before an attack lands is risk and reward, simple yet deep (for example, holding the reticle on an enemy while dashing around it). The touch screen aims like a trackball: a swipe gives the camera inertia, a tap stops it, a drag fine-tunes, and sensitivity is adjustable. One attack button handles both shooting and melee; the game picks based on weapon and enemy distance. A twin-stick peripheral arrived just before launch, but it added about 5% processor load while the game was already at the hardware's limits, so it couldn't be fully supported.
- Story is delivered during play like a radio program, with dialogue but generally no briefings or full cutscenes, and can be turned off. The tone is comical and light, following the original game, but still has big moments. He meant to outsource the scenario but wrote it himself, because air battles had to line up exactly with story beats. Several hostile armies (Medusa and Hades' Underworld Army, Viridi's Forces of Nature, the Aurum, the Chaos Kin) add variety, plot twists and room for weapon origin stories.
- 108 weapons in nine categories were treated like characters, changing things like Pit's speed and not just melee or ranged strength. Varied strength made them collectibles. With no stream of updates, he's glad the balance kept everyone from reaching for the same weapon in multiplayer. Other systems: Powers (fit panels tightly to maximize buffs), the Fiend's Cauldron difficulty, Light vs. Dark (stronger weapons drain the team gauge more when their wielder falls; the last player defeated becomes a tough angel who works like a moving base), and AR Idol Cards. Development struggled with unstable performance on still-in-development 3DS hardware and a mixed team that clashed over how to do things, lessons he carried into later projects.

## Do this
- List what your target hardware does uniquely and which player groups competitors already serve, then aim your concept at the gap.
- Open with a simpler version of the core action (such as an on-rails segment) before asking players to dodge, aim and fire at the same time.
- On small screens, scale enemies well up (Kid Icarus standard enemies are 4–5x the hero's size) so players aren't squinting at tiny distant targets.
- Use fixed-length segments to sync music to specific beats, and write the scenario in-house when story beats must match gameplay timing.
- Deliver story as in-play dialogue instead of briefings and cutscenes, with an option to turn it off.
- Make one attack button context-sensitive (ranged vs. melee by weapon and distance), and give weapons differences beyond damage, such as movement speed.

## Avoid
- Making a game's opening demand dodging, aiming and firing all at once.
- Following genre conventions "by the book" instead of rethinking the genre.
- Requiring players to focus on tiny areas of a small handheld screen.

## Examples from the episode
- **Kid Icarus: Uprising — air battles:** rail-shooter openings that ease beginners in and allow music timed to exact beats.
- **Kid Icarus: Uprising — land battles:** standard enemies 4–5x Pit's size; flick-to-dash dodges as risk and reward; trackball-style touch aiming.
- **Kid Icarus: Uprising — weapons:** 108 types across nine categories, each changing Pit's play (including speed).
- **Kid Icarus: Uprising — Light vs. Dark:** weapon value drains the team gauge on defeat; the last player becomes an angel; Daybreak debuted here and is now in Smash.
- **Touch! Generations (DS):** already covered the casual market, so Uprising targeted hardcore players.
- **Super Smash Bros.:** source of the flick input, and a model for answering a genre (fighting games) with a fresh take.
