# 096 · The Family Computer (and the NES)

**Category:** Grab Bag · [Video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=PHBKYrnIUmY) · [Transcript](../transcripts/096-the-family-computer-and-the-nes.md)

## In one sentence
A platform (or a game) wins when strong graphics, sound, smooth performance, easy controls, a fair price and above all fun arrive together, and technical skill can pack far more game into the same tiny budget.

## Key ideas
- Sakurai is proud to have made a Famicom game, Kirby's Adventure. Only a small handful of directors his age ever did, and even he almost missed his chance despite entering the industry young.
- The Famicom launched in Japan on July 15, 1983. Most games then had single-color pixel blobs that jumped between frames as they walked, so it felt like a breath of fresh air: gorgeous graphics, memorable music and sound effects, smooth and speedy picture processing, an easy-to-use controller, an affordable price and, best of all, fun games. You could even play the arcade hit Donkey Kong with more or less the same game essence.
- He bought one in 1983. The first controllers had square rubber buttons that could stick after heavy use. He believes they switched to round buttons after about a year, but says he isn't positive.
- The NES came out overseas in 1985, North America first. In those two years Japanese games made big leaps in craft and content: Mario Bros. (1983) was 24 KB of ROM and Super Mario Bros. (1985) was 40 KB, a small-sounding increase that "allowed for a lot more game." NES bundles included the Action Set (with a Super Mario Bros. combo cart) and the Deluxe Set (with R.O.B., the "Robotic Operating Buddy"). Older, simpler titles were on sale at the same time, so players may have been surprised by how different the games were.
- Bigger ROMs and custom chips on cartridges, which were cheap enough to put in individual carts instead of the console, turned the Famicom into almost a different machine. Famicom connector pins allowed expanded audio, so Japanese versions sometimes had richer music than overseas ones.
- Famicom mania swept Japan in the mid-to-late 1980s, with people lining up in the middle of the night for hot releases (preorders and mail order weren't a thing yet). That's how he got a limited-quantity Disk System on launch day. Cartridges were expensive for kids, so they bought few, played them a lot and lent them to friends.
- Without the Famicom, the whole game industry would look completely different. At the time it was "an absolute dream machine."

## Do this
- When memory or storage is tight, look for technical ingenuity before asking for more resources. Super Mario Bros. delivered far more game than Mario Bros. with only 40 KB vs 24 KB.
- Review your game on every front the Famicom got right: visuals, music and SFX, smooth motion (no choppy frame-jumping), easy controls, and whether it's actually fun.

## Examples from the episode
- **Famicom vs. other 1983 games:** smooth, colorful sprites instead of single-color blobs that jumped between frames.
- **Donkey Kong (Famicom):** the arcade hit at home with more or less the same game essence.
- **Mario Bros. vs. Super Mario Bros.:** 24 KB vs. 40 KB of ROM, with a huge jump in game content.
- **Famicom controller:** square rubber buttons that could stick, later replaced with round ones.
- **Custom chips / expansion audio:** cartridges carried extra processing, and Japanese carts sometimes had richer music than overseas versions.
