# 110 · Canceled Games

**Category:** Grab Bag · [Video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5Ob9OA0peQY) · [Transcript](../transcripts/110-canceled-games.md)

## In one sentence
Any game can be canceled, usually when remaining costs outweigh expected revenue, so everyone on the team should understand what it takes to actually finish the game instead of assuming it will ship once they've done their own tasks.

## Key ideas
- Many games die partway through development, many of them before they're ever announced, including some from established series that would stun players. They're shelved quietly.
- It's heartbreaking for developers who spent months or years on them. Employees still get paid as long as the company survives, though. The company takes the hit: it loses the potential profit and still pays the development costs, and one failed title can send it reeling.
- He expects a common reason is that the company can't otherwise keep itself going, which doesn't necessarily mean it's going out of business. When funding is tight, it's not uncommon to drop a less promising project for a safer one.
- Cancellations usually come from weighing future development costs against expected revenue. A game can sell far better than predicted, but without signs that it will, it's hard to be optimistic.
- Costs peak toward the end of development, when testing, marketing and the largest headcount all hit at once. Cancellation is about cutting losses, so if you don't think you can make it, it's best to get out while you still can.
- Developers should keep the risk in mind and not assume a finished product will appear just because everyone completes their assignments. The more each member understands what it takes to finish, the better the odds of seeing it through, and everyone has to work together constructively.
- Countless games never come out, so be thankful whenever one reaches release safely.

## Do this
- Make sure every team member can see what's still needed to finish the whole game (not just their own task list), for example with a shared, visible list of what remains to ship.
- Re-weigh remaining development cost against realistic expected revenue before entering the expensive final stretch (testing, marketing, peak staffing), and cut losses early if the project won't make it.
- Assume cancellation is a real risk on every project and plan as a team to finish, not just to complete individual assignments.

## Avoid
- Assuming the game will ship just because everyone completes what they were assigned.
- Pushing a project you don't believe can make it into its costliest late phase.

## Examples from the episode
- **Unannounced cancellations (general):** titles from established series quietly shelved before players ever heard of them.

## Quote
> At the very least, you shouldn't assume that you'll end up with a finished product just because everyone completes what they're assigned. (02:17)
