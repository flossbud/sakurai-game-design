# 011 · Kirby's Adventure

**Category:** Game Concepts · [Video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=khJa2vIW0xY) · [Transcript](../transcripts/011-kirbys-adventure.md)

## In one sentence
When your audience mixes beginners and veterans, layer optional depth on a simple core, as copy abilities sit on top of inhale-and-spit, so novices can finish the game and experts can dig deeper.

## Key ideas
- Background: right after Kirby's Dream Land, the company was insolvent and in settlement proceedings, with "seized" stickers on the PCs. He was told to make Kirby's Dream Land for the NES, probably meant as a straight port. Planning began in 1992. They chose the NES over the SNES because the company wanted a game it could develop and sell quickly, and setting up an SNES dev environment would have taken too long.
- The challenge: by then the NES market had matured and few beginners were starting on it. "How do I make something both beginners and veterans can enjoy?" More than that, how do you satisfy both groups?
- His answer was the copy ability, which took the original concept of "using enemies" one step further. Novices can beat the whole game just by inhaling enemies and spitting them out. Experienced players can dig into copy abilities to make parts more fun or pull off tricks. "There's something there for everyone."
- The copy ability also boosted Kirby's appeal as a character. Watching him change forms is fun in itself, and "it makes you look forward to each new enemy encounter." "Every individual enemy now had meaning, which was a huge deal." He shows pages of his rough design document, drawn in a rush.
- Kirby kept the same pixel count as on the Game Boy, so on the NES's larger display he looked smaller, and the different aspect ratio made him wider. With more screen space, flight got even stronger: "It's possible to fly past just about any challenge in the game." He considered removing flight but kept it and changed the context instead:
  - He enlarged the info panel at the bottom of the screen. That limited vertical space, made the characters look bigger, and gave a spot for fun pictures of each copy ability.
  - He confined mid-boss fights to one screen, as in the first game.
  - He reduced flight speed, among other tweaks, and "I think it ended up nicely balanced."
- He added three one-button minigames as an extra for novices ("All the fun, none of the complex controls"). Their entrances were on the world map, so they were completely optional and easy to skip.
- Development was stressful ("it really did a number on my stomach"), but the copy ability greatly raised Kirby's popularity and shaped the rest of the series.

## Do this
- Layer an optional depth system on top of a minimal core, then play through using only the core to confirm the game can be finished that way.
- Give each enemy type something distinct the player can take, such as an ability, so every encounter is something to look forward to.
- When a movement ability becomes too strong, try changing its context before cutting it: shrink the visible play area, confine key fights to one screen, lower its speed.
- Make HUD space earn its place. A larger panel can limit play space, make characters read bigger, and show the current ability's art.
- Offer novice-friendly extras, like one-button minigames, as optional entries on the map so others can skip them.
- When moving to a new screen size or aspect ratio, check how character size and abilities read and play.

## Avoid
- Cutting a signature mechanic just because it became too strong when context changes could rebalance it.
- Forcing optional extras on players who aren't interested.
