# 161 · Avoiding Comparison

**Category:** Planning & Game Design · [Video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gYuvggptkDM) · [Transcript](../transcripts/161-avoiding-comparison.md)

## In one sentence
When you pitch or present a new game, don't define it by comparison to existing titles. Explain what makes it its own thing and let listeners make comparisons on their own.

## Key ideas
- Whenever an innovative game is announced, people compare it to something familiar, because it's hard to understand something new without a reference point.
- Pitching is hard for the same reason. Early in planning, only the designer, plus maybe a few people they consulted, knows what the game will become, yet outside decision-makers have to grasp it. Judge it hopeless and you get no budget or team; pitch it well and you win allies.
- People can only judge your game against what already exists, so you'll be tempted to explain it that way. Resist as much as you can. Listeners are free to make comparisons, but you shouldn't invite them.
- Reason one: if your game really is that close to another title, people may just play the original. Only sequels in the same series should sell themselves by pointing to other games, and a company should avoid having to fend off copycat accusations.
- Reason two: a reference means different things to different generations and experiences. Say "Mario" and some people picture Super Mario Bros. on the NES, others Mario Odyssey, and some the character rather than any game. Don't measure things by your own personal history.
- Comparisons are still handy for sharing information depending on the situation or person, but "the best answer will fall somewhere outside those comparisons." Make the proposal and the finished game shine in their own way.

## Do this
- Rewrite your pitch's opening so it describes your game's core experience and appeal without naming another game, then check whether a listener could still picture it.
- Before you lean on a reference, ask whether this particular audience shares it. "Mario" might mean the NES game, Mario Odyssey or just the character.
- Save "it's like X" for informal explanations to people you know share the reference, and never make it the headline selling point unless you're pitching a sequel in the same series.
- In the proposal, spend your space on what is new and only yours, the part no comparison covers.

## Avoid
- Pitching a new game as "X, but with Y". It invites "then why not just play X?" and copycat accusations.
- Assuming your own play history is your audience's reference point.

## Examples from the episode
- **Mario — one word, many pictures:** Super Mario Bros. on the NES, Mario Odyssey, or the character himself, depending on the listener's generation and experience.

## Quote
> In your proposal and your final product, do your best to let things shine in a way all their own. (02:35)
