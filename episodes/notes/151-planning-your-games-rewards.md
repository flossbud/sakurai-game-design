# 151 · Planning Your Game’s Rewards

**Category:** Planning & Game Design · [Video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=44ONFHla-sw) · [Transcript](../transcripts/151-planning-your-games-rewards.md)

## In one sentence
Design your rewards in the planning stage and pick types that fit your concept. If making the character stronger would undermine the game (as in a fighting game), reward with unlocks, collectibles and goals instead.

## Key ideas
- You must reward players, but you can't if you have nothing to give them, so think hard about rewards during planning.
- **Getting stronger is the most effective in-game reward:** buying better equipment with currency, leveling up with experience, unlocking abilities with skill points, powerful enemy drops, treasure chests, or fusing materials into equipment, items or facilities. "These kinds of inclusions make the player want to keep going." In Kirby games, not knowing which copy ability comes next makes players want to continue. Letting players lose things they've collected can add depth.
- **Not every concept fits:** In a fighting game where characters grow stronger as you progress, it can feel like the "real" game doesn't start until every level is maxed out. So Smash rewards differently.
- **Smash's rewards:**
  - **Fighters:** must be unlocked, and the unlock is "gamified" by making players fight incoming challengers, who show up at a good pace.
  - **Trophies** (Melee through 3DS/Wii U): taught players about the many games Smash references while rewarding them, but took a lot of work and inflated production costs.
  - **Stickers** (Brawl): placed on a trophy stand to power up a fighter. With no descriptions or models to make, far more characters could be included.
  - **Spirits:** characters presented to fit the story, equippable to boost strength, and as easy to scale up as stickers. It's fun to see so many characters appear.
- **Other rewards:** Coins are earned by any kind of play, but there's no stronger gear to buy with them (remember the Coin Launcher). Some music is allocated as rewards, though this has been cut back in recent years because some people want the music from the start. Beating the game lets you adjust how often menu music plays, and unlocked in-game movies can be rewatched any time.
- **Challenges** (first used as the "Checklist" in Kirby Air Ride, before consoles had achievement systems): meeting a condition breaks its panel and reveals the unlock conditions of adjacent panels. Many panels give rewards, but the feature is more about giving players things to work toward.
- Objectives are rewards in their own right: motivation and reward are "two sides of the same coin." It's hard to avoid going overboard, but a game without rewards or new developments is "a drab game," so plan compensation from the planning and design stages.

## Do this
- During planning, list every reward the game will give and roughly when it arrives. If the list is thin, design more reward systems before production starts.
- Check whether stat-growth rewards fit your concept. If they would make the "real" game seem to start only at max level (e.g., a competitive fighting game), reward with unlocks, collectibles and options instead.
- Gamify unlocks: have a new character arrive as a challenger the player must beat to unlock them, and pace those encounters so they don't become frustrating.
- Choose collectible formats with production cost in mind. Items with models and written descriptions (trophies) are expensive. Image-based items (stickers, spirits) let you include far more.
- Add a goal grid: each completed condition breaks its panel and reveals the conditions of neighboring panels, so players always see their next targets.
- Don't lock content that many players want from the start (e.g., music) behind progression.

## Avoid
- Leaving rewards until late in development. You can't reward players if you have nothing to give them.
- Character-strengthening progression in a game where it would make the "real" game start only at max level.
- Going overboard with reward systems.

## Examples from the episode
- **Kirby games:** not knowing which copy ability comes next drives players forward.
- **Smash Bros. — challengers:** unlocking fighters by beating incoming challengers.
- **Smash Bros. Melee through 3DS/Wii U — trophies:** educational rewards, but costly.
- **Smash Bros. Brawl — stickers:** power-ups with no models or descriptions, so far more characters.
- **Spirits:** story-fitting, equippable, and easy to scale.
- **Smash Bros. — coins, music and options:** coins earned by any kind of play with no stronger gear to buy (and the Coin Launcher). Music unlocks, since cut back. After beating the game, adjustable menu-music frequency and rewatchable movies.
- **Kirby Air Ride — Checklist (later "Challenges"):** a panel grid that reveals adjacent conditions, predating console achievement systems.

## Quote
> You could say that game objectives are rewards in their own right, and that motivation and reward are simply two sides of the same coin. (04:34)
