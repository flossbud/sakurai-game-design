# 014 · Assigning Animations

**Category:** Animation · [Video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fV8xIP480qk) · [Transcript](../transcripts/014-assigning-animations.md)

## In one sentence
Before animation starts, give animators an animation list with every move's length and attack timing (chosen around the game's push and pull) plus photos of a posed figure for key poses, and give direction with all the animators in the room.

## Key ideas
- When planning fighters for a game like Smash Bros., you have to think about how their actions will be animated, and getting those ideas across to the animation team is vital.
- Step one is an animation list: a written document listing every animation in the game, with each movement described in text. He notes each animation's length and when the attack itself occurs, choosing timings "that feel right" with the game's "push and pull" in mind. Loops like idle and walk get "Loop" or "Any" for timing.
- For key poses (idle, movement, attacks) he uses a posable figure. Pose it, photograph it, cut out the shape, adjust the contrast, and put it into the spec sheet.
- An average fighter gets 70 to 80 pages of these images. If a move references the character's original game, he may include a screenshot from it. For fighters that aren't remotely humanoid, he may skip visual reference and use text alone.
- This is only the first step. Animators have to fill in gaps and work out details, and a figure's range of movement is much smaller than an in-game model's, so they do "a good bit of heavy lifting."
- Some things don't go as planned no matter how much he steps in. He may let an idea go, but he always takes "extra care to try and make the character shine and offer the best game experience possible."
- He gives animation direction with all the animators present. Sharing practical knowledge and pointers means everyone avoids the same missteps and improves together.

## Do this
- Write an animation list for each character: every animation, a text description, its total length, and when the attack becomes active. Mark idle and walk loops "Loop" or "Any."
- Set those timings from gameplay (push and pull) before animators begin, not after.
- For key poses, pose a physical figure, photograph it, cut out the silhouette, raise the contrast, and paste it into the spec sheet.
- Add source-game screenshots when a move references a character's original game. Use text only for non-humanoid characters a figure can't represent.
- Give animation feedback in a session with every animator present, so each critique teaches the whole team.
- Expect animators to fill gaps, and be willing to drop an idea that doesn't work while holding to the goal of making the character shine.

## Avoid
- Treating the director's spec as the finished animation. Figures move far less than in-game models, so the animators' interpretation is essential.
- Giving direction to one animator at a time, which leaves the others to repeat the same missteps.

## Examples from the episode
- **Super Smash Bros. — fighter spec sheets:** an animation list plus photographed figure poses, 70–80 pages per fighter on average.
- **Super Smash Bros. — moves from a character's original game:** conveyed with screenshots from that game.
- **Super Smash Bros. — non-humanoid fighters:** specified in text alone.
