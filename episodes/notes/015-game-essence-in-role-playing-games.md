# 015 · Game Essence in Role-Playing Games

**Category:** Game Essence · [Video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BFGkFHb2lBw) · [Transcript](../transcripts/015-game-essence-in-role-playing-games.md)

## In one sentence
Build an RPG's push and pull from exploration (go deeper for bigger rewards at the risk of losing your spoils) and combat (read enemies and exploit their weaknesses), and match the complexity to how casual your game is.

## Key ideas
- Exploration: RPGs need "an adventure into unknown dangers." The deeper you go, the stronger and scarier the enemies, but you push on for a greater reward. "Do you press your luck? Or get out while you still can?" Falling in battle means losing the spoils you've found, so the risk and reward are clear.
- More "casual" games let you leave dungeons whenever you want or skip resource management, but they can still have nail-biting challenge by making individual battles harder.
- Combat (turn-based here, since action RPGs also have to balance action scenes): when an enemy appears, the player sizes it up by how it looks and acts. "Maybe it seems weak to fire." If the player tries something and it works, they gain experience: "Player experience, that is."
- Exploiting weaknesses to maximize damage is "both strategy and game essence at play." But players don't always think that hard, so a casual game shouldn't get too complex.
- In games like Persona, exploiting weaknesses sets up combos, which gives battles more variety and rhythm. The design pushes you to exploit weaknesses, "which makes for great balance."
- RPGs are a vast genre, so "don't let existing conventions limit how you design your push and pull!"

## Do this
- Make dungeon depth a risk-reward dial. Put stronger enemies and better rewards deeper in, and make defeat cost the spoils collected, so retreating versus pressing on is a real decision.
- If you make leaving dungeons or managing resources easy (a casual design), make individual battles harder to keep the tension.
- Give enemies looks and behavior that hint at their weaknesses, so players learn by experimenting.
- Reward exploiting a weakness with more than extra damage, for example a chance to set up a combo, as Persona does, to add variety and rhythm.
- Scale your weakness system's complexity to your audience, and keep it simple for casual players.

## Avoid
- Overly complex weakness systems in a casual game.
- Letting genre conventions limit your push-and-pull design.

## Examples from the episode
- **Persona — weakness combos:** exploiting a weakness sets up combos, giving battles variety and rhythm.
- **RPG dungeons in general — press on or retreat:** going deeper raises both the reward and the risk of losing your spoils.
