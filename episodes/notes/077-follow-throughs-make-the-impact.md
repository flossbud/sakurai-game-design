# 077 · Follow-Throughs Make the Impact

**Category:** Animation · [Video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cIB0BUe6Ihk) · [Transcript](../transcripts/077-follow-throughs-make-the-impact.md)

## In one sentence
The recovery after an attack takes up most of its screen time, so animate it big and expressive to sell the attack's weight, and place cancel frames where players expect to get control back.

## Key ideas
- The follow-through is the motion after an attack, when the character is briefly vulnerable (the recovery period). It may seem unimportant, but it takes up the most screen time.
- In a competitive action game like Smash Bros., attacks should be responsive, so lead-ins move quickly into the hit. But for push and pull, strong attacks must leave the character proportionally vulnerable afterward, or the game feels unrefined.
- Standard attacks generally finish in under a second, and even Smash Attacks with large vulnerability windows almost never exceed one second. The hit registers about a quarter to a third of the way in, and some faster attacks register at a fifth. Everything after that is follow-through: short before, long after.
- He shows Smash Bros. attacks marked with when they register, when they finish, and where cancel frames end the recovery.
- The hit itself is over almost instantly, a fraction of a second. So the follow-through animation must clearly show what the character just did.
- Don't just interpolate straight back to the standby pose. Use the recovery to show the weight of the attack: keep it going as long as you can and make the movements big.
- Assign cancel frames to part of the return to standby, so players can regain control early. Their timing should match when people expect to get control back, and that balance matters.

## Do this
- Lay out each attack's timeline so the hit registers about 1/4 to 1/3 of the way through (about 1/5 for fast moves), with the total move under one second.
- Scale recovery to strength: the harder an attack hits, the longer the character stays vulnerable afterward.
- Animate the recovery as a big, continuing motion that carries the momentum and weight of the strike, instead of blending straight back to idle.
- Mark cancel frames late in the recovery, at the point players instinctively expect control back, and playtest that timing.

## Avoid
- Interpolating straight from the hit back to the standby pose.
- Strong attacks with little vulnerability afterward. Push and pull collapses and the game feels unrefined.
- Letting the follow-through fizzle out instead of staying expressive to the end.

## Examples from the episode
- **Super Smash Bros. — attack breakdowns:** footage of attacks with hit-registration, end and cancel-frame points marked, showing how long recovery lasts after a normal attack.
- **Super Smash Bros. — Smash Attacks:** even with large vulnerability windows they almost never exceed one second.

## Quote
> The follow-through after an attack determines the impact of the attack itself. Don't let it fizzle out—keep those actions expressive right to the end. (04:01)
