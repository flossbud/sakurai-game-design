# 223 · Flashing Effects

**Category:** Effects · [Video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Mmw39T_dBLI) · [Transcript](../transcripts/223-flashing-effects.md)

## In one sentence
Use brief full-screen flashes for impact and character flashes to show status changes, and build dark elements into character flashes so they keep their contrast instead of fading into the background.

## Key ideas
- "Flashing" means making the screen or a character shine. Stricter regulations mean modern ports of classic games keep it mild, but on CRTs it used to be dazzling.
- A full-screen flash works like screen shake: because it covers a large area, it adds a lot of impact. A character flash emphasizes a change in that character's status. Sakurai calls both crucial.
- Character flashes usually use additive blending (rapidly alternating normal colors with a white-added version) or specific lighting. Adding one color evenly lowers contrast, because the same amount goes onto dark and light areas and they average out. A white flash also makes the object harder to see on a light background.
- In past games he mixed light and dark elements into flashes so the dark parts still stood out on light backgrounds. For Smash Bros. Ultimate he had shadows added to the hit flash to give it internal contrast, and drew a mockup to show the team.
- Flash strength changes with camera distance. In Ultimate the shadowed flash shows more when the camera is pulled back. A stronger effect up close would make more of the screen flash, and he calls that "no good."
- In Smash Ultimate, colors carry meaning. White flashes show invulnerability (for example while getting up). A yellow flash after being grabbed and thrown shows the window when the character can't be grabbed again. Rim lighting, which looks like light shining from behind and emphasizes the outline, shows certain damage types and status changes such as invulnerability.
- Flashes almost always last a single brief moment. LCD response times change how they look, so they are hard to get right, but they add enough impact to be worth perfecting.

## Do this
- Add a brief full-screen flash to your heaviest impacts, as you would use screen shake. Keep the intensity within modern photosensitivity limits.
- For hit flashes, mix shadow or dark elements into the flash instead of only adding white, so the character keeps internal contrast on bright backgrounds.
- Give each status its own flash color or treatment and use it consistently, e.g., white = invulnerable, yellow = can't be grabbed again yet.
- Use rim lighting to signal states like invulnerability, since it emphasizes the character's outline.
- Scale flash strength with camera distance so close-up shots don't fill the screen with flashing.
- Check every flash on real LCD screens, because response time changes how one-frame flashes look.

## Avoid
- Flashing a character by adding white or one color evenly, which removes contrast and makes them vanish on light backgrounds.
- Full-strength flashes when the camera is zoomed in, which make too much of the screen flash.

## Examples from the episode
- **Smash Bros. Ultimate — hit flash:** shadows were added for internal contrast, based on a mockup Sakurai drew. The effect is most visible with the camera zoomed out.
- **Smash Bros. Ultimate — white flash:** the character is invulnerable, e.g., while getting up.
- **Smash Bros. Ultimate — yellow flash:** after a throw, the period when the character can't be grabbed again.
- **Smash Bros. Ultimate — rim lighting:** used for certain damage types and status changes such as invulnerability.
