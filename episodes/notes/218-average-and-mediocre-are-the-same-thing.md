# 218 · Average and Mediocre Are the Same Thing

**Category:** Design Specifics · [Video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KW4x-_Y8BmM) · [Transcript](../transcripts/218-average-and-mediocre-are-the-same-thing.md)

## In one sentence
When balancing, keep characters distinctive by amplifying their shortcomings to offset their strengths, not by sanding their strengths down, because average is mediocre.

## Key ideas
- The Smash Bros. tuning team proposes changes based on research and play tester feedback. If you balance purely on fighter strength, abilities quickly level out: weaknesses get patched, strengths get cut, and every fighter ends up average. That isn't fun.
- Sakurai's instruction to the team is to keep fighters unique "by amplifying shortcomings to offset advantages."
- A Mario vs. Mario match has no balance problems at all, but it's no fun if that is all you have. Push and pull still exists between identical sides, but only up to a point. Pitting two distinct sides against each other produces far more variety in strategy.
- If you treat uniqueness as the ideal, you will get mixed reactions. The internet can be an echo chamber, so once people call something strong or weak, that view gains momentum and seems truer than it is. Even so, those differing values lead to different choices and a richer experience. People who want to prove a point should "settle it in an actual match."
- Without individuality there's no point in having a large roster. Fighter reveals are exciting because each character's traits are firmly established, and something with no strengths or weaknesses has little charm.
- Uniqueness doesn't have to hurt competitive balance. Real online win-rate data from Smash Bros. Ultimate, gathered worldwide, put the highest fighter at 51.43% and the lowest at 47.18%. All 87 fighters fall in that narrow range in a smooth gradation, so any fighter can win. Win rates aren't everything, though, because "the true metric is how each fighter feels."

## Do this
- When a character is too strong, first try exaggerating its weaknesses instead of cutting its signature strengths.
- Check every balance proposal by asking whether it makes the character more average, and reject changes that wear away its identity.
- Give each character clear strengths and clear weaknesses, so that matchups between different characters create varied strategy.
- Check overall balance against real match data such as win rates (Smash Bros. Ultimate's 87 fighters fell between 47.18% and 51.43%), but keep how each character feels as the final measure.
- Expect strong-or-weak debates online, and remember that echo chambers make those opinions seem more certain than they are.

## Avoid
- Balancing purely on strength by trimming advantages and patching weaknesses until every character is average.
- Relying on identical or near-identical sides, as in Mario vs. Mario, to avoid balance problems.

## Examples from the episode
- **Smash Bros. — Mario vs. Mario:** perfectly balanced but limited in push and pull.
- **Super Smash Bros. Ultimate — online win rates:** a high of 51.43% and a low of 47.18% across all 87 fighters, in a smooth gradation.

## Quote
> Hence, I advise our team to keep fighters unique by amplifying shortcomings to offset advantages. (00:30)
