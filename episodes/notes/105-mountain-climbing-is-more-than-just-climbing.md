# 105 · Mountain Climbing Is More Than Just Climbing

**Category:** Game Essence · [Video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Be6km65CuuM) · [Transcript](../transcripts/105-mountain-climbing-is-more-than-just-climbing.md)

## In one sentence
Don't scale enemy strength in lockstep with the player's growth; shape progression like a mountain trail with peaks (bosses that demand a strategy and pay off) and flat stretches (quiet areas, places to rack up gains), so advancing feels like adventure rather than work.

## Key ideas
- In RPGs and other games with character growth, you have to balance player strength against tougher enemies and obstacles. Plotted on a graph, a straight diagonal line is "no good." Many games use a quadratic curve that rises toward the end, but that isn't his point either. His point is that player and enemy strength shouldn't stay in direct proportion.
- Think of mountain climbing. The same steady incline all the way would be a chore, especially if the scenery never changes or the only highlights are rest stations at regular intervals. "It's just work." Real climbs have peaks and valleys: challenges to overcome and flat plains to breeze through.
- The challenge peaks are usually boss fights. Bosses shouldn't just be stronger versions of regular enemies. A boss should force the player to form a strategy, and pulling it off should earn a nice reward.
- Plan flatter areas too. In games with a constant stream of enemies, that could be an unexpectedly empty space. Spots where players can rack up gains also work. These make exploring more fun.
- Games where you can progress in several directions at once make this balance very hard. If enemies get stronger along with the player, there's no incentive to get ahead. Whatever fix you choose should leave room for interesting strategies.

## Do this
- Plot player power and enemy difficulty across the whole game and reshape the curve into deliberate peaks and valleys instead of a straight line or steady ramp.
- Design each boss around a strategy the player has to figure out, not just bigger stats, and give a clear reward when they beat it.
- Mix in breathers: an unexpectedly empty area inside an enemy-dense zone, and spots where players can farm experience or resources.
- Vary the scenery along the route so the climb never feels like one unchanging slope.
- In open or multi-path games, don't scale enemies up with the player. Find balancing that keeps getting stronger worthwhile and leaves room for strategy.

## Avoid
- Keeping enemy strength in direct proportion to player strength.
- Bosses that are just beefed-up regular enemies.
- Level scaling that removes any reason to get ahead.
- Progression where the only highlights are rest stops at fixed intervals.

## Examples from the episode
- **Mountain climbing (analogy):** a constant incline with unchanging scenery is tedious; real trails have climbs and flat plains.
- **RPG growth curves (general):** straight-line and late-rising quadratic curves both miss the point if player and enemy strength stay proportional.

## Quote
> My point is, it's not a good idea to keep player and enemy strength in direct proportion. (00:25)
