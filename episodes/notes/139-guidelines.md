# 139 · Guidelines

**Category:** Grab Bag · [Video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HgvheCHsExA) · [Transcript](../transcripts/139-guidelines.md)

## In one sentence
Know and respect the guidelines your game must meet, since they often exist to give players consistent rules, but expect them to clash with your intent at times, and if you write guidelines yourself, leave room for leniency.

## Key ideas
- Most products, unless extremely basic, have guidelines that must be strictly observed. A product or service that doesn't follow them can't be released.
- **Tetris as an example:** If you keep rotating a block after it reaches a surface, you can delay it from locking down, and in some versions you can move it around forever. The Tetris Company's first guidelines, in 2002, addressed this. Older Tetris games had no unified control scheme or rotation rules, so every version felt a bit different, and "changing the rules with every new game makes it harder to play."
- The Tetris guidelines cover each Tetrimino's color and shape, the number of blocks in the Next preview, each block's rotation points, and how ghost pieces are displayed. They are the same worldwide, because guidelines exist to serve a purpose.
- **Kinds of guidelines a game faces:** age ratings, platforms, storefronts, online services and internal company rules. A creator may have a specific intent behind a visual or feature that the guidelines don't allow. If players don't realize the developer's hands were tied, they may walk away with a bad taste in their mouth.
- Exceptions exist. For Tetris arcade games, where profitability can be an issue, some exceptions are allowed.
- People still make the decisions, whether or not a guideline exists. Sometimes guidelines fit and sometimes they don't, and that's hard to quantify. Sakurai thinks guidelines should allow a little leniency, "because the best outcome is that we can make something good."

## Do this
- At the start of a project, list every guideline that applies (age rating, platform holder, storefront, online service, internal company and any licensor or franchise rules) and check planned visuals and features against them before you build.
- When working in a series or genre with standardized rules (e.g., piece colors, rotation points, preview count, ghost-piece display), keep those core rules and controls consistent instead of reinventing them each release.
- If you write guidelines for a franchise or team, state each rule's purpose and allow justified exceptions (as Tetris does for arcade profitability), so the rules don't block a better result.

## Avoid
- Changing a series' basic rules and controls with every new game. It makes the games harder to play.
- Applying guidelines with zero leniency when they clearly don't fit the case.

## Examples from the episode
- **Tetris — lock delay:** rotating a piece on a surface can delay lockdown, in some versions forever. This was addressed in The Tetris Company's 2002 guidelines.
- **Tetris guidelines:** fixed Tetrimino colors and shapes, the Next preview count, rotation points and ghost-piece display, all the same worldwide, with some exceptions for arcade versions.

## Quote
> I think if possible, guidelines should allow for a little leniency. Because the best outcome is that we can make something good. (02:30)
