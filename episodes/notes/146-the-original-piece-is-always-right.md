# 146 · The Original Piece is Always Right

**Category:** Audio · [Video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2iWqCSO98jU) · [Transcript](../transcripts/146-the-original-piece-is-always-right.md)

## In one sentence
When you arrange beloved game music, the original as players heard it in the game is the authority, including its quirks, its structure and even changes forced by the hardware, over what music theory says.

## Key ideas
- This episode continues the previous one on music supervision.
- **Athena (SNK arcade):** While a piece was being arranged, the revisions kept coming back as the exact opposite of Sakurai's feedback. He eventually realized there was a misunderstanding about what he called the "main melody." From his time with the game and the NES version, he heard "melody A" as the main melody. From a musical perspective, he was told it was "melody B."
- He hears that, but "the song you hear when playing the original game is what's right," no matter how far it strays from musical methods and conventions.
- The Athena piece has a curious section that hits the on-beats the first time and shifts to the off-beats the second time. Sakurai isn't sure why it was written that way, but Smash reproduces it faithfully.
- **Psycho Soldier (SNK, 1987):** Reportedly the first game in the world with real vocals in its background music. For Smash Ultimate, Yuzo Koshiro arranged it with vocals by Kanako Kotera, Sakurai sat in on the recording, and the team aimed for the feel of a 1980s Japanese pop song.
- **Structure follows the original's hardware:** Like the original, there are separate Japanese and English versions. The Japanese song has always had three verses and the English one only one. So the Japanese arrangement recreates three verses, while the English one goes first verse, instrumental, first verse again. The reason: in the original game, the music switches to the internal audio processor after the first verse.
- The Japanese and English versions even differ in intonation. The Japanese version's "Japanized English" has more distinct syllables, which may sound a little odd overseas.
- "The original piece is always right." Arrangers must take care not to detract from each player's own past experience with the piece.

## Do this
- Before briefing an arranger, decide which melody is "main" by what players heard and remember from playing the original (including ports they knew), and play that reference together so you both mean the same thing.
- When revisions keep coming back opposite to your notes, stop and check whether you and the musician define key terms like "main melody" the same way.
- Keep the original's oddities in the arrangement (e.g., a section that hits on-beats the first time and off-beats the second), even when they break convention.
- If the original's structure was shaped by hardware (e.g., vocals for only the first verse before the internal sound chip takes over), mirror it: put an instrumental where the original lost its vocals.
- When the original had separate language versions, keep them distinct, including verse counts and pronunciation or intonation.

## Avoid
- Letting music theory or convention override how the original actually sounded in play.
- "Correcting" quirks of the original in ways that detract from players' memories of it.

## Examples from the episode
- **Athena:** the "melody A" vs. "melody B" misunderstanding, and the on-beat/off-beat shift reproduced in Smash.
- **Psycho Soldier:** arranged by Yuzo Koshiro with vocals by Kanako Kotera. The English version runs first verse, instrumental, first verse because the original switched to the internal audio processor after verse one.
- **Smash Bros. Ultimate:** 1980s Japanese pop styling, and separate Japanese/English vocals with different intonation.

## Quote
> However you cut it, when arranging beloved songs, the original piece is always right. (06:20)
