# 214 · A World with Less Color

**Category:** Planning & Game Design · [Video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=obnjR8KJ9nk) · [Transcript](../transcripts/214-a-world-with-less-color.md)

## In one sentence
If gameplay depends on telling colors apart, check it through a color-blindness filter and separate similar hues by brightness, and check your art in black and white so backgrounds never outshine the foreground.

## Key ideas
- Meteos was the only one of Sakurai's games where color coding really mattered, so making it playable for color-blind players was essential before he could call it complete.
- If a game is built mainly around color and relies on color alone, color-blind players can't play it. In Meteos the blocks (the "Meteos") look different on each planet, so it wasn't as simple as checking single colors.
- He checked the game with a "color blindness filter" color plugin, which Photoshop now includes by default, and showed how the Meteos look with protanopia. Color blindness takes different forms, but certain types of colors generally look alike.
- For those colors he gave the pieces significantly different brightness. Making Meteos too dark would lose them against the background, and some designs needed follow-up work, but he felt the game needed that extra care.
- It takes real effort. The same problem affects other genres too, with power-ups or interactive stage objects that are hard to tell apart.
- A common developer trick is to check art and visuals in black and white, especially background art. It shows clearly which parts are light or dark and what stands out. Backgrounds shouldn't be more prominent than the foreground, and this check catches the problem early.
- It works much like flipping your artwork to find inconsistencies.

## Do this
- Run screenshots of every color-coded element (puzzle pieces, power-ups, interactive objects) through a color-blindness simulation filter. Photoshop includes one by default.
- Where hues merge under the filter, separate them by brightness and not just hue, without making them so dark that they disappear into the background.
- Don't let color be the only thing that tells gameplay-critical items apart.
- Convert background art and full screenshots to black and white, and tone down any background that stands out more than the foreground.
- Flip your artwork to check for inconsistencies.

## Avoid
- Building gameplay on color differences alone.
- Backgrounds that are more prominent than the foreground.

## Examples from the episode
- **Meteos:** the blocks were checked with a color-blindness filter (protanopia shown) and redesigned with big brightness differences, while staying visible against each planet's background.
- **Black-and-white checks of background art:** show which elements stand out before the problem spreads.

## Quote
> If your game is primarily built around color, solely relying on that will make it so that players with color blindness won't be able to play. (00:24)
