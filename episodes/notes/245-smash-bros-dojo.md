# 245 · Smash Bros. DOJO!!

**Category:** Marketing · [Video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=v4BG0wnc72s) · [Transcript](../transcripts/245-smash-bros-dojo.md)

## In one sentence
If people don't "get" your game, explain its essence to players directly and regularly, ideally in the words of the person who knows it best, because an unknown game might as well not exist.

## Key ideas
- Sakurai says the original Smash Bros. had deep game essence and push and pull from the start, but it was hard to get across. People just saw Nintendo characters beating each other up, and its rules, controls and feel were unlike earlier fighting games, so many dismissed it before release.
- In the long gap between going gold and hitting shelves, he wrote a document on the game's essence and unique features, "The Secrets of Smash Bros.", and sent it to many magazine publishers. It didn't seem to have much impact.
- So he made it public as a handbook for players: the "Smash Bros. DOJO!!" website, built by himself in an HTML editor. Connections were slow, pictures had to be small, and the tools and fonts were lacking. He needed Nintendo's permission, and nothing else like it existed. He doesn't know how effective it was, but word spread and the game became a massive hit.
- For Melee, the site returned with "sokuhou" ("news flash") in its name and posted screenshots and news during development. Melee's development was brutal, yet he updated the site himself, which is uncommon for a director: he knew the game best, and having the team do it would have meant briefing them on every post and reviewing it anyway. He also replied directly to comments, a form of player interaction before social media. Today, risk management may leave less freedom for that.
- For Brawl, the Dojo updated five times a week, one new detail every weekday, in six languages: Japanese, English, Spanish, French, German and Italian. It needed help from Nintendo of America and Nintendo of Europe. He still wrote the text; the team helped with tasks like screenshots. Updating every day mattered: people came to the site excited to see something new, which works well once it becomes a daily habit.
- It isn't always good for development, since the director is the busiest person on the team, so delegate if at all possible. Even then he spends time reviewing, because the director knows the content and its fun best.
- He sees development and marketing as "two wheels on one axle." Today many people scroll sites like Twitter, so always think about how best to get your information to them; his "Mr. Sakurai Presents" videos are another attempt at that.

## Do this
- If screenshots alone don't convey your game's appeal, write a plain document on its core essence and unique features, and publish it directly to players instead of relying only on the press.
- Run a fixed, frequent update schedule (Brawl's Dojo posted one new detail every weekday) so checking in becomes a habit for fans.
- Have the person who knows the game best write, or at least review, public explanations, and delegate production tasks like screenshots.
- If you want a global audience, plan from the start to localize updates (Brawl's Dojo ran in six languages with regional partners).
- Plan how each piece of news will reach people scrolling social feeds, and shape it for that format.

## Avoid
- Relying only on magazines or press coverage to explain a game whose appeal is unusual.
- Treating marketing as separate from development.

## Examples from the episode
- **Super Smash Bros. (original) — "The Secrets of Smash Bros." and the first Smash Bros. DOJO!!:** a handbook site Sakurai built himself to explain the game.
- **Smash Bros. Melee — Dojo with "sokuhou":** development screenshots and news, updated by Sakurai himself, with direct replies to comments.
- **Smash Bros. Brawl — Smash Bros. DOJO!!:** five updates a week in six languages.
- **Mr. Sakurai Presents:** a newer attempt at delivering information to players.

## Quote
> I see development and marketing as two wheels on one axle. No matter how good something is, an unknown game might as well not exist. (04:58)
