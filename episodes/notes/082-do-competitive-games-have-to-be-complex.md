# 082 · Do Competitive Games Have to Be Complex?

**Category:** Game Essence · [Video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1ggw9xXbzig) · [Transcript](../transcripts/082-do-competitive-games-have-to-be-complex.md)

## In one sentence
A competitive game needs enough mechanics to create push and pull that lasts hundreds of matches, but every rule must earn its place, so question your genre's "givens" at the start of each project.

## Key ideas
- Modern competitive games take courage to get into. They're complicated, and even watching, the rules can be hard to follow.
- Thought experiment: a fighting game where you can't walk and have only one punch has no push and pull, and mashing is boring. Add walking and you get range, but it's still not enough. Add a kick with longer recovery than the punch, so a missed kick can be countered, and push and pull appears. Jumping, guarding and throwing each add more depth.
- Mario Bros., seen as a competitive game, has fun core play, but it's hard to imagine playing it for hours.
- Early fighting games felt deep because they let players do more than other arcade games, which mostly used one button per action. The two-player Karate Champ (1984) already had over 20 moves. The original Street Fighter (1987) had many moves, but they were so hard to perform that it was hard to feel the push and pull. Street Fighter II set the genre ablaze with carefully designed push and pull and moves that each had a specific role.
- Not every game needs that much depth, and there's always room for casual fun. But a game meant to last through hundreds and hundreds of matches needs a certain level of complexity, and judging the right breadth and depth is hard.
- Genres with a long history of sequels can pick up complicated rules that add nothing to push and pull. Creators and players take familiar features for granted, so re-examine the mechanics and rules at the start of every project. Because he does this, every Super Smash Bros. has slightly different jump mechanics.

## Do this
- Build your competitive ruleset up from a minimal core (one move), adding one mechanic at a time and writing down what new push and pull each one creates. If a mechanic creates none, cut it.
- Give stronger moves longer recovery than weaker ones, so a whiffed strong move can be punished. Risk then has a cost.
- Give every move a specific role, and merge or remove moves that overlap.
- At project kickoff, list the conventions you inherited from the genre or your previous game (jump physics, guard rules, and so on) and decide for each whether it still earns its place.
- Decide whether the game is for casual sessions or hundreds of matches, and size its depth to match. Keep inputs easy enough that players can actually feel the push and pull.

## Avoid
- Complex rules that don't add push and pull, often leftovers from years of genre iteration.
- Moves so hard to perform that players can't feel the push and pull (as in the original Street Fighter).
- Treating your genre's "givens" as fixed.

## Examples from the episode
- **Hypothetical one-punch fighter:** no walking and one punch means no push and pull. Adding walking, a punishable kick, jumping, guarding and throwing builds depth step by step.
- **Mario Bros.:** fun core, but too shallow to play competitively for hours.
- **Karate Champ (1984, two-player version):** the first notable competitive arcade fighter, with over 20 moves.
- **Street Fighter (1987):** many moves, but too hard to perform to feel the push and pull.
- **Street Fighter II:** carefully designed push and pull, with each move playing a specific role.
- **Super Smash Bros. series:** jump mechanics differ slightly in every version because he re-examines them each time.

## Quote
> To some extent, there's no avoiding complexity in games— but it's best to always approach your "givens" as if they aren't givens at all. (03:36)
