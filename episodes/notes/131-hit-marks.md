# 131 · Hit Marks

**Category:** Effects · [Video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=B-P4ysHSjCg) · [Transcript](../transcripts/131-hit-marks.md)

## In one sentence
Build hit marks to a checklist (flashiness that matches the impact, visual balance, correct placement, layered elements, not hiding characters, fitting duration, and both bright and dark tones), and use hitstop to animate them in more detail.

## Key ideas
- "Hit marks" are the visual flair shown when an attack connects. Sakurai's games tend to use comparatively flashy, cartoony ones, which can look strange on realistic subjects, so he strikes a balance for each represented title. Other games use effects like blood spray instead. Guard effects count as hit marks too.
- 1) Flashiness should match impact. Their purpose is clear visual feedback and fun for the player, so flashy is generally best. 2) Visual balance: relying only on additive blending or weak contrast can leave the game feeling lacking.
- 3) Position must match the hit. A hit happens when attack and target hitboxes meet (damage collision); if this isn't checked correctly, the mark appears in a strange spot. Smash's pulled-back camera means large hitboxes, which makes placement tricky.
- 4) Use a variety of elements. Rather than one effect, Smash adds a shockwave behind the character and an upward spray, plus sparks for mechanical opponents. Avoid clutter, but well-timed color additions let effects work against many backgrounds. 5) Don't eclipse the characters. Lowering the hit mark's display priority can backfire, leaving you seeing nothing but terrain.
- 6) Duration should fit the hit. Effects for quick repeated hits should be short and others longer, but depending on the game, resolve even the "long" ones fairly quickly. 7) Contrasting brightness: marks should read on both light and dark backgrounds. All-bright effects aren't visible enough on light backgrounds, so include dark colors, such as a dark backdrop.
- In games that use hitstop to mark impacts, hit marks can usually keep animating during the hitstop, so use that time to pack in more detailed presentation.
- Bonus: in Smash Ultimate, if the player being hit inputs a direction during hitstop, an effect shows the flight path they'll take, since they can tweak that vector slightly. It's an effect that arose from the game's rules.

## Do this
- Scale each hit mark's size and flashiness to the attack's impact: small and quick for rapid repeated hits, bigger for strong hits.
- Spawn hit marks at the actual contact point where the attack and target hitboxes overlap, and check placement in-game, especially when hitboxes are large.
- Build each hit mark from layers: a main flash, a shockwave behind the target and an upward spray, plus material-specific extras such as sparks for mechanical targets.
- Pair bright additive elements with a dark element (for example, a dark backdrop) so the mark reads on both light and dark backgrounds.
- Keep effects short (shortest for rapid repeated hits, and resolve even big ones fairly quickly) and keep them from covering the characters. Check them against your lightest and darkest backgrounds.
- Let hit-mark animations keep playing during hitstop while the characters freeze, and use that window for extra detail.

## Avoid
- Purely additive or low-contrast effects that feel weak and vanish on bright backgrounds.
- Hit marks that cover the characters, or "fixing" that by lowering draw priority so terrain hides the effect.
- Cluttered effects, or cartoony marks used unchanged on realistic characters.

## Examples from the episode
- **Super Smash Bros. Ultimate — hit marks in slow motion:** many types, with shockwaves, upward sprays, sparks on mechanical opponents and dark backdrops for contrast.
- **Super Smash Bros. — camera:** pulled back, so hitboxes are large and placing effects is challenging.
- **Super Smash Bros. Ultimate — hitstop direction effect:** shows the flight path the hit player can slightly adjust during hitstop.
