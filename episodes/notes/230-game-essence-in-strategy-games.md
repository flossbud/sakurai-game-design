# 230 · Game Essence in Strategy Games

**Category:** Game Essence · [Video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3eG3expp5KM) · [Transcript](../transcripts/230-game-essence-in-strategy-games.md)

## In one sentence
In strategy games, start from symmetric fairness, then build push and pull through counters, costs, abilities and range, so the fun comes from out-configuring an evenly matched opponent.

## Key ideas
- Sakurai explains strategy-game essence in terms of combat power, and says the same thinking applies to MOBAs, card games and more.
- Two identical armies should generally draw, or the game isn't fair. In Rock, Paper, Scissors, identical teams draw. The core essence of strategy games is changing your setup to win decisively.
- Differences in power and abilities add depth: rocks that are cheaper so you can field more, paper with double attack power, scissors that can leap over others to attack.
- A counter cycle with clear strengths, weaknesses and fair resource costs makes the game richer. His example: infantry lose to tanks, tanks to helicopters, helicopters to anti-air cannons, and anti-air cannons to infantry.
- Random chances to dodge attacks are common, but a game will likely work without them. They keep players on their toes because the same battle can turn out differently, but they can feel unfair.
- In shogi and chess the push and pull comes from attack range, not unit strength. Games with map-based weapons or long-range attacks are likewise about taking the best positions, and the goal is still to beat an evenly matched opponent.
- The more players must think about and do, the deeper the strategy, but strengths and weaknesses must be allocated wisely. Video games are good at calculation, so each attack can have detailed properties. Explore unit abilities and interactions fully.

## Do this
- Test mirror matches: two identical armies should come out roughly even. Fix any built-in side advantage.
- Build a counter cycle in which each unit type beats one type and loses to another, and price each unit to match its strengths.
- Vary units on more than one axis: cost (more units), attack power, movement abilities (e.g., leaping over others) and attack range.
- Use range and position as a second source of push and pull, so taking key spots matters as much as raw power.
- Before adding random evasion, check whether the game works without it, and weigh the unfairness it adds against the uncertainty it brings.

## Avoid
- Randomness, like chance-to-dodge, that makes the same battle swing in ways players feel are unfair.
- Piling on complexity without allocating strengths and weaknesses wisely.

## Examples from the episode
- **Rock, Paper, Scissors — three-way deadlock:** identical teams draw; tweaks like cheaper rocks, double-power paper or leaping scissors add depth.
- **Infantry, tanks, helicopters, anti-air:** a four-way counter cycle.
- **Shogi and chess:** push and pull from attack range and position rather than unit strength.

## Quote
> Changing your configuration to achieve a crushing victory is the fundamental game essence found in strategy games. (00:43)
