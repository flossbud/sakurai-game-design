# 224 · All to Destroy One Wall

**Category:** Graphics · [Video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rV1Vnt1dVlQ) · [Transcript](../transcripts/224-all-to-destroy-one-wall.md)

## In one sentence
When supervising art, give precise, drawn feedback until an asset clearly reads as what it should be, and when repeated rounds still fall short, accept it and move on.

## Key ideas
- Of all Smash Bros. Ultimate's stages, the Great Fox and the Mishima Dojo's destructible wall were the hardest to supervise. The wall was a small issue but a real struggle. Sakurai gave feedback with retouched versions of the team's images.
- Round one notes. The cut faces looked sawn, so he asked the team to avoid perpendicular cuts and give the fronts and backs of the boards jagged edges. The boards twisted outward like metal, so he asked them to keep the X-axis flat and put jagged edges only on the Y and Z axes. The jagged edges, and from afar the three remaining beams, were too evenly spaced, so he asked for more irregular spacing.
- The fixes created new problems. Edges were rounded like stalactites or too similar to each other, the broken section looked square from far away, and one pattern looked copy-pasted. Splinters hung down into the space fighters move through, so he had them raised.
- The real goal was simple: it had to look like wooden boards had actually been broken. If the team made something different from what he pictured but the quality was there, he approved it.
- When repeated feedback still doesn't get there, he accepts it and puts it in the game as-is. His goal isn't to make everything match his ideals, so he makes this call "surprisingly often", though sometimes with regret.
- Supervision often brings a half-step of progress when you hoped for a whole step. Asking for big changes and then pulling them back can be the easiest way to converge, but it can feel like the direction changes every round. The director and the artist share responsibility for the result.

## Do this
- Give art feedback as paint-overs or retouched images, not only written notes.
- Make feedback concrete: name the axis ("keep X flat, jag only Y and Z"), the pattern ("break up the even spacing") and the repetition ("don't copy-paste the same splinter shape").
- Review assets both up close and from the gameplay camera distance. Even spacing and square outlines often only show from far away.
- Check that decorative geometry like splinters and debris stays out of the space characters move through.
- Write the asset's core goal in one sentence ("it must look like broken wood") and approve anything that meets it, even if it differs from your mental image.
- Set a stopping point: if several rounds don't reach the bar, ship it as-is and spend your time elsewhere.

## Avoid
- Evenly spaced, copy-pasted or perfectly straight breaks that look manufactured instead of broken.
- Trying to make every asset conform to the director's personal ideal.
- Swinging the direction back and forth with each round of feedback.

## Examples from the episode
- **Smash Bros. Ultimate — Mishima Dojo destructible wall:** several rounds of retouched feedback (sawn-looking cuts, metal-like twisting, stalactite edges, copy-pasted patterns, splinters in the fighters' path) until it looked like broken boards.
- **Smash Bros. Ultimate — Great Fox stage:** the other hardest stage to supervise, a much larger job.

## Quote
> My goal isn't to make everything in the game conform to my ideals, so I make these decisions surprisingly often. (03:17)
