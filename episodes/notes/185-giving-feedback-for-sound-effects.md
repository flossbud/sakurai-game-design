# 185 · Giving Feedback for Sound Effects

**Category:** Audio · [Video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=B9CokyQ2Tes) · [Transcript](../transcripts/185-giving-feedback-for-sound-effects.md)

## In one sentence
Sound is hard to describe, so give sound designers concrete descriptions of a sound's role, comparisons everyone knows and links to real examples, then approve whatever fits the game even if it isn't what you first imagined.

## Key ideas
- Sometimes the sound team delivers effects that differ from what Sakurai had in mind or that don't make the game feel enjoyable. Asking for changes is extremely hard, because audio has no physical form and you can't really approximate it with your voice.
- He uses concrete phrases like "strong attack". Comparisons also work if they're to something everyone knows, such as "make it sound like a jet engine".
- Not everyone knows every specific sound, even a real one, so don't assume they'll understand; often they won't. Sometimes he includes a YouTube link to a sound similar to what he wants.
- Especially with remote work, he tries not to use onomatopoeia. An email asking for more "clang" or "boom" tells people you had something in mind, but the idea doesn't come across in text.
- His sound lead disagrees a little: "Every now and then, onomatopoeia can be helpful." It can fill in the gap between a sound and an emotion, and the team wants as many details and clues as possible, even if that goes beyond reasoning. The sound team says they build effects by trying to work out the sounds playing in his head, which is extra work for them.
- As director, he's fine with sounds that don't exactly match his idea as long as they fit the game, and he approves things quite different from what he imagined. Sometimes they sound even better.
- Supervising sound is tough because it's so imprecise, but it has to be done: sounds directly convey feedback and feeling to the player, so they should land as close to the goal as possible. That depends on having a clear idea of what you want from the very start.

## Do this
- Describe what the sound has to do in concrete terms, such as "this is a strong attack", rather than with vague adjectives.
- Compare only to sounds everyone knows ("like a jet engine"), and for anything more specific, attach a link to a real example, such as a YouTube clip, instead of assuming people know it.
- In written feedback, don't rely on onomatopoeia alone ("more clang"). Add it only as an extra clue next to a concrete description, since it can help connect a sound to an emotion.
- Judge delivered sounds by whether they fit the game, and approve good ones even when they differ from what you imagined.
- Before requesting a sound, decide exactly what feedback and feeling it should give the player.

## Avoid
- Assuming everyone knows the specific real-world sound you're thinking of.
- Emails that only ask for more "clang" or "boom".
- Rejecting a sound only because it doesn't match the one in your head.
- Starting sound requests without a clear idea of what you want.

## Examples from the episode
- **"Strong attack":** a concrete phrase Sakurai uses in sound requests.
- **"Make it sound like a jet engine":** a comparison that works because nearly everyone knows the sound.
- **YouTube links:** shared to point to a similar sound.
- **His sound lead's advice:** onomatopoeia can sometimes help bridge sound and emotion.
