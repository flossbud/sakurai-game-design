# 086 · Game Essence in Falling-Block Puzzle Games

**Category:** Game Essence · [Video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QOkY6BULsgo) · [Transcript](../transcripts/086-game-essence-in-falling-block-puzzle-games.md)

## In one sentence
Understand *why* a genre's core action is fun (its risk and reward) instead of copying the action itself. Then you can come up with ideas quickly, even in genres you're bad at.

## Key ideas
- Jigsaw-style puzzles have no push and pull. Their fun comes from the achievement of finishing them.
- Tetris grew out of Pentomino, where you fit 12 unique pieces into a rectangle. Like a jigsaw, Pentomino has no game essence, while Tetris has plenty. The difference is risk and reward.
- In falling-block puzzle games the risk is the blocks piling up: the higher the stack, the less time you have to think. Lining up and clearing blocks lowers the risk.
- Clearing many blocks at once requires putting yourself at great risk, which makes the payoff feel even better. Tetrominos gradually falling faster also raises the risk and makes the game more exciting.
- Later ports let you rotate and slide pieces after they land. He calls it a significant addition, because being able to cling to life invites even greater risk.
- You could give Pentomino game essence by making it competitive, but placing pieces takes trial and error, and the rules would have to account for that.
- Falling-block puzzlers are simple and intuitive, so their game essence is easy to see.
- Don't start designing with "blocks have to disappear." Ask why making blocks disappear is fun. The core Meteos idea of launching blocks took him only five minutes, because he already understood the genre's essence.

## Do this
- For your genre, write down three things: what the risk is, what lowers it, and what the biggest reward requires (for example "taller stack = more risk; a multi-line clear needs a tall stack").
- Make the biggest rewards available only to players who take the biggest risks.
- Raise the risk over time, as Tetris speeds up its falling pieces.
- Add "last-chance" mechanics (like moving or rotating a piece after it lands) that let skilled players survive at the edge, which encourages risk-taking.
- Before choosing the player's core action, answer "why is this kind of action fun?" and brainstorm other actions that would deliver the same payoff.

## Avoid
- Starting design from the genre's default action ("blocks have to disappear") instead of from why it's fun.

## Examples from the episode
- **Jigsaw puzzles / Pentomino:** satisfying to complete but no push and pull, so no game essence.
- **Tetris:** stack height is risk, multi-line clears reward risk-taking, and rising speed increases tension.
- **Later Tetris ports:** rotating and sliding pieces after landing lets players cling to life and take bigger risks.
- **Meteos:** the core launching idea took five minutes because he understood the genre's essence.

## Quote
> You shouldn't start your design with the idea that blocks have to disappear, but instead consider why it's fun to make those blocks disappear. (01:55)
