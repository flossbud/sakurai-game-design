# 164 · Making Sequels

**Category:** Planning & Game Design · [Video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=plPR6-Gqemc) · [Transcript](../transcripts/164-making-sequels.md)

## In one sentence
Use a sequel's head start (an existing system and tools) to expand the game, but make it clearly more fun and impactful than the one before, because a sequel that just repeats the original can expect about half the sales.

## Key ideas
- Popular works get sequels, but if you make the same thing again, expect sales to be roughly halved. That's especially tough for works that were on the unique side.
- The number one reason games get sequels is that the underlying systems already exist. A film sequel means making almost entirely new content, just like the first film. A game needs its underlying system, a consistent structure and its dev tools before real content can be made, which takes a lot of work. A sequel lets you improve that system and then either expand it or get a big head start on content.
- Unlike film, you can't read a plot at the start and know how a game should turn out. You often only discover what's fun once it's finished. Sequels have advantages beyond marketing, and games unlike anything seen before are more at risk of cancellation; far more titles than you'd think never see the light of day.
- Smash Bros. rebuilt its game system every time, even as a sequel, because the dev team changed with each game. Smash for 3DS/Wii U and Ultimate shared a team, so they could use a similar dev environment.
- Why sequels sell less: people buy the first game because it looks fun, so how well it was made drives the sequel's sales. If people had a bad time with one entry, they may skip the next. If the sequel is the same scale with similar content, fans may feel they've already experienced it. The most vital thing is convincing players it's more fun and impactful than its predecessor. It won't sell much if it isn't well made, so give it everything you've got.
- Sakurai has no strong preference between sequels and originals and sees meaning and value in both. Which he makes depends on what the people offering him work want. What matters is doing each assignment to the best of his ability.

## Do this
- At sequel kickoff, list what the existing system and tools already give you, and decide explicitly whether to spend that head start on expanding the system or on more content.
- Write down, in one line, how the sequel is more fun and more impactful than the last game, and make sure the reveal shows that.
- Compare scale and content against the predecessor. If a returning player would feel they've "already experienced it", expand or change it.
- Treat the first game's quality as the sequel's marketing. A bad experience with one entry makes people skip the next.

## Avoid
- Remaking the same game at the same scale and expecting the same sales.
- Assuming a sequel will sell on its name alone if it isn't well made.

## Examples from the episode
- **Super Smash Bros. series — rebuilt systems:** each game rebuilt its system because the team changed, until Smash for 3DS/Wii U and Ultimate shared a team and dev environment.

## Quote
> But above all, the most vital thing is convincing players that it's more fun and impactful than its predecessor. (02:41)
