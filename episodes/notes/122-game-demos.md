# 122 · Game Demos

**Category:** Marketing · [Video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=PfNtg0tSGDY) · [Transcript](../transcripts/122-game-demos.md)

## In one sentence
A demo is one of the best ways to get a game noticed, but plan it like a small separate game: strip out spoiler data, maintain and test a second build, and only ship it once it is balanced.

## Key ideas
- Most games go unnoticed, and publicity alone usually isn't enough to draw people in. Demos are very effective at getting people to try a game.
- Making a demo is far more work than players think. It isn't just carving a piece off the full game; if you're not careful it becomes almost another small game.
- A demo released before the full game must not leak. People can and likely will find spoiler data left in the build, so it has to be removed entirely. That's manageable at the file level; otherwise just knowing which files hold what is a real challenge. Removing data also greatly raises the chance of something breaking.
- From the moment you decide on a demo, you maintain two versions internally: separate management, updates, tuning and QA. Anything released to the public needs nearly as much testing as the final product.
- A pre-release demo may not be fully balanced yet. A half-baked demo raises the risk that people write the game off, and negative word of mouth can turn players away, so "something is better than nothing" doesn't hold.
- Releasing a demo after launch, built from the finished and balanced game, is easier but may not make as much of a splash.
- Pre-COVID expo demos were somewhat easier because players couldn't keep them. Downloadable demos are common now, which may make them more effective than ever, even as bigger, more complex games make them harder to build. Whether to make one is up to publisher and developer policy, but it's better to be known than unknown.

## Do this
- Decide early whether you'll ship a demo and schedule it as its own deliverable, with its own tuning, updates and a near-full QA pass.
- Organize your data so spoiler content lives in separable files, which lets you strip it from a demo build cleanly.
- Before release, check the demo build for leftover spoiler data, then retest thoroughly, since removing data tends to break things.
- Only release a pre-launch demo once its content is balanced enough to represent the final game. If it can't be, consider a post-launch demo and accept the smaller splash.

## Avoid
- Treating a demo as a quick slice of the full game.
- Leaving spoiler data in a pre-release demo build.
- Releasing an unbalanced, half-baked demo that creates negative word of mouth.

## Examples from the episode
- None tied to a specific game; he contrasts pre-COVID expo demos (players couldn't keep them) with today's downloadable demos.
