# 128 · Is Your Game Appealing Enough to Start Over?

**Category:** Game Essence · [Video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=atHnu2fKtZA) · [Transcript](../transcripts/128-is-your-game-appealing-enough-to-start-over.md)

## In one sentence
If failure sends players back to the start, your game must keep showing them something they want to see next, so plan what stuck players will face on every retry instead of copying hard-game systems and hoping they persist.

## Key ideas
- Since the late 2010s, partly because of Demon's Souls and Dark Souls, more and more difficult games have come out, feeding each other's trend, especially games with permadeath or similar "start over when you fail" mechanics. Roguelikes and roguelites build whole genres on this.
- Definitions, kept simple: a roguelike has random dungeons and items, makes you start over when you die, and has allies and enemies taking turns (from the early PC game Rogue; Mystery Dungeon is a famous Japanese example). A roguelite borrows only some of Rogue's elements, usually action games with brisk action and presentation where randomized equipment, items and events make each run after a defeat play differently. The distinction only arose with the word "roguelite," so calling them all roguelikes does no harm.
- Starting over fits indie games well: games already involve repeating similar activities, and this mechanic needs fewer unique assets.
- But most players won't enjoy repeating the same content forever. They need motivation, usually wanting to see what comes next, and to create that desire you have to show them something to look forward to. Without appeal or enough variety, players won't keep repeating the same gameplay.
- So consider difficulty carefully. If more players than expected hit a wall, what scenery will they be looking at? Possibly the same things again and again.
- In Dark Souls, players push on out of a strong desire to reach what's next, and for some that rests on trust in FromSoftware or Mr. Miyazaki. Don't expect players to stick with a game that mimics those systems without creating the same pull. What keeps them going depends on the game's volume, how its story unfolds and how fleshed out it is, not on other games being hard or on you being able to scrape by in your own game.
- Sakurai often quits these games early: his time is valuable, and he'd rather play many games for fresh stimulus. Creators should think hard about each game's appeal, and about how players will feel replaying the same area over and over.

## Do this
- For each spot where many players are likely to fail and restart, write down what they'll see on the way back. Then add variety (randomized equipment, items, events) or shorten the replayed stretch so it isn't the same scenery every time.
- Make upcoming content visible or hinted (the next area, story beat or reward) so "what comes next" pulls players into another attempt.
- Tune difficulty by watching testers other than the developers, and plan for more players than expected hitting a wall.
- If you borrow permadeath or Souls-like systems, write down what your own game offers as the reason to push on; don't lean on the genre's reputation or another studio's trust.

## Avoid
- Copying hard-game systems without creating a desire to see what comes next.
- Assuming players will persist because other games are hard, or because you can scrape by in your own game.
- Low variety in the content players are forced to repeat.

## Examples from the episode
- **Demon's Souls / Dark Souls:** drove the trend of difficult games; players push on from a desire to reach what's next, partly built on trust in FromSoftware and Mr. Miyazaki.
- **Rogue:** the early PC game that gave roguelikes their name.
- **Mystery Dungeon series:** a famous roguelike in Japan.

## Quote
> In other words, the game itself needs to be appealing enough to make players want to push on. (02:37)
