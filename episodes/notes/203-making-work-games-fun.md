# 203 · Making "Work" Games Fun

**Category:** Game Essence · [Video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4SFOO23m-bI) · [Transcript](../transcripts/203-making-work-games-fun.md)

## In one sentence
A game with little push-and-pull can still hook players if it simulates real-world work, runs a work-earn-upgrade loop and keeps the ending out of sight.

## Key ideas
- Sakurai describes game essence as "push and pull" plus "risk and reward," but he admits that doesn't explain all fun. Some games feel fun with almost none of it.
- His examples: Car Mechanic Simulator (no risks or penalties, yet you keep working at it), Cookie Clicker (exponentially growing cookie output and a strange world), House Flipper (cleaning up a room feels great) and PowerWash Simulator ("That's all there is to it," yet getting the job done feels great).
- These games share four traits. They draw on something from the real world, you profit from the work, you tend to get sucked in, and they can feel like a waste of time.
- **Real-world subject:** the player's mind fills in extra information. The same work feels different depending on whether you are remodeling or cleaning, and working toward the result you want keeps players doing the work.
- **Profit:** the loop is work, earn money, then buy tools and skills that make the work easier. Without this loop a game about work "wouldn't have nearly as much staying power." He calls it "the first element you should be considering."
- **Getting sucked in:** this grows out of the first two traits, because people are made to enjoy work and simple tasks. If the game has no clear ending, put the end so far away that players can't see where they are heading, as Cookie Clicker does.
- **Feeling like a waste of time:** these games have no ultimate goal or concrete ending, yet they are hard to put down. They are "extremely 'game-like'" even without push-and-pull.

## Do this
- Base a task-driven game on real-world work (repairing, renovating, cleaning) so players' imaginations fill in the meaning and the result they picture motivates them.
- Design the loop first: work earns money, and money buys tools and skills that make the next job easier to finish.
- Balance the three parts (working, profiting, growing stronger) so that each purchase visibly makes the work easier.
- If your game has no real ending, place the goal so far away that players can't see it, for example with production that grows exponentially, as in Cookie Clicker.
- Use presentation that puts players in the mood to role-play the job.

## Avoid
- Shipping a work simulation without the earn-and-upgrade loop. Sakurai says it won't have staying power.

## Examples from the episode
- **Car Mechanic Simulator:** no risks or penalties, yet sprucing up a car keeps you working.
- **Cookie Clicker:** exponential cookie production, a strange world and an ending far out of sight.
- **House Flipper:** cleaning and renovating feels great, and a house can start to feel like your own.
- **PowerWash Simulator:** pressure-washing cars, houses and parks is the whole game, yet it is satisfying.

## Quote
> To find the fun in these games, it's crucial to strike a balance between working, profiting, and growing stronger (02:51)
