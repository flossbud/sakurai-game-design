# 020 · Kirby Super Star

**Category:** Game Concepts · [Video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=PkeETXCSn5M) · [Transcript](../transcripts/020-kirby-super-star.md)

## In one sentence
Meet hard requests with structural ideas, as Kirby Super Star made co-op work by splitting players into a lead and a Helper and offered lots of content without wearing players out by packaging short, distinct scenarios as an "omnibus."

## Key ideas
- Background: the game came out on the Super NES in 1996. While the SNES dev environment was being set up after Kirby's Adventure, he moved from computers like the PC-9801 to a Macintosh with a "windowing system." The game rests on two concepts: two-player simultaneous play and an "omnibus" format.
- Two-player play: Shigeru Miyamoto asked if a two-player Kirby was possible. He had wanted it for Mario, but the fast pace didn't allow it, and Kirby "moves slower than Mario." In side-scrollers, players tend to pull each other along as the screen scrolls. Confined belt-scrolling beat 'em ups manage it, but co-op is hard when players jump over obstacles. Copy abilities moved at drastically different speeds, which made it maybe even harder than Mario, unless the hardware could zoom in and out.
- His answer was the "Helper" system, which splits players into lead and supporting roles. The camera follows the lead, and the system allows the supporter to be off-camera. A supporter too far off-screen "Space Jumps" back. "Instead of letting both characters scroll the screen with equal weight, I placed the focus on only the lead character."
- He combined Helpers with the copy ability so enemies could become playable characters, adding both fun and personality ("two birds with one stone"). It was tough to build, since nearly every copy ability needed its own traits and parameters. Copy hats began as just an item to create Helpers, but they dramatically boosted Kirby's appeal because his simple design suits the added flair. They later caused "many headaches" during Smash Bros. development.
- Copy abilities went from one move each (in Kirby's Adventure) to many, using repeat presses, directional inputs and even command techniques. To compensate, standard enemies could survive a few hits instead of one, so stronger moves could set abilities apart, which became essential to later Kirby games. The health bar changed from a 6-segment gauge to something more analog.
- The omnibus format: scenarios are pinned to a cork board, each a different game with its own theme, such as a racing game about eating food and a more serious story-driven one. He chose it because "we were seeing too many games that took too long to beat." Short, distinct scenarios could deliver lots of content "without causing players to grow tired of any one style."
  - The first scenario, Spring Breeze, wasn't meant to have the copy ability. He added it reluctantly at a Nintendo producer's request and still wishes it could be played without it, at least the first time, since "Nothing really sets it apart from the next scenario."
  - A planned scenario, "Kagero Mansion," had Kirby's mouth sealed shut, forcing a survival adventure with items based on copy abilities. It was cut for schedule and ROM space.
- Mid-development, the team switched to CG backgrounds, following games like Donkey Kong Country. He doesn't think it turned out especially well: the staff weren't familiar with it, and it didn't suit Kirby's pieced-together terrain. Still, "it's important to learn new skills. The things you make today can lead to what you make tomorrow." (He directed only three side-scrolling Kirby games: Kirby's Dream Land, Kirby's Adventure and this one.)

## Do this
- For local co-op in a scrolling game, make one player the lead whom the camera follows, and warp any partner who drifts too far off-screen back to the lead.
- When you give characters or abilities more moves (repeat presses, directional inputs, commands), make enemies tougher so the difference in move strength shows.
- To offer lots of content without fatigue, split the game into short, self-contained modes, each with its own rules and theme, chosen from a hub screen.
- Keep each mode's identity distinct. If two modes end up playing the same, cut one or change one.
- Before adopting an unfamiliar technique mid-project (like pre-rendered CG backgrounds), check that your team knows it and that it fits how your levels are built.
- Look for ways a functional addition can also add character appeal, as the Helper item turned into copy hats.

## Avoid
- Letting all co-op players scroll the screen with equal weight in a jump-heavy side-scroller.
- Adopting a trendy art technique mid-development that the staff don't know and that doesn't fit how the game is built.
- Making a game long just because big games are the trend.
