# 109 · Don't Rely on a "Plan B"

**Category:** Work Ethic · [Video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QgkLrqGrwy4) · [Transcript](../transcripts/109-dont-rely-on-a-plan-b.md)

## In one sentence
In proposals and design docs, commit to the one spec you think is best instead of listing alternatives, and course-correct as a team if problems come up.

## Key ideas
- When writing a proposal or design doc, you'll often be unsure which spec is right. Which is easier to program? Which will feel more fun to the most people? It's tempting to approve both and leave the decision to someone else, which is why so many docs say "or we could do this" and leave both doors open.
- Especially in proposals, drop Plan B entirely. Leave out the hypotheticals and secondary ideas, look at the whole picture, and choose the method you think will work best. Further suggestions can come later.
- Always start from an ideal spec, then course-correct from there to maximize cost-effectiveness. That's more than good enough.
- To do this well, take in plenty of suggestions from the team with an open mind. The writer starts with the ideal, and when problems come up the team deals with them together. You might end up back at your Plan B, but in the proposal phase you should stand by your idea and show you've thought it through. That also builds experience.
- Directors are often expected to think about programming and dev efficiency too. Even so, the proposal is the skeleton of the game: "Don't twist it out of shape."

## Do this
- Before sending a proposal or spec, delete every "or we could do X" alternative and keep only the option you judge best overall.
- Weigh implementation ease and appeal yourself while writing, rather than handing the choice to the reader.
- Write the ideal version first. Trim for cost only once the team surfaces concrete problems.
- After circulating the proposal, actively ask for team feedback and resolve issues together, falling back to an alternative only if real problems justify it.

## Avoid
- Leaving both doors open in a proposal and pushing the decision onto someone else.
- Bending the core of the proposal out of shape up front for the sake of dev efficiency.
