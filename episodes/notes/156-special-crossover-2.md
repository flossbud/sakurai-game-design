# 156 · Special Crossover! #2

**Category:** Special · [Video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ll01fYUI_iQ) · [Transcript](../transcripts/156-special-crossover-2.md)

## In one sentence
Play the classics that moved the medium forward and pin down the specific techniques that made each one feel ahead of its time (shading, named enemies, enemy tables the player can push back, weapon recoil, smooth frame rates) so you can reuse them on purpose, and watch out for the costs that came with them: punishing power-up loss, longer sessions and display lag.

## Key ideas
- Sakurai and Arino Kacho (*Retro Game Master*) keep working through arcade games from about 1982 to 1985 as versus and co-op challenges. Front Line was "around standard for the era" graphically. Then Xevious (copyright 1982, released 1983) leapt ahead: its enemies were drawn in grayscale (a white-to-black gradation), it had a proper story, and every enemy had an in-world name (a "Torkan") where Galaxian only had a "Purple Alien" or "Red Alien".
- Xevious has no built-in AI that reacts to skill. It uses predefined enemy tables that advance as you play. Destroying the enemy that looks like a radar station sends you back to an earlier point in the table and makes the game easier, so the player has a hidden lever on difficulty.
- Sakurai praises Star Force's "5-frame explosion" (which he has covered on his channel) and its recoil animation: every time you fire missiles, the left and right cannons pull back "bam, bam, bam". Arino had never noticed it. Small details like this add force to firing.
- Frame rate and display lag carry the feel. The NES Ghosts 'n Goblins was a faithful port, with everything in the same place and the fun parts still fun, but its low frame rate meant it didn't feel "this smooth or this creepy". Halfway through the session Sakurai swaps to the low-lag monitor from his "Mr. Sakurai Presents" recordings, because modern TVs do so much image processing, and more post-processing means more lag.
- Controls: Pac-Land (1984, before Super Mario Bros.) originally made you press A to run right and mash B to run left. Sakurai used Arcade Archives' remapping to change it to D-pad movement and A to jump, and Arino says movement became easy. Karate Champ's two-stick controls need the sticks held until the move comes out, and the game opens with a training session that teaches them.
- Power-ups and session length: in Gradius one Speed Up is usually enough and two "feels right". Go too fast and you crash into the terrain. Losing a life resets every power-up, which the players say makes you want to quit. Sessions also got much longer than Galaxian's, and as content grew more complex, arcade games made less money.
- The hosts contrast Japanese games (cute, colorful) with overseas games that still used line graphics and aimed for more realistic movement. One example is Major Havoc, which Sakurai calls an inspiration for *The Quest of Ki*: you explore inside a spaceship, you die if your oxygen runs out, and enemies are removed from play once defeated.

## Do this
- Give players an indirect lever on difficulty. Drive enemy waves from an ordered table that advances as they play well, and add one recognizable target that rolls the table back to an easier point when it's destroyed.
- Add recoil to weapons. Pull the gun or cannon back for a few frames on every shot, and draw explosions as short, deliberate frame sequences (Star Force uses 5 frames).
- Playtest and record on a low-latency display with TV image processing turned off. If timing feels wrong, rule out display lag before you retune the game.
- When porting or cutting back for weaker hardware, protect the frame rate. A port can keep every enemy in place and still lose its mood if it runs choppy.
- Offer remappable controls for awkward schemes, such as replacing button-mashing to run with stick or D-pad movement. Open complex two-stick or command controls with a short guided training session.
- Give enemies in-world names and some story context, even in an arcade-style game, so the world feels authored.

## Avoid
- Wiping every power-up on death in a game built around big power-ups. Players lose the will to keep going.
- Letting display post-processing add input lag during playtests or footage capture.

## Examples from the episode
- **Xevious — grayscale art, named enemies, enemy tables:** grayscale shading gave far better graphical expression than other games of the time, and destroying the radar-station enemy rewinds the enemy table to an easier point.
- **Star Force — explosion and recoil:** a beautiful 5-frame explosion, and side cannons that pull back each time you fire missiles. For the boss Larios, Sakurai says you "really have to press the attack" instead of just dodging.
- **Pac-Land (Arcade Archives) — remapped controls:** the original scheme (press A to run right, mash B to run left) became D-pad movement and A to jump. The hosts also note the background scrolling slower than the foreground and the "true, proper animation".
- **Gradius — Speed Ups and power-up loss:** one or two Speed Ups is right, and too many make you crash into the terrain. Losing a life loses everything.
- **Ghosts 'n Goblins — arcade vs. NES:** the NES port's low frame rate cost smoothness and creepiness. Against the Red Arremer, don't let him take to the skies: beat him on the ground, or wait for him to land or charge.
- **Karate Champ — two-stick input:** hold both sticks until the move comes out. A match is two points (half or full points). Some say the follow-up *Player vs. Player* was the first competitive fighting game.
- **Mario Bros. (arcade) — co-op:** many more green fireballs than the NES version (two at once), and the Slipice enemy. Its NES counterpart, the Freezie, was a little thinner, roughly 8x16 pixels.
