# 087 · Mastering Up

**Category:** Grab Bag · [Video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=y2Webks5iTs) · [Transcript](../transcripts/087-mastering-up.md)

## In one sentence
Finishing a game ("mastering up") often feels more bruising than triumphant, so thank the staff who got through it with you, and approach other people's games with gratitude for the same struggle.

## Key ideas
- After years of work the game is finally finished. (The subtitle explaining the term "mastering up" is garbled in the OCR transcript.)
- Mastering up isn't always a moment of closure. Getting there usually meant tough decisions and cutting content, and with online updates you may keep chipping away at issues that slipped into the final version.
- It may be more common to feel a bit wounded at this point, and he says that was probably true for him too. It makes you want to show appreciation for the staff who came through that hardship with you.
- In the NES and Game Boy days the internet wasn't widespread, so finished games were delivered by hand. The ROM was finished in Tokyo, and someone rode the bullet train clutching it to Nintendo's headquarters in Kyoto or its Uji Plant, carrying company secrets across the country. This was before (or just as) the faster Nozomi train started running.
- With physical ROMs and no online updates, development ended there, and even with an overseas version to make, the end was clearly defined. Today online updates take away both the relief and the closure of mastering up.
- Still, after a long, hard path it's nice to have a completed product. It reminds him how many other developers go through the same struggles, so he tries to approach the games he plays with gratitude.

## Do this
- At master-up, explicitly recognize and thank the staff who made it to the end, whatever state the team is in.
- Plan time and people for post-release updates, since issues that slipped into the final version may need fixing after master-up.
- When you play (or critique) other developers' games, remember the cuts and struggles that went into finishing them.
