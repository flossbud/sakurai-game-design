# 107 · Voice Recording

**Category:** Audio · [Video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=C3ANiCI1Em4) · [Transcript](../transcripts/107-voice-recording.md)

## In one sentence
Voice sessions are short but their results live in the game forever, so script every vocalization, bring reference audio, keep in-battle lines short and non-competing, and budget plenty of editing time afterward.

## Key ideas
- One Smash fighter takes several people at least a year, with tuning sometimes running right up to release. Recording that fighter's voice takes about one hour, and no more than two even with reveal or story-cutscene lines. Those voices go straight into the game, and players really feel them.
- **Casting:** many characters already have established actors. The team uses the original game's actor when possible, but if a series has recast a character, they sometimes go with the new actor.
- **Scripts:** game documents are usually written horizontally, but Japanese voice scripts are vertical and read right to left. Every "ha" and "hmph" has to be in the script or it won't get recorded. That means basics (attacks, jumps, damage reactions) plus unique lines (special moves, Final Smashes, fighter-reveal dialogue). Lines that come from the original game get reference audio taken from that game.
- **Restraint:** Smash lines are short and used sparingly, because longer lines or constant battle chatter draw too much attention and break immersion. Voices shouldn't compete either, so feral-sounding characters like Bowser and Donkey Kong get extra thought about how their voices overlap.
- **Sessions:** after coordinating agencies and schedules, the team books a studio. Bandai Namco Studios had its own facility for Smash for 3DS/Wii U and Ultimate, but other projects often have to go elsewhere. Original rights holders often attend. Actors are briefed on their character and eased back into the voice before going into the booth. Sakurai usually directs over the "talkback" button, though a series' own sound director sometimes takes the lead. Overseas voices, led by NOA and NOE, are recorded based on the Japanese takes. Pokémon take extra work because they say different things in every language.
- **After recording:** this is when things get busiest, as voices are edited over and over to suit the game. For Kid Icarus: Uprising he tried to record cast members together to capture their banter, with some solo sessions when schedules didn't line up.
- You hear these lines constantly through development, and some players hear them even more, so writing and directing dialogue carries a lot of responsibility.

## Do this
- Write a complete recording script listing every vocalization down to each grunt ("ha," "hmph"), covering the basics (attacks, jumps, damage reactions) and the unique lines (special moves, super moves, reveal lines). Anything missing won't get recorded.
- For characters from existing games, prepare reference audio taken from the original game, and prefer the original or current series actor.
- Keep combat voice lines short and infrequent, and check how loud or feral voices overlap when several characters vocalize at once.
- Brief each actor on the character and ease them back into the voice before recording, then direct the session over talkback.
- Schedule substantial post-recording time for repeated editing of the voice files. That's when the real work starts.
- For dialogue built on banter, try to record the cast together.

## Avoid
- Long lines or constant chatter during battle, which pull attention and break immersion.
- Voices that compete with each other in the mix.
- Leaving small grunts and reactions out of the script.

## Examples from the episode
- **Super Smash Bros. — recording time:** about one hour per fighter (two hours at most with reveal/story lines), against a year or more of work on the fighter itself.
- **Super Smash Bros. — Bowser and Donkey Kong:** feral voices that need extra care so they don't clash.
- **Super Smash Bros. — Pokémon:** they say different things in every language, which makes localization extra work.
- **Kid Icarus: Uprising — group sessions:** cast recorded together where schedules allowed, to capture natural banter.
