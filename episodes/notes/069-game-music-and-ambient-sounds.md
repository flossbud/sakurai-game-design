# 069 · Game Music and Ambient Sounds

**Category:** Audio · [Video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=t_tRnBj9tqc) · [Transcript](../transcripts/069-game-music-and-ambient-sounds.md)

## In one sentence
Use fewer, memorable songs (such as one main theme with arrangements), let realistic visuals be backed by realistic ambient sound instead of music that sticks out, and make every song fit what the player is feeling.

## Key ideas
- A common complaint is that old game music was memorable and hummable, while modern soundtracks sound "straight out of Hollywood." He answers it by playing modern games with NES-style music: it is noisy, fun in a way, but it feels off.
- As graphics evolved and there was more visual information to process, music that stood out too much started to feel "off." Richer visuals change what the audio has to do.
- Realistic graphics call for realistic ambient sounds: wind flowing through leaves, feet pounding on dirt, armor clinking. A soundscape has many elements, and each needs its own appropriate presence in the mix.
- He thinks too many songs get produced for a single game (admitting he's guilty of it too). Huge soundtracks are harder to remember and harder to get excited by, so show restraint and use fewer songs more effectively.
- His own method: write a main theme, then use arrangements of it throughout the game. A song has more impact if people can remember its melody.
- It depends on the type of game, but in general make songs that complement how the player is feeling.

## Do this
- Write one main theme early, then plan where arrangements of it will appear across the game, instead of commissioning a new unrelated song for every scene.
- Audit the track list: cut or merge songs that don't have a melody players could remember and hum.
- If your graphics are realistic, budget for ambient layers (wind in foliage, footsteps by surface, armor and gear sounds) and mix each to an appropriate level so the music doesn't dominate.
- For each scene, write one line on what the player should be feeling there, and check the music supports that feeling.

## Avoid
- Producing so many songs for one game that none of them sticks.
- Music that stands out too much over detailed, realistic visuals.

## Examples from the episode
- **NES-style music over modern games (demo):** shows why loud, foreground chiptune melodies feel noisy and "off" against today's detailed visuals.
- **Realistic soundscapes:** wind through leaves, feet on dirt and clinking armor as the ambient layer realistic graphics need.

## Quote
> However you do it, a song will have more impact if people can remember its melody. (02:34)
