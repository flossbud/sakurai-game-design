# 166 · Facial Animations

**Category:** Animation · [Video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=TUHL7Ex-RH8) · [Transcript](../transcripts/166-facial-animations.md)

## In one sentence
When faces are small or stylized, push expressions past the point where they feel finished, because CG loses information and "too much is about right", while shaping them to the character's anatomy and source material.

## Key ideas
- Facial animations are what create expressions. Characters in Smash Ultimate are often too small to make out clearly, but the team was still diligent about faces. Even Zelda, who is less emotive than other fighters, has plenty of expressions.
- Smash doesn't need lip sync or subtle facial animation, so Sakurai wanted faces as lively as possible. He set the direction by retouching artwork before models were made, and most of the artwork he received had shifts in expression that were too small.
- Example: Wolf's damage face. The original wasn't unworkable, but Sakurai's retouch added more emphasis, which suited the structure of a wolf's jaw.
- Rule of thumb: "Get it to where it feels done, then go a step further and make the movements even bigger." Details to watch include showing the whites of wide-open eyes at both top and bottom, and not lining up the upper and lower teeth directly. The right details depend on your game's aims.
- Cutting-edge games convey emotion through tiny facial changes, and realistic and cartoony styles aim at different goals. But because you lose information working in CG, "too much is about right" in both cases.
- Robot expressions count as facial animation too. Steve's only facial animation is a blink, so his other movements have to make up for it. That's part of putting the source material first.

## Do this
- Once an expression looks finished, push it one more step (wider eyes, bigger mouth shapes, stronger brows) and judge it in-game at the size players actually see.
- On shocked or wide-open eyes, show eye white above and below the iris.
- When a mouth is open, offset the upper and lower teeth instead of stacking them exactly.
- Shape expressions around the character's anatomy, such as a bigger damage gape for a long animal jaw.
- Retouch concept art for key expressions before modeling begins, so modelers aim for the right intensity from the start.
- If the source character has no facial range (like Steve, who only blinks), carry the emotion through body movement instead of inventing faces the source lacks.

## Avoid
- Subtle expressions on characters players see at small sizes. They read as nothing.
- Overriding the source material just to add facial expressiveness.

## Examples from the episode
- **Smash Ultimate — Zelda:** not very emotive, yet with many distinct expressions.
- **Smash Ultimate — Wolf's damage face:** retouched for more emphasis to suit a wolf's jaw.
- **Smash Ultimate — Steve:** blinking is his only facial animation, so movement does the rest.
- **Smash Ultimate — robot fighters:** robot expressions also count as facial animation.
