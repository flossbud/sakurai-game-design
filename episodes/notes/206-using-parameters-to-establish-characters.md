# 206 · Using Parameters to Establish Characters

**Category:** Planning & Game Design · [Video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zwiS1L6QVY0) · [Transcript](../transcripts/206-using-parameters-to-establish-characters.md)

## In one sentence
Start each character from a neutral, workable baseline, then push their parameters and unique mechanics far enough that their identity fits in a word or two, and budget the programming that takes.

## Key ideas
- Whether you are making playable characters or enemies, try to maximize their eccentricities.
- Don't make something impossible to use, such as a shooting-game ship that fires everywhere except the direction it faces. Within a game's rules there is a near-ideal fit, a set of neutral capabilities. The trick is seeing how far you can deviate from that neutral point to create new ways to play. The process differs a lot between playable characters and enemies.
- Kirby's Gordo is invincible to every attack, can't attack and has no movement pattern. Dropped in alone it would be a completely uninteresting enemy, but placed near other enemies it becomes significant in several ways. Players remember it differently from the other enemies.
- Making characters or enemies unique introduces strategy. A character should be distinctive enough that you can describe its traits "in just a word or two."
- The unavoidable cost is programming. For a character to do something no one else can, there has to be specific code supporting it.
- Every Smash Bros. Ultimate DLC fighter has a special attribute: the Rebellion Gauge and Persona, MP and Commands, Gold Feathers for a charge attack, Super Special Moves, four Heroes' Relics, the double ARMS system, building with blocks, Winged Form, instant changing, eight-way directional commands, magic cycling and more. It took a lot of work, but it established the characters and produced a broad range of play styles, "After all, it's no fun if everyone's basically the same!"
- Numbers are a big part of what brings characters to life. Don't make small changes. Emphasize the differences, then iron out problems as production goes on.

## Do this
- Define a neutral baseline character that fits your rules, then deliberately push each new character away from it in specific parameters.
- Write a one- or two-word identity for every character. If you can't, push the character's traits further.
- For enemies, try traits that only matter in combination. An invincible, harmless obstacle like Gordo becomes interesting when placed next to other enemies.
- Budget programmer time in the schedule for each character's one-of-a-kind mechanic, such as a gauge, a resource, a stance switch or a special input.
- When tuning, start with bold parameter differences and fix the problems they cause during production. Don't start with small nudges.

## Avoid
- Eccentricity that makes a character unusable, like a ship that shoots every direction except forward.
- Timid, small parameter changes that leave every character basically the same.

## Examples from the episode
- **Kirby series — Gordo:** invincible, never attacks, has no movement pattern. It is dull alone, but placement near other enemies makes it significant and memorable.
- **Super Smash Bros. Ultimate — DLC fighters:** each has a unique system (Rebellion Gauge/Persona, MP and Commands, Gold Feathers, Super Special Moves, Heroes' Relics, double ARMS, block building, Winged Form, instant changing, eight-way commands, magic cycling), and each needed dedicated programming.

## Quote
