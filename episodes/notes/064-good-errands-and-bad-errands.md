# 064 · Good Errands and Bad Errands

**Category:** Planning & Game Design · [Video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IM9P9NIKo10) · [Transcript](../transcripts/064-good-errands-and-bad-errands.md)

## In one sentence
If you send players on errands, package them well: fitting rewards, frequent moments of joy, freedom to choose a strategy, a quick trip home, and a hint of mystery tied to your world.

## Key ideas
- Errands are the stretches where the story sends you back and forth across the world. In older games most people disliked them, but that may not hold for modern games, which have become better and more diverse; combat and foraging along the way provide fulfilment throughout the journey.
- The topic comes from page 102 of his column collection *Thoughts on Playing Games 2*. His example of a bad errand chain: a sick daughter needs herbs from the meadow, come back to the village, then go buy a mortar and pestle in the neighboring town. The same errands could be expressed much better.
- **1. Fitting rewards and returns:** players should receive things that are useful in the game. Watching an enjoyable story unfold can also be a reward.
- **2. Frequent moments of joy:** pack many pleasing moments into even a single errand. Interesting rewards from combat or foraging can be enough, and an unexpected twist helps too.
- **3. Freedom to strategize:** for example, a safer, longer route versus a dangerous shortcut. Players feel better when they choose the path forward themselves.
- **4. A speedy exit once the deed is done:** for example, whisking players back to town after the objective. As with travel, the journey home isn't as exciting as the journey there, so let players focus on the fun parts.
- **5. A sense of future developments:** don't send players off with "Go buy me some meat!" Give the same short trip a mystery tied to your world: look for a girl no one has seen lately, at a time when children have been going missing, and investigate a house where people have heard strange noises. Mechanically it's the same errand setting a few flags, but it's packaged far better.

## Do this
- Make each errand's reward something actually useful in play, or a story beat players want to see.
- Place several small payoffs along the route (combat drops, foraging finds, an unexpected twist) so a single errand contains many pleasing moments.
- Offer at least two ways to the objective, such as a long safe route and a risky shortcut.
- Once the objective is done, return the player to town automatically or offer an instant trip back instead of making them walk.
- Rewrite fetch-quest text so the task hints at a mystery in your world (a missing child, a house with strange noises) instead of a mundane chore.

## Avoid
- Mundane, flavorless tasks like "Go buy me some meat!"
- Back-and-forth fetch chains that offer nothing along the way (herbs, return, then go buy a mortar and pestle).
- Making players trek all the way back after they've finished the objective.

## Examples from the episode
- **Sakurai's column example — the sick daughter:** herbs from the meadow, return, then a mortar and pestle from the next town; functional but unexciting.
- **Rewritten errand — the missing girl:** the same short distance, but now an investigation tied to children going missing and a house with strange noises.

## Quote
> if you're going to make players move around, there are plenty of better ways to package it. (03:15)
