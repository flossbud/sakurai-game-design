# 092 · Sodatete! Kouchuu Ouja Mushiking

**Category:** Game Concepts · [Video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=b3Fd8pBK0EA) · [Transcript](../transcripts/092-sodatete-kouchuu-ouja-mushiking.md)

## In one sentence
When adapting an existing hit to a new format (here, a handheld digital pet), reuse its proven push and pull, smooth out growth-stage walls, add small touches that sell the fantasy, and test on the real production hardware before release.

## Key ideas
- Sodatete! Kouchuu Ouja Mushiking is a small handheld digital game, and it had the smallest team of any game he has made.
- The original Kouchuu Ouja Mushiking (2003) was a Japanese arcade card game. For 100 yen it printed a card of a rhino or stag beetle, and scanning the card's barcode made the Mushi (insects) fight via rock-paper-scissors. Each Mushi specialized in one of the three shapes, which created push and pull: play your strong "paper," or try to win by surprise? It was all about reading your opponent. It was a big hit with early elementary schoolers, and adults played it too.
- Tadashi Takezaki (then at Sega, now CEO of TMS Entertainment) asked him to cross Mushiking with handheld digital pet games. The final game stayed close to Sakurai's proposal (dated 2005.5.9). The proposal footage is mostly garbled in the transcript.
- In pet games, some growth stages are stronger and others weaker, so they took care that players wouldn't hit a wall in battles. To strengthen the feeling of raising a pet, the Mushi grazes on its food instead of scarfing it down instantly.
- The tiny device has many features: affection, likes and dislikes, items, illness, training, fatigue, mutations, presents, farewells and runaways. There are hidden features too. If you wake your Mushi early in the morning, it adjusts its schedule to match.
- Right after, at E3, he was asked to make Smash Bros. Brawl, and doing both was hard. He worked remotely ("SOHO"), meeting key staff in person only a few times at Sega headquarters and finishing over email and online communication. Programming was by Kan Naito, main programmer of Dragon Quest III and IV.
- The development board was custom and just under one square meter: circuit boards attached to a plain piece of wood, which he had to stash away while working on other things.
- He never tested on the production handheld before release. Possibly because of a difference in processing speed, the Mushi gets hungry faster than planned and almost always wants food, which puts unintended stress on the player. He still thinks the core is plenty fun, and the IR-link battles were a treat.
- Because Mushiking already existed, he didn't have to think hard about push and pull for this project.

## Do this
- When adapting an existing game to a new format, carry over its proven push and pull (here, rock-paper-scissors with each creature specializing in one shape) and spend your design effort on what the new format adds.
- In games with growth stages, check every stage's power in battle and smooth out dips so players never hit a wall.
- Sell the fantasy with small behaviors, such as a pet grazing over its food instead of eating it instantly.
- Hide a few responsive behaviors for players to discover, like a pet adapting its schedule to when the player wakes it.
- Test all timer-driven systems (hunger, fatigue, growth) on the actual production hardware before release, since processing-speed differences can change their rates.

## Avoid
- Shipping without testing on production hardware. Timing differences can turn a pacing value into constant, unintended stress.

- **Sodatete! Kouchuu Ouja Mushiking — feeding:** the Mushi grazes on its meal to create a "pet" feeling.
- **Sodatete! Kouchuu Ouja Mushiking — hidden schedule:** waking the Mushi early shifts its daily routine.
- **Sodatete! Kouchuu Ouja Mushiking — hunger bug:** untested on production hardware, the Mushi gets hungry faster than planned.
- **Sodatete! Kouchuu Ouja Mushiking — IR-link battles:** a highlight of the finished game.
