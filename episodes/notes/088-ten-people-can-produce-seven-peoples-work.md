# 088 · Ten People Can Produce Seven People's Work

**Category:** Team Management · [Video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1-iZFmenARA) · [Transcript](../transcripts/088-ten-people-can-produce-seven-peoples-work.md)

## In one sentence
Budget for management overhead (ten people generally produce about seven people's worth of work), and keep a growing team aligned with regular meetings where each subteam shows what it has finished.

## Key ideas
- A 10-person team doesn't produce 10 people's work. It depends on the situation, but generally it's about seven, because running a team takes management time. Everyone working 100% on their own tasks wouldn't really be a team.
- At minimum you need someone managing workloads, someone communicating with companies and other outside parties, and supervisors who set the creative direction. The director sometimes does that directly, and on bigger projects a lead designer or lead programmer may oversee overall quality.
- When a skilled worker moves into leadership, they usually can't produce their old output at a larger scale, so you lose the work they would have done. That's how teams work: talented people become the "brains" who set creative direction, maintain quality and keep the team moving toward its goals.
- On small projects of a few people, everyone can contribute normally. The bigger the team, the more management effort it needs and the longer it takes to get everyone on the same page, and not just creatively: tools, security and rules all have to be set up and understood.
- He recommends meetings where each subteam presents its results, for example the Character Team showing the characters finished in a given period. Everyone sees what others are doing, presentation skills improve, and people become more aware that they're making something for others.
- Customers will eventually have the game in their hands, so get plenty of eyes on it before then and polish it together.

## Do this
- When scheduling, assume effective output of roughly 70% of headcount (10 people ≈ 7 people's work), and expect overhead to grow as the team grows.
- Name who handles workload management, external communication and creative or quality supervision, instead of assuming those jobs happen on their own.
- When you promote your strongest creator to lead, plan for losing their individual output and backfill it.
- Set up shared tools, security practices and team rules early, and make sure every member understands them.
- Hold a recurring all-team showcase where each subteam presents what it finished that period.

## Avoid
- Planning as if headcount equals output.
- Expecting a newly promoted lead to keep producing their old individual output while also leading.

## Examples from the episode
- **Character Team presentation:** a subteam showing everyone the characters it finished in a period, as a model for showcase meetings.

## Quote
> It depends on the situation, of course, but 10 people can generally produce seven people's worth of work. (00:04)
