# 073 · Say No to Slow!

**Category:** Design Specifics · [Video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3qEKNxp_-Qs) · [Transcript](../transcripts/073-say-no-to-slow.md)

## In one sentence
Cut every second players spend not playing (waits, empty stretches, bloated cutscenes), because slowness is the one trap that can make nobody love your game.

## Key ideas
- Tastes differ, so don't dismiss a game just because you can't get into it. But one trap could make *nobody* love your game: making it slow.
- "Slow" includes characters that move too slowly, but more importantly it means things taking too long and long stretches with nothing to do. It's about how much time players spend not actually playing.
- Slowness can sometimes build suspense, but that may not land with today's players.
- Some waits can't be avoided. No matter how much you optimize, load times may not get as short as you'd like. What really irritates players is a wait that feels like a mistake, as if someone in development dropped the ball.
- Trimming waits gives a denser experience. Cutscenes can work as a palate cleanser, and that's better than keeping the player busy every second. But cutscenes that are just needless downtime should be tightened, and leaner cutscenes suit modern games better.
- He tracks actual playtime, meaning the time the player has control. How long is the player in control, how long not, and how much is just emptiness? He feels bad about every second players aren't enjoying the game.
- Modern game scale may require some spectacle, but trimming and slimming gets you where you need to be.

## Do this
- Time a full session and split it into three buckets: player in control, player not in control (cutscenes, loads, forced animations), and empty time with nothing to do. Set targets to shrink the second and third.
- Review every cutscene. Keep the ones that work as a deliberate breather and shorten or cut the ones that are just downtime.
- Optimize load times. Where waits remain, make sure they don't look like an oversight left in by accident.
- Find long stretches with nothing to do (empty traversal, slow menus, slow movement) and shorten them or fill them with play.

## Avoid
- Making players wait. He calls it "a sin."
- Relying on slowness to build suspense. It may not land today.
- Waits that feel like someone dropped the ball in development.

## Examples from the episode
- **Load times:** may never get as short as you want, but shouldn't feel like a development mistake.
- **Cutscenes:** useful as a palate cleanser, but leaner ones fit games better now.

## Quote
> This might sound like an exaggeration, but I'll say it anyway: To make players wait is a sin! (02:45)
