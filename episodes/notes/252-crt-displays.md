# 252 · CRT Displays

**Category:** Grab Bag · [Video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9FDDPZtWvhM) · [Transcript](../transcripts/252-crt-displays.md)

## In one sentence
Games are built for the screens they're shown on, and older games were made for CRTs (fast response, bright phosphors, light-gun timing, interlacing and hidden screen edges), so account for that hardware when you study, port or imitate them.

## Key ideas
- A CRT (cathode-ray tube) is a funnel-shaped vacuum tube. Electron beams from the cathode light up red, green and blue phosphors, drawing narrow rows left to right and moving down row by row; the picture is built from afterimages, so the screen flickers a little. CRTs were still the norm through the Super Nintendo, Nintendo 64 and GameCube eras, so dev monitors were large and deep (people lined up figures on them, such as Gundam models).
- Fast response time: unlike LCDs, CRTs need no buffer or image processor. Many score-chasers in classic games still go out of their way to use them.
- Bright, flashy screens: the phosphors produce a more radiant white than an LCD shows for the same image, and at high brightness you can see halation. Sakurai loves that look.
- Light guns: the NES light gun worked like a camera looking at the screen. When you pull the trigger, the screen goes black and white boxes appear over the hittable elements; if the gun senses a white box while the scanlines are at a certain point, it's a hit. That's hard to do on an LCD, so modern light-gun play needs an external sensor like the Wii Sensor Bar.
- Interlacing: NTSC TVs have 525 scanlines. Odd lines are drawn first, then even lines, each half being a "field." Normally both fields show the same picture, taking 1/30 of a second, but game hardware could draw different pictures to the odd and even fields, giving a 60 fps display.
- The "safe area": of the 525 scanlines only about 490 are usable, and a CRT never shows the whole image. In the NES era, developers assumed the outer area would never be seen, so odd behavior there, like scrambled characters at the screen edge, wasn't a problem.
- Games of the past were made solely with CRTs in mind, so they feel different today. A checkered pattern of pixels, for example, takes on a completely different texture on a CRT.

## Do this
- When you study, port or draw inspiration from retro games, judge their art the way it was meant to be seen. Dithering such as checkered pixel patterns was designed for a CRT, not a sharp LCD.
- Keep display response time in mind for timing-heavy play; skilled players notice it enough that score-chasers seek out CRTs.
- Keep essential information away from the edges of the image on any display that may crop them, the way the CRT "safe area" assumed the outer lines were never visible.

## Examples from the episode
- **NES light gun — hit detection:** the screen flashes black with white boxes over targets, and the gun reads them as the scanlines pass.
- **Wii Sensor Bar:** the kind of external sensor light-gun play now needs without a CRT.
- **Checkered pixel patterns:** look completely different on a CRT than on a modern display.
