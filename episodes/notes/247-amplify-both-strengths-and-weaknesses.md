# 247 · Amplify Both Strengths and Weaknesses

**Category:** Game Essence · [Video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fc-hOvTBTCc) · [Transcript](../transcripts/247-amplify-both-strengths-and-weaknesses.md)

## In one sentence
When you balance characters or moves, keep their peaks and valleys and nerf a secondary parameter instead of averaging everything out, because options that feel alike make the whole game bland.

## Key ideas
- For Smash Bros., the Balancing Team played against each other day in and day out, and used those results plus data from online matches to find what needed adjusting.
- The usual fix for a fighter that's too strong is to shave down the strength and lessen the weaknesses to compensate. It's the "obvious" solution and sometimes unavoidable, but it usually leads to your downfall: the more alike fighters' abilities become, the more they feel like the same character. Players choosing the same fighter on purpose is fine; the game making different fighters similar gives everything a bland feel.
- Sakurai approves of peaks and valleys: every option has strengths and weaknesses. That can be divisive, and it would be a problem if it weren't. You want one player to say "A is better!", another to say "B is better!", and both to say "Then let's settle it in a match!" Social media and echo chambers spread biases quickly and make this harder than it used to be, but a creator can't shy away from it.
- Variety among fighters is the lifeblood of the game and what keeps it fresh, so push the dynamic range as far as you can.
- Even one attack has many components: attack power, speed, duration, windup, follow-through length, range, hit size, animation speed, knockback, combo ability, shield damage, shield recovery, and more. If you must adjust a move, you can leave its characteristic attack power untouched and lengthen the follow-through (the window when the fighter is vulnerable), or make the hitbox smaller.
- This applies to games without competitive elements too. When there are many actions or moves, don't make the balance mediocre. The differences are what let players find their personal preferences.

## Do this
- Before nerfing a move, list all of its parameters (power, windup, speed, duration, follow-through, range, hitbox size, animation speed, knockback, combo ability, shield damage, shield recovery) and mark the one that defines its identity.
- Leave that defining stat alone and adjust a secondary one instead, for example by lengthening the vulnerable follow-through or shrinking the hitbox.
- When something is too strong, don't also soften its weaknesses in the same patch. Keep the trade-off intact.
- Compare your roster or move list side by side, and wherever two options feel interchangeable, push their stats further apart.
- Use internal matches and live data to find problems, but judge every fix by whether each option still has a distinct identity.

## Avoid
- Shaving down strengths and filling in weaknesses until different characters feel the same.
- Mediocre, flat balance across a large set of moves or options.
- Backing away from divisive designs because of social-media bias.

## Examples from the episode
- **Super Smash Bros. — balancing process:** the Balancing Team's daily matches and online match data were used to spot issues.
- **Super Smash Bros. — adjusting a move:** keep the attack power but lengthen the follow-through, or shrink the hitbox.

## Quote
> When there are lots of possible actions or moves, don't make the balance mediocre! Keep your peaks and valleys! (02:29)
