# 029 · Spec Changes

**Category:** Work Ethic · [Video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bzWibA7izF8) · [Transcript](../transcripts/029-spec-changes.md)

## In one sentence
Avoid mid-development spec changes by planning for fun from the start, but when change is unavoidable, surface problems immediately, put every option on the table, and decide fast.

## Key ideas
- Spec changes scrap finished work, cause detours, cost money and can demotivate the team, so avoid them when you can, while staying flexible.
- Some things only become clear once you actually build the game; trying to picture the finished game up front can mean spending as much time on documentation as on development. Team direction, differences between programmers and schedule pressure also force changes; it happens all the time.
- The change to really avoid is one forced because the game isn't fun, so keep the fun in mind from the planning stage.
- Games combine many disciplines: making one enemy move takes planning, art design, modeling, animation, visual effects, programming, scripting, sound effects and more. A delay upstream or a snag downstream halts development. Better technology, cutting man-hours or poor performance can also justify changes.
- Decide case by case, and don't dig in your heels on an idea that isn't working.
- Report problems immediately: "I knew that was wrong!" after the fact helps no one. Players can't fix what's wrong, but someone on the team might. Use collective knowledge: lay out every option and pick the best one for now that also works long-term.
- Sakurai's style is to decide the moment a problem appears, once he's heard what he needs, because long deliberation wastes time. Decisions need accurate information, so he wants people to tell him everything they know. Teams often waste time arguing about how to raise a problem, which makes it worse.

## Do this
- Check the core fun during planning (and in a prototype) so "it isn't fun" never becomes the reason for a late spec change.
- Write enough spec to start building and expect details to settle during implementation, instead of documenting the whole finished game up front.
- Make it a team rule that whoever spots a potential problem tells the responsible person right away, with everything they know, without debating how to phrase it.
- When a problem comes up, list every possible option, then choose the one that works both short-term and long-term.
- As a lead, decide as soon as you have the facts instead of deliberating at length.
- Before approving a spec change, trace every discipline it touches (planning, art, modeling, animation, VFX, programming, scripting, sound) and how it shifts each schedule.

## Avoid
- Digging in your heels on an idea that isn't working.
- Keeping problems to yourself and saying "I knew that was wrong!" afterwards.
- Spending time arguing over how to bring up a problem.
- Trying to document the entire finished game before building it.

## Examples from the episode
- **A single enemy character:** needs planning, art design, modeling, animation, visual effects, programming, scripting and sound effects, so one late task can halt everything after it.

## Quote
> The sooner problems get brought up, the less time you'll end up wasting. (04:02)
