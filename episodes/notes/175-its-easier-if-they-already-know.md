# 175 · It’s Easier If They Already Know

**Category:** Planning & Game Design · [Video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ANerCiyfJjo) · [Transcript](../transcripts/175-its-easier-if-they-already-know.md)

## In one sentence
Use the genre's de facto standard controls so you can skip explaining what players already know, but still support newcomers and let everyone remap buttons.

## Key ideas
- First-person shooters made outside Japan share more or less the same control scheme and even feel largely the same. Sakurai reads this as those games putting immersion, the game world and a compelling story ahead of original game systems.
- Developers still have to remember that some players won't be familiar with their game's genre.
- The big advantage of familiar controls is that you can abridge or entirely omit explanations. Few FPS games tell you "fire with R2", "your guns need ammo" or "throw a grenade with L1"; at most they show an attack-button icon when an enemy appears.
- Developers tend to avoid copying other titles, but the industry has "de facto standards" you're better off using, because they make "the handshake between human and computer" a little warmer.
- Include button remapping even if it makes things more complicated. Designers used to think they had already found the best controls so nobody would need to change them, but players want the freedom to play how they like, and games should deliver that whenever possible.
- Some requests can't be met. Smash Bros. can't be made playable with the D-pad, because an analog stick gives granular values while a D-pad only gives 0 or 1.
- Aside: Sakurai once played Chelnov at an arcade with bizarre controls (a button to walk forward, stick up to fire, stick sideways to jump) that turned out to be a miswired harness. The weirdness was actually fun, which makes him feel overly standardized controls could cost players some unique fun.

## Do this
- Map your controls to the genre's established conventions (for example, the standard fire and grenade buttons in an FPS) unless you have a deliberate reason to break them.
- Cut tutorial text for conventions your audience already knows. At most, show the attack-button icon the first time an enemy appears.
- Still check your onboarding with someone who doesn't play your genre, since not every player knows the conventions.
- Ship a button-remapping option, even though it adds complexity to menus and code.
- Before promising support for a new input device, check whether your mechanics depend on analog values that a digital D-pad (only 0 or 1) can't produce.

## Avoid
- Avoiding standard controls just so you don't look like you're copying other games.
- Assuming your default layout is so good that nobody will need to change it.
- Spelling out things genre players already know, such as "your guns need ammo".

## Examples from the episode
- **FPS games made outside Japan — shared controls:** they rarely explain "fire with R2" or "throw a grenade with L1" and at most show an attack icon when an enemy appears.
- **Smash Bros. — D-pad requests:** not possible, because the game relies on the analog stick's granular values.
- **Chelnov — arcade cabinet:** a miswired harness gave it strange controls that Sakurai found strangely fun.

## Quote
> By doing so, you're making the handshake between human and computer just a little bit warmer. (01:06)
