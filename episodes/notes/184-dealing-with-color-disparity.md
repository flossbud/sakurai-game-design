# 184 · Dealing With Color Disparity

**Category:** Graphics · [Video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=240NQ--7xvU) · [Transcript](../transcripts/184-dealing-with-color-disparity.md)

## In one sentence
Different monitors and room lighting change how color looks, so give your team a process for matching colors: a shared reference screen, calibration by eye and neutral lighting, so art isn't judged on screens that are lying.

## Key ideas
- A longstanding problem in graphics checks is monitor disparity: each team member's monitor shows color slightly differently. Even the same model from the same company can vary in color and brightness, and ambient lighting changes how color is perceived too.
- It may not matter much to most designers, programmers or bug testers, but it's critical for artists and for supervisors like Sakurai. On the monitor he uses for checking, any variance is fatal.
- Color-measuring and calibration devices exist, but because games are often shown on ordinary TVs, they can be of limited use ("maybe we're just not using them right").
- In the end, his team sets up a monitor far enough away for everyone to see and calibrates color by sight. Sometimes the gap between monitors is too big to fix.
- Example: the same Mewtwo image on two monitors. On one, the shadow on the right disappears and the contrast is weaker, and no amount of calibration fixes that.
- Better monitors aren't the whole answer, because the same thing happens on everyday TVs; the games you play may look different from what their creators intended. Inside the team, though, you should remove color disparity as much as possible.
- Lighting matters as well. One company he worked for filled its office with moody yellow lights, and all its art came out with a bluish tint. He recommends lighting that's as neutral as possible.

## Do this
- Set up one shared reference screen where the whole team can see it, and calibrate everyone's monitors by eye against it.
- Don't rely on calibration devices alone. Games are usually viewed on ordinary TVs, so finish with a visual check on a shared screen.
- If a monitor loses shadow detail or contrast that calibration can't fix, stop using it for art checks.
- Light art and review areas with neutral lighting. Tinted light such as warm yellow pushes artists to compensate, as with the bluish art at the office lit in yellow.
- Make color matching a defined step in the art-review process, especially for artists and whoever supervises them.

## Avoid
- Assuming everyone on the team sees the same colors.
- Moody or tinted office lighting in places where art is made or reviewed.
- Approving art on a monitor known to crush shadows or flatten contrast.

## Examples from the episode
- **Mewtwo image on two monitors:** one hides the shadow on the right and shows weaker contrast, a gap calibration can't close.
- **A former workplace with yellow lights:** its art consistently came out with a bluish tint.
