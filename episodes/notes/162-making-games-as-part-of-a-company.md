# 162 · Making Games as Part of a Company

**Category:** Team Management · [Video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fiCLKUQtKg0) · [Transcript](../transcripts/162-making-games-as-part-of-a-company.md)

## In one sentence
If you make games at a company, understand the trade you made (steady pay and shared risk, in exchange for not personally profiting from hits) and choose to support your colleagues instead of feeling forced into it.

## Key ideas
- The obvious upside of company work is a steady monthly salary. The flip side is that even a great game probably won't change your salary or bonus.
- Global game sales are polarized: titles that sell really sell, and ones that don't really don't. If production, advertising and management costs exceed revenue, the game is in the red and eats into the company's assets.
- Poor sales can't be pinned on any one person. The visuals, the title, a lack of advertising, competing titles, market timing and platform choice all play a part. People who tried the game and didn't enjoy it matter less than you might think, since they did try it.
- If a company's games keep failing it can go under, but the employees were still paid for their work. The company takes the hit. It can also use hits to cover deficits and reinvest in future development.
- Companies do far more than make the game. They provide the office and equipment, onboard staff, research dev tools, manage middleware and licenses, and handle translation and market adjustments. The bigger the company, the higher those costs. The company balances overall risk and reward so that everyone can focus on what they do best.
- Friction between different positions and viewpoints is inevitable, but company work is about supporting others and being supported in return. You chose this path, so don't think of it as being forced on you; cooperate however you can. Sakurai, a freelancer, works with other companies, does what's expected of him and always follows their rules.

## Do this
- When a game underperforms, review the whole chain (visuals, title, advertising, competing releases, timing, platform) instead of looking for one person to blame.
- When you join or partner with a company, learn its rules and follow them, as Sakurai does as a freelancer.
- When friction comes up between roles, treat it as a question of mutual support: ask what you can do so the other side can do their best work.
- When weighing solo versus company development, count the hidden support a company provides: equipment, onboarding, tool research, middleware and licensing, localization, and absorbing the financial risk.

## Avoid
- Thinking of your job as something forced on you. It's a path you chose.
- Pinning poor sales on a single person.

## Examples from the episode
