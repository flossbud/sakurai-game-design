# 170 · The Pros and Cons of Leaderboards

**Category:** Game Essence · [Video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=g7nC6NtTMIk) · [Transcript](../transcripts/170-the-pros-and-cons-of-leaderboards.md)

## In one sentence
Use rankings to give players purpose and fair matches, but present standing so that most players feel successful (a score where higher is better, measured against the whole player base, with the top player hidden) instead of a raw leaderboard that tells nearly everyone they're losing.

## Key ideas
- Upside one is purpose: "Without a leaderboard, you're just fighting—and there's little thrill to that." The result of your hard work has to be linked to an incentive to aim higher.
- Upside two: with matchmaking, rankings can connect players of similar skill. But the odds of finding someone at your level who is also close enough for a stable connection and playing at the same time are very slim, especially in low-population regions. If the matching range is too narrow, players never find a match; if it's too broad, they face opponents far outside their level. It's a hard balance.
- The downside, to exaggerate a little, is "eternal humiliation". If you aren't number one, you're not reaching the goal, so you're continually losing. Attitude plays a part (you could also say you're continually winning), but leaderboards aren't good at making people feel successful. Trying your hardest and landing 400th or 10,000th can sap motivation.
- Smash's answer was Global Smash Power (GSP), an inverse ranking that uses the maximum global player count as the denominator to measure how powerful a player is within that group, so a higher number is better. It doesn't always work as well as Sakurai hoped, but he thinks it's a good bit better than ordinary leaderboards. With Smash's huge player base, nobody would be happy being told they're in "x-millionth place".
- GSP also hides who the top player in the world is, which isn't helpful to anyone outside the very elite and just makes people see the gap and give up. And it naturally excludes cheats and invalid uploaded data.
- Elite Smash gives top players the satisfaction of being a chosen elite and matches them against each other, which keeps them from matching with lower-ranked players.
- The obvious choice isn't always right, so think beyond a standard leaderboard.

## Do this
- Show each player's standing as a number that rises as they improve, measured against the total player population (how many players they're above), rather than a position where lower is better.
- Don't show the identity or score of the world's #1 to general players. Show progress against the broad population instead.
- Tune matchmaking range with population in mind. Widen it enough that players in low-population regions and hours still find matches, but cap it so skill gaps stay reasonable.
- Add an elite tier above a threshold that rewards top players with status and keeps them matching each other instead of newcomers.
- Design the ranking metric so impossible or cheated results can be filtered out automatically.

## Avoid
- Raw position leaderboards for a huge player base, where most people see a discouraging rank like "x-millionth place".
- Showing everyone exactly how far they are from the top player.
- Matchmaking ranges so narrow nobody finds a game, or so broad that matches are lopsided.

## Examples from the episode
- **Super Smash Bros. Ultimate — Global Smash Power (GSP):** an inverse ranking against the global player count where higher is better. It hides the #1 player and filters invalid data.
- **Super Smash Bros. Ultimate — Elite Smash:** a status tier for top players that also separates them from lower-ranked matchmaking.

## Quote
> The obvious choice isn't always the right one, so try to think outside the box and consider methods beyond a standard leaderboard system. (02:54)
