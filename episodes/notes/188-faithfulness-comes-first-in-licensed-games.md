# 188 · Faithfulness Comes First in Licensed Games

**Category:** Game Essence · [Video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=EgUvH3YMVCM) · [Transcript](../transcripts/188-faithfulness-comes-first-in-licensed-games.md)

## In one sentence
In a licensed game, faithfulness to the source and satisfying fans' love for it come first and fun comes second, so look for source settings that naturally become gameplay and use the gaps the source leaves to invent.

## Key ideas
- Speaking generally, licensed games have had a reputation since the NES days (the 1980s) for being less fun than "regular" games. Sakurai thinks that's natural if they're judged on fun alone.
- Why they're harder to make fun: the source material restricts the design. Game stories have to work differently from other kinds of narrative, and making the two mesh is a herculean task. It's hard to change the original's story and characters, and a story that follows the original is already "spoiled".
- Sometimes the original setting is a great base for a game. His prime example is the Megami Tensei series, which the Persona series descends from. It was originally a novel, and its concept of summoning demons from a computer to battle was a perfect fit for a game. It still took skilled developers, since demon fusion was an idea unique to the game.
- What people want most from a licensed game is faithfulness to the original and something that satisfies fans' love for it. Fans see the game as one form of the property among many (comics, anime, figures, events and games), and they respond best when those come out at the right times. Whether the game has good push and pull, or is fun, matters less to them.
- It's product value versus fun. Achieving both is great, but you'll never get a perfect score on all fronts.
- The Naruto games have especially impressive battle event animations. Sakurai likes to imagine the game developers' sequences pushing the TV animators to up their game, and both sides pushing each other higher.
- Super Smash Bros. is a licensed title in a sense. Its characters come from games, but as with any license the team couldn't change too much and had more restrictions than in a normal game. Fox and Captain Falcon were the two whose moves they could create relatively freely, because both are pilots and don't do much else in their own games. If Captain Falcon had used his sidearm more in his own game, he might have become a ranged fighter, and the Falcon Punch might never have existed.

## Do this
- On a licensed game, set faithfulness to the source and satisfying fans' love as the top goal, then make the push and pull as good as you can within those limits.
- Look in the source for a setting or concept that naturally maps to gameplay (as summoning demons from a computer did), then add mechanics unique to the game on top (as demon fusion did).
- Time the game's release to fit the property's other media, such as comics, anime, figures and events.
- When adapting existing characters, stay close to what the source shows, and invent freely where the source shows little, as with characters who mostly just pilot vehicles.
- Build showpiece moments, like spectacular battle event animations, that can stand next to the source material.

## Avoid
- Freely rewriting the original's story and characters.
- Judging a licensed game by fun alone, or expecting a perfect score on both faithfulness and fun.

## Examples from the episode
- **Megami Tensei (and its descendant, Persona):** a novel whose idea of summoning demons from a computer suited a game, plus demon fusion invented for the game.
- **Naruto games:** impressive battle event animations that Sakurai imagines spurred the TV animators on.
- **Super Smash Bros. — Fox and Captain Falcon:** pilots with little on-foot action in their own games, which gave the team freedom and produced the Falcon Punch.

## Quote
> It's product value versus fun. It's great if you can achieve both, but you'll never get a perfect score on all fronts. (02:21)
