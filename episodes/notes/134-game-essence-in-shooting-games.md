# 134 · Game Essence in Shooting Games

**Category:** Game Essence · [Video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=iN3IyqPjLkc) · [Transcript](../transcripts/134-game-essence-in-shooting-games.md)

## In one sentence
Build risk and reward into your systems by tying power to danger (firepower that grows as you close in, rewards placed beside hazards, weapons with real trade-offs), and study why a game is fun instead of copying it.

## Key ideas
- This builds on the first Game Essence video (Space Invaders). The foundation is push and pull: take a risk to earn a reward.
- Darius: the screen is wide and only a few shots can be on screen at once, so it's easy to end up unable to fire. That would usually feel bad, but here it creates push and pull. At the back of the scroll area it's easy to use up all your shots; at the front you can fire far more (he shows the Genesis version because it's easier to see). Bombs work the same way: you can drop them quickly near enemies or terrain, but not from far away.
- In Darius's famous boss fights, staying back lowers risk but means fewer shots and less reward; getting right up to the boss lets you take it out quickly with rapid fire.
- Gradius: touching terrain costs a life, so enemies placed on terrain are already a risk, and beating them is the reward. Power-ups are sometimes placed next to terrain. The weapons that can kill enemies without getting close to terrain each have pros and cons, which broadens the game essence. The double cannon makes hitting enemies above you easier, but fires only two shots at a time and is worse than the laser at shooting straight ahead.
- "What downsides will you accept, and what upsides will you gain?" This kind of thinking works in any game.
- Easily wiping out every enemy on screen might feel good in the moment, but it has no push and pull. Strong game essence isn't everything, though: focusing on it too much can cost flair and even make the game feel like a chore.
- To make a game with its own flavor, don't just mimic an existing game; think about why that game is fun in the first place.

## Do this
- Cap how many player shots can be on screen at once, so the effective fire rate rises the closer the player gets to enemies, which rewards the risk of approaching.
- Design boss fights so aggressive, close positioning kills faster while hanging back is safer but slower.
- Place rewards (power-ups, valuable enemies) next to hazards such as deadly terrain, so collecting them means taking a risk.
- Give each weapon or option a clear upside and a clear downside (e.g., covers upward angles but fires only two shots at a time and is weaker straight ahead), so choosing one is a real trade-off.
- When you study a game you admire, write down why it's fun (its risk/reward structure) before borrowing anything from it.

## Avoid
- Letting the player easily wipe out every enemy on screen; it feels good briefly but has no push and pull.
- Pushing game essence so hard that the game loses its flair or feels like a chore.
- Mimicking an existing game without understanding why it's fun.

## Examples from the episode
- **Space Invaders:** the first Game Essence video's example of push and pull.
- **Darius (Genesis version) — shot limit, bombs and bosses:** being near enemies lets you fire more and drop bombs faster; close-range rapid fire kills bosses quickly.
- **Gradius — terrain and weapons:** terrain is deadly, power-ups sit near it, and the double cannon trades straight-ahead power and shot count for upward coverage compared to the laser.
