# 202 · Handicap Systems

**Category:** Design Specifics · [Video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-aA_mjpCPMg) · [Transcript](../transcripts/202-handicap-systems.md)

## In one sentence
Decide on purpose how much your competitive game should even out skill: casual games need catch-up systems so everyone gets turns winning, while a game meant to reflect skill should limit leveling to chaotic elements and optional settings.

## Key ideas
- Competitive games are most fun when "it's anyone's match." If the skill gap is too large it hardly counts as a game, and in reality that gap can be painfully large. Some games add handicap systems for this reason.
- Mario Kart is the extreme example. Like almost every racing game it gives trailing players speed boosts, and its items also level the field. The leader can easily be dethroned, so it isn't quite fair.
- Some people dislike this, but Sakurai calls it "an absolute must" for a casual game, because a game the most skilled player wins every time isn't fun. Online tournaments are different, but in an ordinary household it is best when different people take turns winning.
- Smash Bros. deliberately does not do this. Sakurai wanted results to reflect skill, so trailing players get no attack boost and no reduced knockback. Fighter-specific handicaps such as Sephiroth's Winged Form belong to the characters themselves.
- Smash has only one handicap that applies to everyone: the player in the lead has a harder time getting a Smash Ball, and the Final Smash Meter should charge faster for a losing player. Terrain and items can also swing who wins. Chaotic, anything-goes content (Final Smashes, items, stages, Final Smash Meters) fits a party game well.
- He designs Smash mainly for play at home, and he noted in his Smash Bros. Brawl proposal that it is in some ways unsuited to online play. At home you can talk out minor differences. If someone keeps using a cheap move, you can declare it off-limits, and that becomes part of the fun or a good memory, so "it's OK if there's a bit of weirdness there."
- When he plays children or beginners, he lets them grab the Smash Ball or land a couple of good hits. He calls human adjustments like this possibly "the most effective form of handicap there is." He also reminds viewers that Smash has an automatic handicap setting.

## Do this
- Decide early whether your game is casual (everyone should get turns winning) or meant to reflect skill, and choose your handicap mechanics to match.
- In a casual multiplayer game, add catch-up mechanics: speed boosts for trailing players and items that can pull the leader back.
- If results should reflect skill, don't raise trailing players' attack power or lower their knockback. Route catch-up through chaotic elements instead: make power-ups harder for the leader to get, charge comeback meters faster for losing players, and let items and stage hazards stir things up.
- Add an optional automatic handicap setting so groups with uneven skill can turn it on.
- If your game is mainly played locally, accept small quirks that players can settle among themselves with house rules.

## Avoid
- Letting the most skilled player win every single time in a game meant for casual, at-home play.

## Examples from the episode
- **Mario Kart:** speed boosts plus items for trailing players. It is an extreme leveler, and the leader is easily dethroned.
- **Smash Bros.:** no stat buffs for losing players. The only universal handicap is that the leader has a harder time getting a Smash Ball and the Final Smash Meter charges faster for players who are losing.
- **Smash Bros. Ultimate — Sephiroth's Winged Form:** a handicap built into a single fighter rather than a system for all players.
- **Smash Bros. Brawl proposal:** Sakurai wrote that the game is meant for home play and is in some ways unsuited to online play.
- **Smash Bros. — automatic handicap setting:** a built-in option he wishes more people would try.

## Quote
> Games where the person with the most skill keeps winning every single time just aren't fun. (00:49)
