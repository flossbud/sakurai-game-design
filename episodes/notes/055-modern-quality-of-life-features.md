# 055 · Modern Quality of Life Features

**Category:** UI · [Video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=j2BX4yw8Z4Y) · [Transcript](../transcripts/055-modern-quality-of-life-features.md)

## In one sentence
For big titles, give players extensive, well-explained settings and accessibility options (The Last of Us Part I is the model), because today's players arrive with different controllers, needs and experience and should be able to set the game up however they like.

## Key ideas
- Accessibility means design that makes a game more convenient and playable, including options that help players with disabilities. Sakurai tours the PS5 version of The Last of Us Part I like a Let's Play and calls its options "incredibly well-crafted."
- **Visual aids:** the Accessibility menu has many submenus and pulls in options from other menus (with some overlap). HUD size comes with a preview window that shows what each setting does ("a quality-of-life feature within the quality-of-life features"). There are also background brightness (adjustable mid-game), colorblind mode, a High Contrast Display toggled by swiping the touchpad, and a screen magnifier. Motion-sickness options tone down screen movement at some cost to presentation and can add a persistent center dot.
- **Navigation:** arrows toward your destination help because the player only sees a small part of the world, and the more realistic a game is, the harder the environment is to read. Other options vibrate the controller when you're about to fall off a ledge, enhance Listen Mode, give infinite breath, and let you skip puzzles, all of which can strongly affect difficulty.
- **Senses:** a screen reader voices all on-screen text, not just dialogue. There are cinematic descriptions, traversal audio cues (a sound when you're near a place you can jump or pick something up), combat audio and vibration cues, and Speech to Vibrations. Sakurai mentions experimenting himself with Switch HD Rumble, matching vibrations to the frequency of impact sounds.
- **Difficulty assists:** Combat Accessibility options (grabbed enemies can't break free, allies can't be grabbed) are locked behind an extra unlock step so nobody changes them by accident. An Invisibility Toggle can make you almost invincible, and he thinks that's fine "because there's more to this game than just fighting." Presets change many options at once.
- **Standard options:** named custom control profiles (Smash does the same because many players switch in and out); holding the controller sideways or upside down; swapping sticks. Remaps are confirmed with a long press so short presses can still navigate the menu, and each action's default button is shown so players can revert. Controls are split by context (aiming, swimming, minigames, menus). There are DualSense trigger and vibration settings, including a cutscene-vibration toggle; reticle and HUD options with graphical aids; subtitles with size, background, speaker names, name colors, speaker direction and color; and a quality/performance mode, high frame rate, HDR calibration showing a dark and a bright scene, dynamic range and audio balance presets.
- Early Nintendo products gained something from a fixed control scheme that couldn't be remapped. But modern controllers aren't simple and players come with diverse mindsets and experiences, so they need to be able to configure the game. Even adopting just the features you need is worthwhile.

## Do this
- Add a dedicated Accessibility menu that also links relevant options from other menus, and give visual settings like HUD size a live preview window.
- Put options that lower difficulty (invincibility, slow motion, grab protection, puzzle skipping) behind an explicit unlock step, and offer presets that switch on groups of options at once.
- Make remapping safe: confirm reassignments with a long press so short presses still navigate, show each action's default button next to it, and let players save named profiles.
- Include a separate toggle for controller vibration during cutscenes.
- On brightness/HDR calibration screens, show both a dark and a bright reference scene, and let players adjust such settings during play to compare.
- Offer subtitle options for size, background, speaker names (with colors) and speaker direction, plus motion-sickness options such as reduced screen motion and a persistent center dot.

## Avoid
- Fixed, non-remappable controls on modern, complex controllers.
- Difficulty-lowering options that a player can switch on by accident.
- Cutscene vibration that can't be turned off, which sets off a controller left on the table.

## Examples from the episode
- **The Last of Us Part I — HUD size:** a preview window shows exactly what each option does.
- **The Last of Us Part I — High Contrast Display:** swiping the touchpad makes enemies, allies and items stand out.
- **The Last of Us Part I — Combat Accessibility:** locked options and an Invisibility Toggle that can make you almost invincible.
- **The Last of Us Part I — control remapping:** long-press confirmation, and the default button shown to the left of each heading.
- **Super Smash Bros. — named control settings:** done because many players switch in and out.
- **Nintendo Switch HD Rumble:** Sakurai's own experiment matching vibrations to the frequency of impact sounds.
