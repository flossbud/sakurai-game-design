# 200 · Presenting Scores

**Category:** Planning & Game Design · [Video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FeekeBW26AI) · [Transcript](../transcripts/200-presenting-scores.md)

## In one sentence
Don't take the score for granted: give it a meaningful unit of measurement, such as an age, money or a power rating, and present each increase so that earning it feels great.

## Key ideas
- Early games rewarded effort mainly with a score, and they "just wouldn't have been much fun without a high score to shoot for."
- As games grew in scope, progress became the main goal and scores mattered less. Experience, gold, skills, items and online leaderboards now provide the incentive, and outside "a particular demographic" few players chase high scores.
- High scores should still feel as good as possible, because having something to strive for is what makes games fun.
- **Brain Age** calls your score your "Brain Age," which changes the meaning of the whole game. You naturally improve with practice because it is a game, but the game presents that improvement as if it means something real. **Wii Fit Age** works the same way, and there a lower score makes you happier.
- Tecmo's "Game Deviation Value" (Mighty Bomb Jack, Solomon's Key) appeared at game over. It was never clear how it was calculated, and 50 didn't seem to be the median, but it felt like more than a regular score.
- Burnout's Crash Mode scores you in Crash Dollars. Making the score concrete makes the experience feel more real, and the same idea of counting monetary damage shows up in kaiju games and other thrilling driving games.
- Smash Bros. has Global Smash Power. A line like "My Global Smash Power is in the millions!" is meant to get you hyped.
- The trick is that every one of these variations is still built on a unit of measurement. Even a conventional score deserves careful thought about presentation, such as counting the increase up point by point.

## Do this
- Choose a unit of measurement that fits your game's fantasy (an age, a currency, damage caused, a power rating) and show the score in that unit instead of as bare points.
- Pick the direction that suits the unit. For an "age," lower is better, as with Wii Fit Age.
- Aim for a number players will want to say out loud, as in "My Global Smash Power is in the millions!"
- At game over, consider showing a framed evaluation, as Tecmo's Game Deviation Value did, in addition to the raw total.
- Even with a conventional score, animate each gain point by point on screen so earning it feels better.

## Avoid
- Dropping in a plain score counter by default without asking how it should be presented.

## Examples from the episode
- **Brain Age:** the score is presented as your brain's age, which gives practice-based improvement an apparently real meaning.
- **Wii Fit:** "Wii Fit Age," where a lower number makes you happier.
- **Mighty Bomb Jack / Solomon's Key (Tecmo):** a mysterious "Game Deviation Value" at game over that felt like more than a score.
- **Burnout — Crash Mode:** the score is counted in Crash Dollars, so a concrete unit makes the crashes feel real.
- **Smash Bros. — Global Smash Power:** a big, brag-worthy number.

## Quote
> The trick is that each variation is still based on a unit of measurement. At the very least, you shouldn't take the idea of scores for granted. (03:17)
