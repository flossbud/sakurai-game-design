# 127 · Arranging Music

**Category:** Audio · [Video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qahwb7_67f0) · [Transcript](../transcripts/127-arranging-music.md)

## In one sentence
When commissioning arrangements, give composers clear gameplay-driven rules, let them pick pieces they connect with, review through a sound director, and never lose the original melody.

## Key ideas
- For Smash Bros., Sakurai first picks candidate songs from a wide variety of titles, many more than will actually be arranged, then gathers the composers to give instructions.
- The brief: tempo and energy must suit a competitive game. Since "3, 2, 1, GO!" plays at the start of each match, an intro is good if possible, or the music can start after the countdown. Older pieces can be short, so mixing in another song with the main theme works well. The game is highly dynamic, so shifts in the music should be big, and the piece should build to a climax at the end. The loop point can go before or after the intro. Timed matches last two minutes (two and a half in Ultimate), so an arrangement should ideally finish within that with just a little loop.
- Composers then choose pieces. Ideally they arrange something they have an attachment to; failing that, something they think they'd do well. When he picks for them, he chooses based on their strengths, because even composers who can handle anything have areas where they excel most.
- He checks every arrangement through a few rounds of review and brush-ups, sending feedback through Smash Bros. Ultimate's sound director: he may know what he wants but lack the musical language to say it, and going through the director gets the best results. His most common feedback covers tempo, intonation, mistranscriptions of the original score and the arrangement's general direction.
- After buying many game-music arrangement CDs and being disappointed when pieces came out totally different, he decided the main melody must stay the same, to the point that humming the arrangement is like humming the original. Imagine the Star Wars theme changing with each film. Listeners come for this one song out of many, so keep its identity even if it feels antiquated to you.

## Do this
- Write an arranging brief based on gameplay: tempo and energy for the mode, an intro that covers any start countdown, big dynamic shifts, a build to a climax, and a length that fits a typical match (Smash: 2 or 2.5 minutes) with only a little loop.
- Shortlist many more candidate tracks than you will use.
- Let each composer pick a piece they're attached to or confident with; otherwise assign one that suits their strengths.
- Run several review rounds that check tempo, intonation, transcription accuracy against the original, and overall direction.
- If you lack musical vocabulary, route your feedback through a sound director who can put it into musical terms.
- Keep the original main melody intact so someone humming the arrangement is humming the original.

## Avoid
- Arrangements that change a piece so much it loses its identity.
- Rewriting a melody just because the original feels old-fashioned to you.

## Examples from the episode
- **Super Smash Bros. — music arrangements:** the "3, 2, 1, GO!" countdown sets the intro rule; two-minute timed matches (2.5 in Ultimate) set the target length.
- **Super Smash Bros. Ultimate — sound director:** all of Sakurai's arrangement feedback goes through him.
- **Game music arrangement CDs:** often disappointing when the piece came out totally different.
- **Star Wars theme:** an analogy for why a familiar melody shouldn't change.

## Quote
> Even if the original feels antiquated to you, and even if you have the urge to change it, make sure you maintain the piece's identity. (03:39)
