# 028 · Too Much is Just Right

**Category:** Animation · [Video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zNBKzLzDKtM) · [Transcript](../transcripts/028-too-much-is-just-right.md)

## In one sentence
Exaggerate attack poses past what looks "OK," pushing rigs to their limits and overshooting before settling, because on a small screen merely adequate motion reads as weak.

## Key ideas
- Kazuya's Roundhouse to Triple Spin Kick twists his body so far it could look like a bug. Animation that pushes the rig's weighting to the breaking point is good animation; it was like that in the original game, Sakurai approved it for Smash, and he says they should have gone even further.
- In attack animation there's almost no such thing as "too far." The usual problem is "this isn't anywhere near far enough," because it's a small thing on a small screen. If you think a pose is OK, you still have room to push it.
- In the first Smash (or the Dragon King prototype), arms were rigged directly to the torso and only their angle could change, so adequate animation wasn't possible; real people move their shoulders forward and back. Modern tools allow much more flexible rigs.
- People sometimes ask for corrections when a model looks warped in a paused frame, but making them kills the energy of the animation.
- Virtua Fighter, said to be the first 3D fighting game, already had polished animation in which the upper and lower body sometimes face opposite directions. Staying within what's physically possible isn't always best.
- For linear (frame-by-frame) animation: go past the natural limit, then pull back a bit. He has done this since the NES: in Kirby's Adventure's Needle ability, frame three goes too far and frame four pulls back.

## Do this
- Whenever an attack pose looks "OK," push it further (more twist, more reach, bigger extension) until the rig's weighting is close to breaking.
- Judge attack animations at their real in-game size on the target screen, since they are small on screen.
- Rig characters so shoulders can move forward and back and the upper and lower body can rotate independently, giving animators room to exaggerate.
- Turn down correction requests for poses that only look warped in a paused frame, as long as they read well in motion.
- In frame-by-frame or sprite animation, add one overshoot frame past the target pose, then pull back to the pose on the next frame.

## Avoid
- Stopping at "OK"; it means there's still room to go further.
- Keeping poses within what's physically possible.
- "Fixing" deformation seen only in freeze-frames, which kills the animation's energy.

## Examples from the episode
- **Super Smash Bros. — Kazuya's Roundhouse to Triple Spin Kick:** an extreme body twist carried over from his original game; Sakurai says they should have pushed it further.
- **Super Smash Bros. — an uppercut:** a demo comparing an uppercut that "isn't enough" with a far more exaggerated version.
- **Dragon King / the first Super Smash Bros.:** arms locked to the torso, only angles changeable, so adequate animation wasn't possible.
- **Virtua Fighter:** upper and lower body sometimes face opposite directions, and it looks polished.
- **Kirby's Adventure — Needle ability:** frame three overshoots, frame four pulls back.

## Quote
> If you're animating an attack, the best way to think of it is that "too much is just right." (00:38)
