# 032 · Strike a Balance with Sound Effects

**Category:** Audio · [Video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qKjD5qbLUeI) · [Transcript](../transcripts/032-strike-a-balance-with-sound-effects.md)

## In one sentence
Set each sound effect's volume by how relevant it is to the player, not by how big the action is, building a clear priority order from match-deciding events down to footsteps.

## Key ideas
- Sound effects play almost constantly. Besides choosing realistic or exaggerated sounds, you must decide how loud, flashy and discernible each one is; for example, Link's footsteps and his sword swing are both quiet, so which comes first?
- His rule: the more relevant a sound is to the player, the louder it should be.
- Smash Bros. priority, loudest first (subtle effects omitted): the KO sound, because it's crucial to push and pull and the match outcome; impact sounds when you hit or get hit, because they're direct feedback (perfect shields share this level); special-move activation sounds, because they carry each fighter's character, with volume varying by the move's strength. Voices get the same priority as specials, but clear speech cuts through other sounds, so they need balancing. Explosions sit midway between impacts and specials, louder the bigger they are.
- Lower down: dodges, shield blocks and ledge grabs (they require conditions and signal success); then attack swings without impact and stick-flick dashes (feedback for button presses, so still a bit audible); then jumps and landings, with landings louder because they're a reaction; then walking, which is also feedback for input.
- Landing sounds match each fighter's clothing or footwear (Cloud's change with his costume), every fighter has unique footsteps, and repeated sounds come in several variations so they don't sound mechanical.
- Footsteps are barely audible in many 3D games, which makes characters feel like they're hovering. Different floor materials need different walking and landing sounds, which is a pain, but believable sounds feel best.
- Prioritize by each sound's importance to the game and to the player character; judging only by the size of the action can give the wrong result.

## Do this
- List every sound effect and rank it by relevance to the player, then set volumes in that order. For a fighter, Smash uses: KO > hits and perfect shields > special-move activations and voices (explosions between hits and specials) > dodges, blocks and ledge grabs > attack swings and dashes > landings > jumps > walking.
- Balance voice lines carefully: give them the priority of special moves, but remember clear speech cuts through other sounds.
- Make landings louder than jumps, and match landing and footstep sounds to the character's footwear or costume and to the floor material.
- Record several variations of frequently repeated sounds (footsteps, landings) and rotate between them so they don't sound mechanical.
- Make footsteps clearly audible in 3D games so characters feel grounded instead of hovering.
- Scale the volume of special moves and explosions with their strength and size.

## Avoid
- Setting a sound's volume only by the physical size of the action.
- Playing the same sample over and over for repeated actions.
- Footsteps so quiet the character seems to hover.

## Examples from the episode
- **Link — walking vs. swinging his sword:** both are quiet sounds, raising the question of which to prioritize.
- **Super Smash Bros. — volume hierarchy:** KO loudest, then impacts and perfect shields, specials and voices, explosions, dodges and ledge grabs, swings and dashes, landings, jumps, walking.
- **Super Smash Bros. — Cloud's landing sounds:** they differ by costume, audible on good speakers.
- **3D games in general — footsteps:** often barely audible, so characters feel like they're hovering.
