# 025 · Make the Tempo Match the Game

**Category:** Audio · [Video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hGp2Jw5dLH8) · [Transcript](../transcripts/025-make-the-tempo-match-the-game.md)

## In one sentence
Choose music whose tempo (BPM) matches the speed of the action and the mood of each scene, and treat tempo as a deliberate design decision.

## Key ideas
- This episode opens the Audio category: games produce music, sound effects, voices and more, and each needs thought.
- Tempo is how quickly beats occur, measured in BPM (beats per minute): one clap per second is 60 BPM, two claps per second is 120 BPM. Some tempos fit a game's content and others don't. Much of it comes down to taste, but you should always be mindful of it.
- Smash Bros. stage music arranged for battle generally runs a little over 120 BPM, ranging from 130 to 170 BPM on average. Some tracks reach around 200 BPM, but those are exceptions, not the rule.
- Even among similar fighting games, the right tempo varies with the speed of the action. Within a single game, different scenes call for different tempos and rhythms.
- Tempo isn't only the composer's concern; anyone making the game should keep it in mind.
- In the first Dragon Quest, the music's tempo gets slower the deeper you go into a dungeon. He thinks it's very effective but probably hard to implement, which is why it's rarely seen.

## Do this
- Clap along to each candidate track and count beats per minute (one clap per second = 60 BPM, two = 120 BPM), and note the BPM next to the scene it will play in.
- For fast battle scenes, start from roughly 130–170 BPM (the Smash battle range) and keep tempos near 200 BPM as rare exceptions.
- Set tempo by how fast your game's action actually is, not by genre: a slower-paced fighter needs a slower tempo than a fast one.
- Give different scenes in the same game their own target tempo and rhythm instead of one tempo throughout.
- Consider tying tempo to game state, such as slowing the music as the player goes deeper into a dungeon, if you can afford to implement it.

## Examples from the episode
- **Super Smash Bros. — battle stage music:** arranged for battle at a little over 120 BPM, 130–170 BPM on average, with a few exceptions near 200 BPM.
- **Dragon Quest (the first game) — dungeon music:** the tempo slows the deeper you delve into a dungeon; effective, but rarely seen because it's hard to implement.
