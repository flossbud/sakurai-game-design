# 187 · Staying True to Your Concept

**Category:** Work Ethic · [Video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2LvPC7bIywM) · [Transcript](../transcripts/187-staying-true-to-your-concept.md)

## In one sentence
Fix a clear concept before production starts and steer by it like a lighthouse for the whole project. Bring out emergent fun along the way, but don't change direction midway, or you'll waste your team's work and get lost.

## Key ideas
- Game production is long, and once development fatigue sets in it's easy to start wondering what's supposed to be fun about the game. That's exactly when you must never lose sight of your original concept.
- A concept is what gives a game its appeal and direction. Simple examples: the fun of sending opponents flying, a fleshed-out narrative in an elaborate story structure, or just the thrill of sprinting down a straight path.
- Even if the game isn't turning out as expected, Sakurai doesn't recommend changing direction midway. The concept is like a lighthouse beam: "come storm or dark of night", keep heading toward it. Change course and you'll get lost, and things only get worse from there.
- You can pick up on emergent fun during development and adjust to bring it out further, which is fairly common. You can also scrap what doesn't work and start fresh, but that risks wasting assets, such as things artists and programmers spent months on. That's costly and demoralizes the team.
- The team, or at least the director, should never push a project forward until the lighthouse is clearly in sight.
- With many new projects, the team won't see the game's appeal until they start making it. Design docs struggle to convey the fun, and people have told him after a project shipped, "Now I see what you were getting at."
- Imagine the proposal for an existing hit game: you can't fully convey its fun on paper, and unplanned elements often end up as a core source of enjoyment. That's why the director must stay locked on the original direction and keep the team from getting lost. Stay the course, and you'll make full use of what the team creates.

## Do this
- Before production starts, write the concept as one clear statement of the game's appeal (for example, "the fun of sending opponents flying"), and don't move into production until it's that clear.
- When fatigue or doubt sets in during development, check decisions against the concept instead of changing direction.
- Watch for emergent fun during development and adjust to amplify it, as long as it still heads toward the concept.
- Before scrapping a part of the game and starting over, count the months of art and programming that would be thrown away and the hit to team morale.
- As director, keep restating the concept to the team, because many members won't feel the appeal from design docs until the game is playable.

## Avoid
- Changing direction midway because the game isn't turning out as expected.
- Starting full production before the concept is clearly in sight.
- Expecting a design document alone to make the team understand the fun.

## Examples from the episode
- **Sample concepts:** sending opponents flying, a fleshed-out narrative in an elaborate story structure, sprinting down a straight path.
- **Post-release feedback:** team members told Sakurai after projects shipped, "Now I see what you were getting at."
- **Thought experiment:** imagining the proposal for an existing hit game shows that fun can't be fully conveyed on paper.

## Quote
> Come storm or dark of night, the one thing you should never change is your trajectory toward that light. (01:03)
