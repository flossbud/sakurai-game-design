# 133 · Color-Coding Your Game

**Category:** UI · [Video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CWvLMK4OKko) · [Transcript](../transcripts/133-color-coding-your-game.md)

## In one sentence
If it suits your game's tone, give each menu section its own color and carry it into the submenus, because color and pictures are understood faster than text, but never mix a jumble of colors on one screen.

## Key ideas
- On projects Sakurai directs, menus are often set apart by color. In Smash Bros. Ultimate's main menu, "Smash" is red and all its submenus are themed red; "Spirits" is green and green dominates its submenus. He has used color this way for a long time.
- Color-based menus aren't always right. Lots of color can make a game feel aimed at younger audiences. Every game has its own tone and manner, and any color choice works as long as it matches the game's atmosphere.
- What he suggests avoiding in any game is a mishmash of colors on the same screen. Depending on the design quality and direction, it often turns into a mess.
- Smash for 3DS/Wii U's menus used large panels of character art, so they mixed many colors, but that doesn't bother him: with the art front and center, the images leave an imprint in your mind after enough play.
- Pictures are recognized much faster than text and parsed largely by intuition. Pictures and color convey more information than words: the left brain has to read words and then understand them, while the right brain can understand through visualization alone.
- Tone and manner come first. He used color because it fit those projects; every game has its own right approach.

## Do this
- Give each top-level menu category its own color and theme all of its submenus in that color, so players always know which section they're in.
- Put large character art or images on menu panels so players learn options by picture over time rather than by reading labels.
- Keep each screen to one dominant color theme instead of many competing colors.
- Set the palette's saturation to match your game's tone; tone it down if heavy color would make the game read as aimed at kids when that's not your audience.

## Avoid
- A mishmash of many colors on the same screen.
- Letting a color scheme override the game's tone and manner.

## Examples from the episode
- **Super Smash Bros. Ultimate — main menu:** "Smash" and its submenus are red; "Spirits" and its submenus are green.
- **Super Smash Bros. for 3DS/Wii U — menus:** large panels of character art; many colors, but the images become memorable and quick to recognize.
