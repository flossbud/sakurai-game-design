# 246 · Speedy Screen Transitions

**Category:** Design Specifics · [Video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9ic7LEHtekM) · [Transcript](../transcripts/246-speedy-screen-transitions.md)

## In one sentence
Make every screen transition as fast as you can without removing it, and when a transition has to cover loading, make it fit your game instead of leaving players on a slow fade and a black screen.

## Key ideas
- Making players wait is a sin. It isn't an unbreakable rule, but it's almost impossible to make a transition too fast. Slow fadeouts followed by long black screens are stretches where the player has no control, which robs them of their time.
- "A hasty speed is just right." Removing transitions entirely would feel jarring, so keep them, and make sure they finish quickly.
- Many games use transitions to hide loading even when no loading icon appears. The doors in the original Resident Evil masked loading and also built fear, and they took a little longer to open when zombies were on the other side. There's always a way to make a loading cover fit your game.
- The first Legend of Zelda scrolls the screen when you reach an edge. That makes a fixed camera easier to play with and shows how the map pieces connect. It suited its era, but today the same idea could scroll more smoothly and quickly. Do what's right for your game, and don't waste the player's time.
- Full-screen transitions are called "wipes." Most are simple fades, but a wipe that matches your game's theme and keeps an acceptable speed is a good trick. Super Mario World's key transition adds mystery and a sense of entering somewhere. Smash Bros. Ultimate uses random wipes to go to the victory screen.

## Do this
- Time every transition in your game (fade-out, black screen, fade-in) and cut each one down to the fastest version that still doesn't feel abrupt.
- Keep a short transition rather than none at all, so scene changes don't feel jarring.
- Where a transition hides loading, turn it into an in-world moment that adds mood (a door opening, a themed wipe) rather than a plain black screen. Resident Evil even slowed its doors when zombies waited behind them.
- For room-to-room movement with a fixed camera, scroll between screens so players see how the areas connect, and make that scroll fast and smooth.
- Swap generic fades for wipes that match your game's theme. For a screen players see often, such as a results screen, consider picking from several wipes at random.

## Avoid
- Slow, dreary fadeouts followed by lengthy black screens.
- Cutting transitions out entirely, which makes scene changes feel jarring.

## Examples from the episode
- **Resident Evil (original) — doors:** hide loading times while creating a sense of fear, and open a little slower when zombies are on the other side.
- **The Legend of Zelda (first game) — edge-of-screen scrolling:** makes a fixed camera easier to play with and shows how the map fits together.
- **Super Mario World — using a key:** the transition adds mystery and the sense that you've gone in somewhere.
- **Super Smash Bros. Ultimate — victory screen:** random wipes are used to transition into it.

## Quote
> It's a sin for a game to make players wait. I can't say this is an unbreakable rule, but it's almost impossible to make things too fast. (00:10)
