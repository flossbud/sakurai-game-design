# 056 · Flash, Blast, and Smoke

**Category:** Effects · [Video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZDopYzDX-Jg) · [Transcript](../transcripts/056-flash-blast-and-smoke.md)

## In one sentence
Build explosions in stages (an instant flash, a crisp blast, smoke that takes over, and a natural conclusion) instead of a single burst of fire, and fit each variant to where and how it goes off.

## Key ideas
- It's tempting to spit out a blast of fire and call it done. Star Force (Tecmo, then called Tehkan; arcade, 1984) used a five-frame explosion: a flash of light that turns to smoke and disappears. That was very well made for its time, and few shoot 'em ups put that much thought into the detail.
- **Flash:** Smash Bros. explosions come in many variations but follow the same rules. They start with an instant flash of light, which immediately tells players that something intense happened. The light rays are long compared with the actual blast range. Pure white is hard to see on white backgrounds, so black elements are mixed in. A shockwave and a flare are added too.
- **Blast:** a flashy roar of flames leaping out from the center. It's the key part of the explosion, so it has to stay crisp, with sparks mixed in that keep the momentum. How long the hit registers varies by game, but it doesn't last for the whole blast.
- **Smoke and conclusion:** black smoke takes over while the flames are drawn waning. For pure gameplay, clearing the smoke instantly would be better, but a shorter explosion lacks impact, and letting it reach its natural end shows players where it happened. Sparkle effects flicker one after another.
- **Extras:** a whole-screen flash or screen shake adds punch. Shake in particular changes how an explosion feels, so choose effects that fit. Some explosions get separate ground and air versions: on the ground, Young Link's bomb blast spreads upward and its shockwave follows the floor. Other types, like a smart bomb's slow, long spread, each need their own know-how.

## Do this
- Sequence every explosion as flash, then blast, then smoke, then conclusion, rather than a single fire sprite.
- Make the opening flash instantaneous with light rays reaching well past the real blast radius, mix in dark elements so it reads on white backgrounds, and add a shockwave and flare.
- Keep the damaging hit active for only part of the blast, not the whole visual duration.
- Let the smoke run its natural course so players can see where the explosion happened, even though clearing it instantly would be cleaner for gameplay.
- Make a grounded variant whose blast spreads upward and whose shockwave hugs the ground, separate from the airborne version.
- Add a screen flash or screen shake only where it suits that particular explosion.

## Avoid
- A single burst of fire standing in for the whole explosion.
- A pure white flash that disappears against white backgrounds.
- Cutting the explosion short, which loses impact and hides where it happened.

## Examples from the episode
- **Star Force (arcade, 1984) — explosion:** five frames, flash to smoke to gone, well made for its era.
- **Super Smash Bros. — standard explosion:** flash with long rays and black accents, a crisp blast with sparks, black smoke, a natural conclusion and flickering sparkles.
- **Super Smash Bros. — Young Link's bombs:** on the ground, the blast spreads upward and the shockwave follows the surface.
- **Smart bomb:** a slow, long spread that needs its own approach.

## Quote
> Letting the explosion reach its natural conclusion and showing players where it took place is vital. (02:11)
