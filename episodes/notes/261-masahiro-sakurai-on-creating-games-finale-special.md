# 261 · Masahiro Sakurai on Creating Games: Finale Special

**Category:** Special · [Video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_qPqBilhEtI) · [Transcript](../transcripts/261-masahiro-sakurai-on-creating-games-finale-special.md)

## In one sentence
When you take on a big creative project next to your main job, plan and script all of it up front, prove it with a pilot, favor speed and brevity over perfection, clear reviews the day they arrive, and remember that shared knowledge only has meaning when people use it.

## Key ideas
- **Why it happened then.** In July 2021 Sakurai was busy on Smash Ultimate DLC (the "Mr. Sakurai Presents Kazuya" video, with Sora in development) and wrote a new game proposal "at lightning speed." After Sora released in late October 2021, the new project was approved, but its team wouldn't be assembled until about April 2022. Directors stay on projects longer than anyone and usually go straight from one job to the next, so a gap of several months was rare. Instead of resting, he treated it as a once-in-a-lifetime chance, because he's old enough to think about retirement and adversity can strike: "do what needs doing while I still have the opportunity."
- **Why a channel.** After going freelance from HAL Laboratory in 2003, he felt he didn't need to make games himself; the games he makes aren't necessarily ones he wants to play (Kirby for beginners though he's a proficient gamer, Meteos despite being bad at puzzle games). He works in games because he's suited to it, and wanted to help the industry as a whole by exchanging knowledge with many people. Supervising other teams had an impact that was hard to measure, and making a game himself to show how it's done might have had more. Lectures at schools were inefficient: lots of prep and travel for a one-off event reaching a few hundred people at best, and pointless if the audience isn't interested. Conferences have the same drawbacks, and not many people read his books. So he chose a lasting archive people can access whenever they like.
- **Scripts first.** Starting around November 2021 he wrote scripts before anything else. Ad-libbing risked losing track or veering off topic, he needed to balance the overall content, and the scripts would also serve subtitling, translation and checks. Process: a few hundred one-line topic ideas drawn from his old Famitsu columns and development memories (most became titles), sorted into categories such as Work Ethic, Game Essence, Graphics and Team Management with a set video count each, some topics removed on review, then outlines and a script for every heading. Every script except the finale was written before production, so the episode count and content were fixed from the start.
- **Why everything in advance.** For a director working on games of his scale, running a channel and a game at the same time would be a tall order, so planning all content up front was the way to raise his chances of success. The cost: he couldn't take viewer requests or change course based on comments.
- **Speed over polish.** With limited time he "just wrote," about a month for everything, roughly 12 scripts a day. Some places could have said more or invited misunderstandings, but over-preventing that would have made things less succinct. "Short and snappy! Speed above all else!", which he calls the secret to a good presentation too. The plan was 256 episodes, plus three unplanned Retro Game Master crossovers and the finale for 260 total, about 300,000 Japanese characters (roughly the text of Tactics Ogre: Reborn). He spread the categories evenly into a release order in an Excel sheet, which then doubled as the schedule and progress tracker.
- **Pilot, then permission.** Before full recording he made a pilot of three real episodes (C03 "Keeping Rewards in Sight," C11 "Praise the Player!", D03 "Jump Physics"), edited at Sora with game footage, to test whether he could really do it. He then took it to Nintendo to make sure he wasn't infringing on their development interests and to get permission for Smash Ultimate development footage. Nintendo checked the scripts and videos as needed but almost never asked for changes.
- **Recording alone.** He recorded at his secondary residence with no crew and no way to preview, only at night (daylight made the footage too uneven, even with the blinds down) and never in rain (the sound). He recorded up to a few dozen episodes a session in category order, using a Sony ZV-1 and, after trying pin and stand mics, a directional mic. His first day, about thirty episodes, was ruined by radio interference, fixed with a ferrite core on the cable. Passing motorcycles and ambulances forced stops. He then trimmed flubs, locked each episode's approximate length, and revised the scripts to match what he had actually said, since they fed checks, subtitles and translation.
- **Footage and organization.** He stockpiled game footage per episode (over 200 clips for the Smash stages episode, over 300 counting rejects), dropping anything like Sonic footage that would need extra Sega approvals. Category letters and episode codes became folder names, and scripts, videos and footage went into a database. Deciding every episode in advance made this organization possible.
- **Partners.** In February 2022 he hired HIKE (then called QBIST) to add footage, images, music and subtitles, since just him talking would be lower impact and harder to grasp. They were suspicious the email was really him until they saw his pilot; their own pilot proved they could do it, at a higher cost than he expected. HIKE came up with the Grab Bag gesture visuals with no instruction ("it would've been strange if I had dictated every last detail"), made the thumbnails and used licensed music and sound effects; Yuzo Koshiro wrote the opening and ending jingles. Editing took the longest of anything. 8-4 handled English translation (he mostly left it to them) and sometimes supplied English footage; he'd planned a Japanese-only channel but decided to reach as many people as possible.
- **Daily routine.** Up around 8 AM to write a daily report to his game team, game direction all day with no breaks until about 6:30 PM, then channel work from 8 PM (reviews and posting, hence the 8 PM Japan time uploads, plus screenshots and end cards). HIKE sent up to four or five videos to check on heavy days. Deliveries used to arrive around 11 PM and wrecked his sleep, so they set a rule: anything to review that day had to arrive by 8 PM. He clears every review the same day: "Putting them off is just asking for trouble." Each episode got about six to eight rounds of feedback across video and thumbnail, sent as email bullet points; when a video needed new game footage, he captured it himself. He re-recorded voiceover only as a last resort, because the new room sounded different. Bed between 1 and 2 AM, about six hours of sleep. He launched at three videos a week, cut to two, and kept it up for over two years (three with prep); each video took about three months start to finish.
- **Reception.** He only had a vague sense of how it landed. Off-topic or mocking comments were discouraging, since they meant the point didn't get through, though they also showed the channel reached a wide audience. Companies and developers told him it helped, it won two top prizes at the 2023 CEDEC Awards, and almost every video in both languages had 99.5% Likes or more, the only concrete numbers he had and something that sustained him.
- **Cost.** About 90 million yen in total, mostly HIKE's editing ("This is what it means to hire people!"), plus about 220,000 yen in licensing for the PRESS START video and about 1.5 million yen for the three Retro Game Master crossovers. That's almost 350,000 yen per episode, and some episodes cost over 1 yen per view. It excludes ideation, scripting, recording, supervision and management, which he and Sora covered, and which need an accomplished person leading to be persuasive. The channel isn't monetized and earned nothing; he calls it closer to a donation or volunteer work than an investment. Since it would be tough to make a modern game with just 100 million yen, rather than add one more title to the flood of releases, he hoped to give all games a small quality boost.
- **Purpose.** He wanted to leave his knowledge in a form other than just another game, while he still can. The channel is a guide for developers, deeper knowledge for players, and a reference on workplace rules and philosophies for professionals. Its sole purpose is to give viewers a "boost": if nobody uses it, it might as well not exist. He has no plans for more episodes and asks people not to request topics, though category compilations continue.

## Do this
- Before starting a large content project next to your main job, plan the whole thing: brainstorm one-line topics, group them into categories with a fixed count each, cut the weak ones, then outline and write every piece before production begins.
- Make a pilot of two or three real pieces at final quality to test whether you can actually deliver, and use it to pitch partners and get permissions before committing.
- Use one ID scheme everywhere (category letter plus episode code) for folders, files and a spreadsheet that holds an evenly spread release order and doubles as the progress tracker.
- Write fast and keep it short. Accept small imperfections instead of agonizing over every line.
- Set a daily delivery cutoff with collaborators (his was 8 PM for same-day review), clear every review the day it arrives, and send feedback as bullet-point lists.
- Drop non-essential content that needs extra third-party approvals, and leave playful creative details to trusted partners instead of dictating them.

## Avoid
- Ad-libbing long-form content without a script, which risks losing track or going off topic.
- Letting reviews pile up into the next day, or starting them late at night.
- Patching recorded voiceover in a different environment unless there's truly no alternative, since the change in sound is obvious.
