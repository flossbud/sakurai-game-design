[00:04](https://youtu.be/Icl-jMlz1iY?t=4) Game controls are complex. Helping new players master them can be extremely challenging. Many genres use familiar control schemes so that experienced players immediately know how to play— if anything, that's most genres.

[00:18](https://youtu.be/Icl-jMlz1iY?t=18) Still, game mechanics won't evolve if we always rely on such conventions.

[00:26](https://youtu.be/Icl-jMlz1iY?t=26) Whether or not your game has a unique control scheme, a tutorial is essential in order to break down the rules of the game for the player. But nobody wants to sit through a hand-holding tutorial, right? The worst thing you can do is make players read long paragraphs like an instruction manual.

[00:44](https://youtu.be/Icl-jMlz1iY?t=44) A better approach you should still avoid is having players learn the controls in an inorganic training area. That will just make them think, "Get on with it already! Give me the real game!" A relatively commo approach is posting text along the path in the opening stage, so players can read it, then put it into practice.

[01:03](https://youtu.be/Icl-jMlz1iY?t=63) That works well enough with simple controls, but it won't be a great fit for every genre. In Smash Bros., the only thing we include is a non-interactive video sequence. But this isn't because that's what we want to do— many times we've planned hands-on ways for players to learn the controls, only to not be able to implement them in the end.

[01:23](https://youtu.be/Icl-jMlz1iY?t=83) In fact, "Break the Targets!" from the first Smash Bros. was designed to help players master the controls. That's why it's different for each character. At any rate, I'd say it's vital that you let players play the game as you teach them. There are plenty of ways I could stand to improve in this area myself, but I also don't want to make players feel restricted, so it's quite the conundrum.

[01:49](https://youtu.be/Icl-jMlz1iY?t=109) As an aside, for Smash Bros. Ultimate, we had tried to implement a hands-on mode to teach players techniques, but we had to let it go due to the project's—or schedule's—needs. I still regret that, even now.

[02:02](https://youtu.be/Icl-jMlz1iY?t=122) Probably more than anything else. That's how strongly I feel about helping players understand a game's controls.
