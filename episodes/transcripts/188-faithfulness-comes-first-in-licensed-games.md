Licensed games, with some exceptions, have a bit of a reputation for not being as fun as “regular” games. People have been saying that since the NES and even before—since the 1980s, in other words. But if we're judging them solely on fun, I think it's only natural that licensed titles might not be able to measure up to the rest.

[00:37](https://youtu.be/EgUvH3YMVCM?t=37) First, we need to consider why licensed games are more difficult to make fun than regular games. There are numerous reasons for this, but one that stands out is that the source material restricts the game's design. Game stories have to present themselves differently than other kinds of narratives, and getting both kinds to mesh is a truly herculean task.

[00:59](https://youtu.be/EgUvH3YMVCM?t=59) It's difficult to change anything about the original's story and characters, and a story that follows the original has already been “spoiled." Though sometimes, the setting of the original work can form the basis for a very fun game. The prime example I'd like to show for this is the Megami Tensei series.

[01:20](https://youtu.be/EgUvH3YMVCM?t=80) The Persona series is descended from it. Megami Tensei was originally a novel. The concept of summoning demons from a computer to do battle was a perfect fit for a video game. Of course, it wouldn't have come together without the skill of the game developers.

[01:38](https://youtu.be/EgUvH3YMVCM?t=98) After all, fusing demons was an idea unique to the game.

[01:44](https://youtu.be/EgUvH3YMVCM?t=104) Getting back on topic, the next point to consider is what people want most from licensed games. It's faithfulness to the original work. That, and satiating fans' love for it.

And they respond best when those works are released at the right times. Whether a game has the right push and pull or whether it's fun aren't as much of a priority.
