[00:04](https://youtu.be/2LvPC7bIywM?t=4) Game production, generally speaking, is a long, involved process. When development fatigue sets in, it's easy to start wondering just what is supposed to be fun about the game. But remember: you should never stray from your original concept!

[00:22](https://youtu.be/2LvPC7bIywM?t=22) Maybe a better way to phrase it is to never lose sight of the concept.

[00:29](https://youtu.be/2LvPC7bIywM?t=29) When starting a project, you'll naturally need to come up with a concept. To give a simple example, it could be the fun of sending opponents flying. Or presenting a fleshed-out narrative in an elaborate story structure. Or maybe just the thrill of sprinting down a straight path.

[00:46](https://youtu.be/2LvPC7bIywM?t=46) In other words, the concept is what gives your game its appeal and direction. Even if your game isn't turning out quite how you expected, I don't recommend changing direction midway through development. A concept is like a beam of light from a lighthouse.

[01:03](https://youtu.be/2LvPC7bIywM?t=63) Come storm or dark of night, the one thing you should never change is your trajectory toward that light. You'll get lost if you do. And once you're lost, things will only get worse from there.

[01:16](https://youtu.be/2LvPC7bIywM?t=76) You can, however, pick up on emergent fun during the dev process and adjust to bring it out further. That's actually fairly common, I'd say. Or you can always just scrap what doesn't work and start fresh. But there's a chance that doing so will mean a lot of dev assets go to waste.

[01:35](https://youtu.be/2LvPC7bIywM?t=95) For example, you may have to discard things that artists and programmers spent months creating. You want to avoid this for a wide range of reasons. Not only will it cost a lot, but it will demotivate your team, too. The dev team— or at least the director— should never move forward with a project until that lighthouse is in clear sight, I'd say.

[02:01](https://youtu.be/2LvPC7bIywM?t=121) To be frank, with many new projects, the dev team won't know the game's appeal until they start making it. No matter how you explain the fun in design docs, it can be hard to grasp what's good about it. I've had people tell me after a project was finished, "Now I see what you were getting at."

[02:22](https://youtu.be/2LvPC7bIywM?t=142) You can understand this best if you imagine the proposal for an existing hit game. No matter how hard you try, you can't fully convey the fun of it on paper, and unplanned elements can often wind up a core source of enjoyability. That's why the director has to stay honed in on the project's original direction, and keep the rest of the team from getting lost.

[02:40](https://youtu.be/2LvPC7bIywM?t=160) As long as you stay the course, you'll be able to make full use of the resources your team creates.
