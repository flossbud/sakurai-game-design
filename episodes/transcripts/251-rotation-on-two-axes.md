[00:21](https://youtu.be/Mh7nTn9_r40?t=21) Rotation is commonly expressed in three ways: on the X axis, and last but not least, on the Z axis. Turning around a vertical line like this would be considered Y-axis rotation.

[00:38](https://youtu.be/Mh7nTn9_r40?t=38) But even if this is the way you want to turn, you shouldn't rotate around only the Y axis. Like how I moved my head earlier. When creating human movement, make it a point to rotate on two axes. Take a head turn, for example.

[00:57](https://youtu.be/Mh7nTn9_r40?t=57) You could look down, then turn. That's rotation on both the X axis and the Y axis. Or you could tilt to the side as you turn. That's rotation on both the Z axis and the Y axis.

[01:15](https://youtu.be/Mh7nTn9_r40?t=75) If I can skip over the details and just get to the point: when you're animating something that's not mechanical, remember to utilize this twisting two-axis rotation. Simply turning your wrist on one axis looks underwhelming—almost like a drill— but tilting it a bit before turning will make things more believable.

[01:36](https://youtu.be/Mh7nTn9_r40?t=96) Pretty straightforward, right? In actuality, the connected parts end up moving, too. The shoulder and collarbone work in tandem with the wrist, for example. When animating twisting motions— things like a character revving a motorcycle— the position of the shoulder and collarbone should move accordingly with that of the wrist.

[01:58](https://youtu.be/Mh7nTn9_r40?t=118) As for rotation speed, you might get something believable out of the function curves your 3D software generates, but I'd suggest taking control yourself and slightly offsetting the rotation of the two axes, as an example. Adding the occasional counter-motion might work as well.

[02:19](https://youtu.be/Mh7nTn9_r40?t=139) As you can likely guess by now, even rotations that finish in the same place can express drastically different personalities depending on how they reach that point. When turning your head to the side, for example, you can turn downward..

[02:37](https://youtu.be/Mh7nTn9_r40?t=157) upward... or directly sideways. They all look a bit different, right?

[02:54](https://youtu.be/Mh7nTn9_r40?t=174) I hope this video provided you with some helpful tips. Oh, and when animating a character's head, try to keep their eye movement in mind as well. Before the facial animations are finished, animators tend to fully turn the whole head, but once the eye line gets added in, that large of a head turn might just end up being a bigger movement than you want.
