[00:04](https://youtu.be/obnjR8KJ9nk?t=4) Of all the games I’ve made, the only title where we really needed to think about color coding was Meteos. Accordingly, there was one thing that was absolutely essential in order to call the game complete. Namely, making the game playable for those with color blindness.

[00:24](https://youtu.be/obnjR8KJ9nk?t=24) If your game is primarily built around color, solely relying on that will make it so that players with color blindness won't be able to play. Moreover, the blocks—or “Meteos"—in Meteos are depicted differently depending on the planet, so it wasn't as simple as thinking of things in terms of single colors.

[00:44](https://youtu.be/obnjR8KJ9nk?t=44) That's where a color plugin called the "color blindness filter" came in. These days, the functionality is included with Photoshop by default. First, I used this filter to check over the game. With Meteos, the result is...

[01:00](https://youtu.be/obnjR8KJ9nk?t=60) something like this. Here are all the Meteos together. This is what they look like to someone with protanopia-type color blindness. For those with color blindness, there are different ways the condition can manifest, but certain types of colors generally tend to look similar.

[01:21](https://youtu.be/obnjR8KJ9nk?t=81) With these colors in particular, I tried to give them significant differences in brightness. If the Meteos were too dark, they would get lost against the background color, and some Meteo designs required a bit of follow-up work— but I felt that the game needed this extra care, at the very least.

[01:44](https://youtu.be/obnjR8KJ9nk?t=104) It may seem like a given, but you really have to put a lot of effort into it. Even outside of puzzle games, other things like power-ups or objects you interact with in a stage can be difficult to tell apart. If you're making a game and this has slipped your mind, please consider making adjustments for those with different color perceptions.

[02:09](https://youtu.be/obnjR8KJ9nk?t=129) One more thing. I'd like to tell you about a trick developers use related to color. Specifically, checking art and other visuals in black and white. This is especially effective for background art.

[02:22](https://youtu.be/obnjR8KJ9nk?t=142) This makes it obvious which parts are light or dark, so it'll be clear which aspects stand out and which aspects don't. Background art shouldn't be more prominent than the foreground, for example, and this method can fix those problems before they truly begin.

[02:39](https://youtu.be/obnjR8KJ9nk?t=159) It's similar to how you're supposed to flip your artwork to find any inconsistencies.
