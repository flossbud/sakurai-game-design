[00:04](https://youtu.be/-aA_mjpCPMg?t=4) When you're playing a competitive game, it goes without saying that you'll have the most fun if it's anyone's match. If the skill gap between players is too large, you can't even call it a game. Yet in reality, that difference in ability can be painfully large.

[00:21](https://youtu.be/-aA_mjpCPMg?t=21) For that reason, some competitive games have implemented a sort of handicap system.

[00:28](https://youtu.be/-aA_mjpCPMg?t=28) An extreme example would be Mario Kart. As with just about every other racing game, the more you're trailing, the more speed boosts you'll receive— but Mario Kart also uses items to level the playing field. That does make it easy for the person in the lead to end up dethroned, so you can't quite call it fair.

[00:49](https://youtu.be/-aA_mjpCPMg?t=49) Some people dislike mechanics like this, but I'd say it's an absolute must if you're making a casual game. Games where the person with the most skill keeps winning every single time just aren't fun. It's one thing if you're playing in an online tournament, but in your average household, it's best to let different people take turns winning— and that's one tenet of casual games that likely won't be going away.

[01:14](https://youtu.be/-aA_mjpCPMg?t=74) By the way, when it comes to Smash Bros., we don't take those concerns into consideration. Even though the game leans more casual, I wanted the gameplay to reflect skill level, so we don't do things like increase the attack power of trailing players or reduce the knockback dealt to them. We do have fighter-specific handicap systems like Sephiroth's Winged Form, but those are part of the characters themselves.

[01:43](https://youtu.be/-aA_mjpCPMg?t=103) There's only one handicap system that applies to all players. Namely, that it's more difficult for the player in the lead to get a Smash Ball. The Final Smash Meter should charge faster if a player is losing, too. At the same time, the terrain and items can drastically impact who's winning or losing.

[02:00](https://youtu.be/-aA_mjpCPMg?t=120) In other words, chaotic, anything-goes content like Final Smashes, items, stages, and even Final Smash Meters are a great fit for a party game, I’d say. But let's put that aside.

[02:14](https://youtu.be/-aA_mjpCPMg?t=134) Personally speaking, I mainly intend for a competitive game like Smash Bros. to be played in a home environment. That means there's some ways in which it's not suited for online play. I actually noted this back in my proposaí for Smash Bros. Brawl. If you're playing at home with family or friends, you can talk out all your minor differences.

[02:37](https://youtu.be/-aA_mjpCPMg?t=157) If, for example, you're playing a brawler and someone keeps getting you with a cheap move, you can solve the problem by telling them to stop cheating and declaring the move off-limits. That can end up being part of the fun, or even an enjoyabie memory. So it's OK if there's a bit of weirdness there.

[02:58](https://youtu.be/-aA_mjpCPMg?t=178) When I'm playing against children or beginner players myself, I'll let them pick up the Smash Ball or land a couple of good hits on me. You might say these human adjustments are the most effective form of handicap there is. That said, I want to draw your attention to something you may have forgotten: Smash Bros. comes equipped with an automatic handicap setting.

[03:22](https://youtu.be/-aA_mjpCPMg?t=202) I'd be thrilled if more people tried this setting out, as well.
