[00:03](https://youtu.be/plPR6-Gqemc?t=3) Popular works often get sequels or further installments. But if you just make the same thing over again, you can expect your sales to be roughly halved. This can be especially tough for works that were on the unique side.

[00:19](https://youtu.be/plPR6-Gqemc?t=19) When it comes to games, there are quite a few sequels and multi-entry series out there. Naturally, there are a variety of reasons for this, but I'd say the number one reason is that the underlying systems have already been developed. With films, creating a sequel involves making almost entirely new content.

[00:38](https://youtu.be/plPR6-Gqemc?t=38) This is true no matter if you're making the first movie or the second. But for games, it's not until you’ve developed the underlying system and have a consistent structure that you can start creating actual content. This also includes things like development tools, and it can really take a lot of work to flesh it all out.

[01:00](https://youtu.be/plPR6-Gqemc?t=60) Hence, by developing a sequel, you can improve on the existing system— and either focus on expanding it, or get a large head-start on the actual content. Another way that making games is different from making films is that you can't just read a plot at the start of the project and know how it should end up.

[01:21](https://youtu.be/plPR6-Gqemc?t=81) Often you'll only discover what's fun about your game once it's finished. Sequels aren't just beneficial for marketing— they can provide all sorts of different advantages. Plus, games unlike anything people have seen before are more at risk of being canceled. There are far more titles than you'd think that never see the light of day.

[01:45](https://youtu.be/plPR6-Gqemc?t=105) As for Super Smash Bros., since the dev team changed with each game, we recreated the game system every time, even though they were sequels. Though, since Smash Bros. for 3DS/Wii U and Smash Bros. Ultimate had the same dev team, we were able to use a similar dev environment.

[02:02](https://youtu.be/plPR6-Gqemc?t=122) Back to what I was saying at the start. About how sequels tend to not sell as well. When people buy the first game in a series, they most likely do so because it looks fun. How well that first game is made will impact how much a sequel sells.

[02:19](https://youtu.be/plPR6-Gqemc?t=139) You don't know what a game's like until you play it, and if people have a bad time with one entry, they may just skip the next. Whether people play your game to the end or stop in the middle depends on its content—and on who's playing. When you put out a sequel, the people who liked your first game will ideally be eager to play more.

[02:41](https://youtu.be/plPR6-Gqemc?t=161) If it's on the same scale and has similar content to the last game, people might feel like they've already experienced it, and not want to play. But above all, the most vital thing is convincing players that it's more fun and impactful than its predecessor. What I can tell you as a creator is: creating a sequel will bring some advantages— but it won't sell much if it's not well-made, so make sure you give it everything you've got.

[03:12](https://youtu.be/plPR6-Gqemc?t=192) Personally speaking, I welcome the opportunity to play more of a fun game! And I'm grateful to the creators for their hard work.

[03:23](https://youtu.be/plPR6-Gqemc?t=203) As an aside... I don't have much of a preference between making sequels and original titles, myself. I’ve made new games, and I've also made sequels— and I believe there's meaning and value in both.

[03:38](https://youtu.be/plPR6-Gqemc?t=218) Whether or not I make a sequel depends on the wishes of the people who offer me work. What matters most is that I carry out my assignments to the best of my ability.
