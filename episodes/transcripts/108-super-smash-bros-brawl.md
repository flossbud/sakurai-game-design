[00:04](https://youtu.be/dXpFTHtlmCo?t=4) It was E3 2005, the day after the Wii was announced, when President Iwata approached me with an offer to develop a Smash Bros. title for the Wii. That offer and how I put the team together would take a long time to explain, so I'll save it for another time.

[00:18](https://youtu.be/dXpFTHtlmCo?t=18) But I'll say this, at least. I had already gone freelance, and had I turned the work down, they might have left Smash Bros. Melee's 26 fighters completely untouched and simply rereleased the game as-is for the Wii.

[00:31](https://youtu.be/dXpFTHtlmCo?t=31) That's what Mr. Iwata once told me.

[00:39](https://youtu.be/dXpFTHtlmCo?t=39) There were two especially key parts of the Smash Bros. Brawl concept. The first was online support. The second was a single-player mode that would take an appropriate amount of time to complete. Let's start with online support.

[00:54](https://youtu.be/dXpFTHtlmCo?t=54) The Wii had online functionality built into the console by default, so it was essential that we supported that. Personally speaking, though, I don't think online play and Smash Bros. are a very good fit for each other.

[01:08](https://youtu.be/dXpFTHtlmCo?t=68) One of the best parts of Smash Bros. is how players can become champions among their friends. But being subjected to online copetition can cause people to lose confidence, which isn't great. What's more, the game has to use fully synchronous communication. Compared to the asynchronous communication seen in games like Mario Kart, that's a major disadvantage.

[01:30](https://youtu.be/dXpFTHtlmCo?t=90) You're limited to playing with people as close to you as possible, so it works best sticking to not just your own country, but even your own local region within it. Plus, network lag tends to make the game a pretty choppy experience. Still, when it comes to technical issues, it doesn't really matter what I personally think.

[01:50](https://youtu.be/dXpFTHtlmCo?t=110) If my job is to do it, I'll do it. And you never know, it could be fun in its own way. In fact, when we got online play working, I remember thinking, "Wow, it's actually possible." The other key point was adding a relatively long single-player mode.

[02:10](https://youtu.be/dXpFTHtlmCo?t=130) We ended up giving that mode a subtitle: “The Subspace Emissary," and it made up a significant part of the final game. It had cutscenes and an engaging story, a map, sticker-based powerups, and a cinematic ending.

[02:24](https://youtu.be/dXpFTHtlmCo?t=144) It was quite the challenge turning a simple fighter like Smash Bros. into a satisfying single-player game. We also wanted to take the opportunity to flesh out the characters a little more. Most of the time in Smash, you're playing as a tiny fighter knocking other fighters around, but that's a bit of a waste for a game all about bringing various franchises together.

[02:45](https://youtu.be/dXpFTHtlmCo?t=165) I wanted to make sure each and every character had their moment in the spotlight. But as you can imagine, that would take a lot of work. Between stages, enemies, and cutscenes, it was almost like making another whole game. During the planning stages, I had hoped to have it developed by someone other than the main dev team, but we unfortunately couldn't find a suitable contractor to handle it.

[03:11](https://youtu.be/dXpFTHtlmCo?t=191) And so, everything but the cutsceneswas developed internally by the Smash Bros. Brawl team. By the way, this was the first time our dev team exceeded 100 people. Incidentally, we had to create separate jump and movement parameters specifically for Subspace Emissary. It didn't work just dropping fighters in with their specs from Versus Mode, so we made all sorts of adjustments.

[03:35](https://youtu.be/dXpFTHtlmCo?t=215) This meant that for every fighter we added, we had two sets of parameters to tune. All that work fell straight to me— which was a real challenge.

[03:48](https://youtu.be/dXpFTHtlmCo?t=228) There were other new elements included in Smash Bros. Brawl as well. First, we implemented Final Smashes. If the point was just to have a powerful attack, we could have simply made an item to accomplish that, but since we wanted to really boost the individuality of each character, everyone got their own unique move.

[04:06](https://youtu.be/dXpFTHtlmCo?t=246) It wasn't easy making all of them, but I'm glad each fighter got to show off a bit. We also introduced Assist Trophies. Poké Balls which summoned all sorts of Pokémon were already a thing, but these allowed us to bring in characters from other Nintendo titles as well.

[04:24](https://youtu.be/dXpFTHtlmCo?t=264) The fact that you summon them on the spot is also part of the game's push and puli. Another unique feature is the My Music option. With Mario, for example, each generation of players will have a different title they call their own, so the same naturally goes for music, too.

[04:40](https://youtu.be/dXpFTHtlmCo?t=280) It seemed wasteful to only have one song for each game world represented, so we adopted a system that let us implement a wide array of songs. This also gave me a chance to assemble a dream team of game composers. Another feature of Smash Bros. Brawl is the guest fighters from outside companies.

[04:56](https://youtu.be/dXpFTHtlmCo?t=296) Snake and Sonic both joined the fight. To be honest, though, that didn't have much impact on how I did my work. From my perspective, Nintendo, too, is an "outside company." And so, I approached the original creators, explained what I was hoping to do, and tried to find a middle ground between their games and Smash Bros.— same as always.

[05:22](https://youtu.be/dXpFTHtlmCo?t=322) Another thing: Smash Bros. Melee was a very fast-paced game, which made it extremely difficult to master the controls and made it too hard on new players.

[05:32](https://youtu.be/dXpFTHtlmCo?t=332) That high level of control was great for top players and spectators, but it was too harsh on casual players. It even gave some competitive players tendinitis. It was a virtue in some ways, but a pain point in others.

[05:47](https://youtu.be/dXpFTHtlmCo?t=347) The Wii, however, was a console targeted more towards casual players, so I felt we shouldn't make the game as hardcore as Smash Bros. Melee. We needed to make it playable with a sideways Wii Remote, too. Similarly, demanding controls would have proven a problem during online matches of the time.

[06:05](https://youtu.be/dXpFTHtlmCo?t=365) The higher the input density, the more frame skips would stand out. That's just how it worked at the time. So, we kept the game's speed a little slower. And we tuned it more toward casual gameplay.

[06:20](https://youtu.be/dXpFTHtlmCo?t=380) By the way, this was the first time we brought on play testers— people whose job it was to play the game and help us balance the fighters. That said, we only had four in total. They certainly weren't what you'd call "professional-level" players, and I was still solely in charge of tweaking fighter parameters.

[06:43](https://youtu.be/dXpFTHtlmCo?t=403) The Wii and DS eras were known for initiatives like Touch! Generations. In comparison, even a casual fighting game like ours must have felt like a steep learning curve, but we did what we had to so that as many people could enjoy it as possible. Excluding the combined sale numbers of Smash Bros. for Wii U/3DS, Smash Bros. Brawl is the second best-selling entry in the series.

[07:06](https://youtu.be/dXpFTHtlmCo?t=426) If we hadn't been able to make the game at all, that may have spelled the end of the Smash Bros. series as we know it, but that's a conversation for another time. Next up in the Game Concepts category of videos will be Kid Icarus: Uprising.
