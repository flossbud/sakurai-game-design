[00:03](https://youtu.be/cIB0BUe6Ihk?t=3) A follow-through is the motion after an attack during which the character is momentarily vulnerable. We sometimes call this the recovery period. For more details, take a look at my “Breaking Down Attack Animations" video. You might be thinking, "The part after the attack can't be that important."

[00:22](https://youtu.be/cIB0BUe6Ihk?t=22) However, this is the portion that occupies the most screen-time.

[00:29](https://youtu.be/cIB0BUe6Ihk?t=29) Take a competitive action game like Super Smash Bros. The more responsive an attack is, the better— so lead-ins move quickly into the actual attack. But assuming you want push and pull, strong attacks should leave a character proportionally vulnerable afterward, or the game will feel really unrefined.

[00:50](https://youtu.be/cIB0BUe6Ihk?t=50) To talk specifics, standard attacks generally finish in under a second. Even Smash Attacks with large vulnerability windows almost never exceed that one second barrier. The attack itself registers near the start of the animation— about a quarter to a third of the way in.

[01:09](https://youtu.be/cIB0BUe6Ihk?t=69) There are some faster attacks that even register a fifth of the way in. In other words, the whole rest of that time is follow-through! The time before the attack is short, while the time after is long. Now, I'd like to show you some Smash Bros. attacks, including when they register and when they finish.

[02:41](https://youtu.be/cIB0BUe6Ihk?t=161) There are cancel frames, too, so I made a point of highlighting where the recovery period ends. Some attacks stay active for multiple frames, but this should still give you an idea of how long vulnerability lasts after a normal attack. The recovery sticks around for a while, so you get plenty of time to see it.

[03:02](https://youtu.be/cIB0BUe6Ihk?t=182) To put it another way, the attack itself ends almost in an instant. Just a fraction of a second, really. That's why a follow-through's animation has to accurately convey exactly what the character was doing before that point.

[03:19](https://youtu.be/cIB0BUe6Ihk?t=199) While the follow-through does bring the character back to their standby pose, you shouldn't just interpolate straight back to it. Use that time to illustrate the weight of the attack action as clearly as possible. Keep it going as long as you can, and make the movements big— that'll help you show everything that happened in the attack before then.

[03:40](https://youtu.be/cIB0BUe6Ihk?t=220) You'll also want to assign cancel frames to a portion of the time it takes to return to standby. These offer the player a chance to regain control early. The timing of these cancel frames should fit with when people might expect they'll have control back. The balance of that is important, as well.

[04:01](https://youtu.be/cIB0BUe6Ihk?t=241) The follow-through after an attack determines the impact of the attack itself. Don't let it fizzle out—keep those actions expressive right to the end.
