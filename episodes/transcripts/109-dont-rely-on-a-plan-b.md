[00:04](https://youtu.be/QgkLrqGrwy4?t=4) When writing proposals or design docs, you might have doubts about which spec is the right one to proceed with.

[00:13](https://youtu.be/QgkLrqGrwy4?t=13) Between two potential choices, which will be easier to program? And which will feel more fun to the most people? It's tempting to approve of both options and leave the actual decision to someone else. That's why so many proposals and design docs say, “or we could do this," and leave both doors open."

[00:34](https://youtu.be/QgkLrqGrwy4?t=34) But with proposals in particular, you're better off ditching Plan B entirely. Leave out the hypotheticals and secondary ideas! Look at the whole picture, then choose the method you think will work best. Further suggestions can come later.

[00:54](https://youtu.be/QgkLrqGrwy4?t=54) We could do A. Or we could do B. I understand the temptation, but on a proposal, that won't cut it. There should always be an ideal spec to start, and from there you can course-correct to maximize cost effectiveness.

[01:07](https://youtu.be/QgkLrqGrwy4?t=67) That should be more than good enough. To accomplish this, you'll need to field plenty of suggestions from your team, and keep an open mind as to what they have to tell you. The proposal-writer should start with the ideal.

[01:22](https://youtu.be/QgkLrqGrwy4?t=82) If problems arise, you can then address them as a team. You may end up returning to what you thought of as Plan B, but in the proposal phase, it's important to stand by your idea and show you've really thought it through. This is a great way to build experience, too.

[01:41](https://youtu.be/QgkLrqGrwy4?t=101) On top of all that, game directors are often expected to think about programming and dev efficiency as well. Still, the proposal is the skeleton of your game. Don't twist it out of shape— make sure it stands tall and proud.
