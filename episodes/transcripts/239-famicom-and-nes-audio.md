[00:03](https://youtu.be/eyQSb_tC0Fg?t=3) In this Grab Bag-esque video, I'll be discussing the audio processor found in the Famicom*. I can't think of another example of such substantial evolution over the lifetime of a single platform.

[00:17](https://youtu.be/eyQSb_tC0Fg?t=17) The Famicom's audio processor consisted of two pulse wave channels, one triangle wave channel, one noise channel, and one DPCM channel.

[00:27](https://youtu.be/eyQSb_tC0Fg?t=27) ...That probably doesn't mean much to most of you, so let me explain. A long-wavelength sound will produce a lower pitch, while a short-wavelength sound will produce a higher one. If that wave looks like a castle's parapets, rising and falling digitally—that's called a “pulse wave." It makes a sort of piiii sound, crisp and unfaltering.

[00:50](https://youtu.be/eyQSb_tC0Fg?t=50) A beep, essentially. These were primarily how music was made on the Famicom, and by changing a wave's time ratios, you could change the texture of the sound. This is called the “duty cycle."

[01:14](https://youtu.be/eyQSb_tC0Fg?t=74) Next is the “triangle wave." Compared to a pulse wave, this one has a more mellow or muffled quality to its sound. It was commonly used for the bass line in a piece of music.

[01:29](https://youtu.be/eyQSb_tC0Fg?t=89) Technically speaking, these were actually pseudo-triangle waves on the Famicom. Next is "noise." This was used less for music, and more for effects such as hit noises, explosions, cheers, or waves. It sounds like tshhh tshhhh.

[01:58](https://youtu.be/eyQSb_tC0Fg?t=118) Naturally, both a game's music and sound effects had to be produced by these same five channels. When a sound effect was playing, a part of the music would often have to cut out in order to handle it. That leaves us with the "DPCM"—which, believe it or not, allowed for sampling. In other words, recording your own sounds and using them in the game.

[02:19](https://youtu.be/eyQSb_tC0Fg?t=139) Though of course, the sapling wasn't as clean as what you might hear nowadays. The most famous example of this is probably the timpani sound from Super Mariò Bros. 3.

[02:43](https://youtu.be/eyQSb_tC0Fg?t=163) This feature existed from the very beginning, but since it took a lot of cartridge space, you didn't see it used much in early-to-midlife Famicom titles. As such, later titles that had increased ROM capacities and improved technology felt like a dramatic evolution.

[03:06](https://youtu.be/eyQSb_tC0Fg?t=186) And there's more. The Famicom Disk System saw the introduction of yet another audio channel. This was called the “wave memory" channel. It has a more sustained quality to it.

[03:21](https://youtu.be/eyQSb_tC0Fg?t=201) And there's still more! Many Famicom games came with expansion audio chips built directly into the cartridge. I suppose you could call them proprietary. In other words, an IC chip was added to the cartridge to produce hiġher quality audio.

[03:37](https://youtu.be/eyQSb_tC0Fg?t=217) However... For one thing, the Famicom Disk System was never released outside of Japan. Similarly, when the NES—or Nintendo Entertainment System—was released overseas, the connector pins used for cartridge-based audio were removed.

[03:53](https://youtu.be/eyQSb_tC0Fg?t=233) This meant that you couldn't include any expansion audio chips in overseas cartridges. As a result, several games had different-feeling music between their Japanese and overseas versions. Let's listen to a few.

[04:56](https://youtu.be/eyQSb_tC0Fg?t=296) Even differences like this became less common in later titles, as greater ROM capacity meant the gap could be bridged through use of the DPCM channel. I recommend checking out the music in Silver Surfer on the NES, for example. It's incredible!
