[00:56](https://youtu.be/C3ANiCI1Em4?t=56) The first thing we do is casting. Who's doing what voice? That said, many characters in my more recent games have their voice actors already set for them, so there's no need to think much about it.

[01:14](https://youtu.be/C3ANiCI1Em4?t=74) We use the original game's actors when we can, but if a character has since been recast for newer games in a series, we'll sometimes go with the new actor instead. Next, we prep the recording scripts.

[01:27](https://youtu.be/C3ANiCI1Em4?t=87) We usually write the text in game documents horizontally, but Japanese voice scripts are formatted as vertical text that's read from right to left. Every “ha" or “hmph” needs to be on there in order to get recorded. All the basic necessities like attacks, jumps, and damage reactions need to be included, as do unique lines like special moves, Final Smashès, and fighter reveal dialogue.

[01:53](https://youtu.be/C3ANiCI1Em4?t=113) If the line comes from a fighter's original game, we'll also prepare reference audio. From the game itself, I mean. Now, lines in Smash Bros. tend to be quite short, and we try not to overuse them if possible. Longer lines or excessive chatter in battle can draw too much attention and break player immersion.

[02:16](https://youtu.be/C3ANiCI1Em4?t=136) It's also not good to have voices competing with each other, so for characters with feral-sounding voices like Bowser or Donkey Kong, we put extra thought into how they overlap.

[02:31](https://youtu.be/C3ANiCI1Em4?t=151) Once we've worked with the agencies and lined up everyone's schedules, we book the recording studio. With Smash Bros. for 3DS/Wii U and Ultimate, this was easy, since Bandai Namco Studios has their own recording facility, but depending on the game, we often have to go elsewhere.

[02:48](https://youtu.be/C3ANiCI1Em4?t=168) Then comes the recording. For Smash Bros., the original rights holders often attend recording sessions as well. We start by briefing the actors on their characters and getting them back into their voices, then they head into the booth and start recording.

[03:10](https://youtu.be/C3ANiCI1Em4?t=190) In most cases, I direct the sessions myself using what's called a “talkback” button. But depending on the franchise, that series' sound director may take the helm. After this, overseas voices are recorded based on the ones we recorded in Japan, spearheaded by NOA—Nintendo of America— and NOE—Nintendo of Europe.

[03:36](https://youtu.be/C3ANiCI1Em4?t=216) Characters like Pokémon take a lot of work since they say different things in every language. Surprisingly, it's after the recording that things really get busy, as voices get edited over and over to make them suitable for the game. And that's how finished voices end up in the game.

[03:54](https://youtu.be/C3ANiCI1Em4?t=234) For Kid Icarus: Uprising, I tried to have cast members record together to really capture the banter between characters, but we did have to record some lines solo when the scheduling didn't work out.
