[00:04](https://youtu.be/Hk2npGxEQRo?t=4) What kind of jobs make up a game development team? For people already in the industry, this is an elementary, everyday topic, but it's not something just anyone would know by default. These roles can vary greatly between genres and game content, but I'd like to guide you through the jobs that are often present in the games I make.

[00:32](https://youtu.be/Hk2npGxEQRo?t=32) First, the director. That's my role. I can't speak for other organizations, but there's generally just one per team. The director comes up with and consolidates game ideas, and makes almost all the final decisions.

[00:47](https://youtu.be/Hk2npGxEQRo?t=47) They supervise all aspects of a game and make adjustments when necessary. If the director conceived the vision for the game, they might also be called the game designer. Next e have lan   r est t e In short, they turn the director's ideas into design documents and distribute them to the team.

[01:08](https://youtu.be/Hk2npGxEQRo?t=68) These documents can also be called spec sheets, and contain detailed information about how the game should be programmed. Planners have diverse responsibilities, and are involved in almost all facets of a game. They have to manage teams, balance gameplay, supervise testing, and sometimes write programming scripts that work outside of the game code.

[01:30](https://youtu.be/Hk2npGxEQRo?t=90) Programmers. Programmers write the code that makes the game work. Their job is to take the spec sheets and craft them into instructions the computer will understand. It's fair to say that programmers pull together and finalize all the assets made by other team members.

[01:48](https://youtu.be/Hk2npGxEQRo?t=108) Computers can be highly uncooperative, so this job requires patience and flexibility. Modelers. More specifically "3D modeling artists," but I'm abbreviating for this video. Modelers use graphics tools to create objects out of polygons.

[02:06](https://youtu.be/Hk2npGxEQRo?t=126) The word “model” can refer to many things: player characters or fighters, stages and backdrops, enemies, items... The list goes on. Naturally, dev teams need a lot of modelers— but each one's work might be drastically different.

[02:22](https://youtu.be/Hk2npGxEQRo?t=142) Artists. Artists create 2D artwork, such as fighter illustrations and the like. The job calls for a different skillset than that of a 3D modeling artist, but some people are capable of doing both jobs, so the lines can be a little blurry.

[02:41](https://youtu.be/Hk2npGxEQRo?t=161) Animators. Animators create the animations that make the models move. The job is more than just creating “realistic” movement— animations need exaggeration, variety, smart posing, and a style of movement that serves the goals of the game.

[02:58](https://youtu.be/Hk2npGxEQRo?t=178) It depends on the genre, but animation teams are often quite large. There arealso "character riggers" who create the underlying skeletons for animation. Effect designers. Effect designers are in charge of on-screen visual effects.

[03:13](https://youtu.be/Hk2npGxEQRo?t=193) Dash clouds, hit effects, blade trails, flames, lightning, and anything else that covers the screen— all of it needs to be designed with impact and believability in mind. Thisjob requires a slightly different animation sensibility from other artist roles. Sound designers.

[03:32](https://youtu.be/Hk2npGxEQRo?t=212) This work can be roughly divided into three categories: music, sound effects, and voices. Music composers craft the various songs that you hear during gameplay. Sound effect designers create the impact sounds, footsteps, and environmental noises that play as the game unfolds.

[03:53](https://youtu.be/Hk2npGxEQRo?t=233) Voice-over supervisors manage recording sessions with voice actors, then edit and add postprocessing to the recorded voiċe clips. UI, or “user interface” designers. This role is responsible for creating things like thè menus in a game.

[04:09](https://youtu.be/Hk2npGxEQRo?t=249) The Ul team includes a mixture of planners, artists, and programmers, but their goals are quite different from those of their counterparts on other teams. Technical operations. These folks manage servers, maintain development environments, troubleshoot, and generally handle any tech support other teams need.

[04:29](https://youtu.be/Hk2npGxEQRo?t=269) Even though their contributions aren't often seen by players, they're truly indispensable. Managers. Their job is to do whatever it takes to keep the team running smoothly.

[04:40](https://youtu.be/Hk2npGxEQRo?t=280) Things like managing the schedule, identifying and resolving development holdups, assigning personnel, and making sure everyone abides by company rules.

[04:49](https://youtu.be/Hk2npGxEQRo?t=289) This job also includes support tasks like office upkeep and procuring hardware. Play testers. They playtest the game and point out any issues, or areas that need tuning. That feedback is then closely examined by the planning team.

[05:07](https://youtu.be/Hk2npGxEQRo?t=307) With competitive games especially, play testers need the appropriate skill level and the perseverence to just keep playing. Bug testers. Just like play testers, these people play the game a lot, but their role is focused primarily onfinding bugs.

[05:25](https://youtu.be/Hk2npGxEQRo?t=325) After they write up a bug report explaining how to reproduce a bug, a manager assigns it to the appropriate team, who takes over until the bug is fixed. Footage specialists. Separate from the testing teams, we also have people who specialize in capturing footage for fighter reveals and the like.

[05:46](https://youtu.be/Hk2npGxEQRo?t=346) Since the in-engine footage was put together by our own team members, I'd like to think we got some extremely high-quality movies for Smash Bros. Ultimate. The game industry also includes PR, sales, and production jobs, but some may not consider those to be "dev" roles, so I've left them out for now.

[06:04](https://youtu.be/Hk2npGxEQRo?t=364) And many dev teams are divided into subteams such as "characters" or "stages," but I've opted to just focus on the jobs themselves this time. Each subteam tends to comprise a varied mixture of these professions. The “fighter team," for example, has planners, programmers, modelers, artists, animators, effect designers, and sound designers— many different roles all working together.

[06:29](https://youtu.be/Hk2npGxEQRo?t=389) And of course, the director is part of just about every subteam. The director supervises every facet of the game, and oversees adjustment after adjustment until it finally reaches release quality.

[06:44](https://youtu.be/Hk2npGxEQRo?t=404) All of these roles need to work in tandem, because nothing would get done without mutual understanding. Objectives and goals should be shared with everyone, so the entire team can move forward together.
