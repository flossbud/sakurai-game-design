# 08 · Audio: Sound Effects, Music and Mixing

> Sakurai can't name a single masterpiece with bad audio, and he says audio is what players remember first and what "hits you right in the heart" [257](../episodes/notes/257-no-masterpiece-has-bad-audio.md). He treats sound as feedback before decoration. Every sound's volume is set by how relevant it is to the player, not by how big the action looks. Impact sounds reach full strength almost instantly and let go quickly ("strong attack, light reverb"). Action sounds are exaggerated, while ambience stays quiet, fairly realistic and never silent. Music is built on a few memorable melodies at a tempo that fits the action, and arrangements of beloved songs follow the original as players heard it. And every mix is checked on a cheap TV, because that's how most players will hear it.

## The big ideas

**Sound is feedback first, so mix by relevance.** Sound effects play almost constantly, and the player has to pick out the ones that matter. His rule is simple: the more relevant a sound is to the player, the louder it should be [32](../episodes/notes/032-strike-a-balance-with-sound-effects.md). The KO sound tops the Smash mix because it decides the match. Hits come next because they're direct feedback, and ambience comes last [204](../episodes/notes/204-balancing-ambient-sounds.md). Input sounds stay audible even at low priority, because a press with no reaction feels lacking [104](../episodes/notes/104-dont-be-unresponsive.md). Timing is part of relevance too: a hit sound must never feel late, and in a busy match its tail must clear before the next hit [167](../episodes/notes/167-strong-attack-light-reverb.md), so check that the playback chain isn't adding delay [8](../episodes/notes/008-down-with-lag.md).

**Fiction or non-fiction: decide what each sound is for.** Fighting games sound nothing like real fighting, and real punches would sound like slaps. Exaggerated impacts are what sell the hit [89](../episodes/notes/089-audio-as-fiction-and-non-fiction.md). Realistic sounds have a different job: they carry information simple graphics can't, like the weight of wood and stone in Minecraft [89](../episodes/notes/089-audio-as-fiction-and-non-fiction.md), and they make ambience believable even in cartoony games [204](../episodes/notes/204-balancing-ambient-sounds.md). The balance shifts with the visuals: the more realistic the graphics, the stranger exaggerated sound feels [89](../episodes/notes/089-audio-as-fiction-and-non-fiction.md), and the more a loud, foreground melody feels "off" [69](../episodes/notes/069-game-music-and-ambient-sounds.md).

**Music is what people carry home.** A song has more impact if people can remember its melody, so he favors a main theme and its arrangements over a flood of tracks [69](../episodes/notes/069-game-music-and-ambient-sounds.md). Tempo is a design decision for everyone, not only the composer [25](../episodes/notes/025-make-the-tempo-match-the-game.md). Structure follows gameplay: an intro that covers the countdown, a climax at the end, a length that fits a match [127](../episodes/notes/127-arranging-music.md). When arranging music players already love, the original as they heard it in play is always right, quirks and all [146](../episodes/notes/146-the-original-piece-is-always-right.md).

**Judge in the player's room, and direct with words that work.** Most players listen through small TV speakers that drop the bass, so never finalize on studio monitors alone [54](../episodes/notes/054-listen-in-various-environments.md). Many play handhelds muted, so sound can't be the only warning [257](../episodes/notes/257-no-masterpiece-has-bad-audio.md), [157](../episodes/notes/157-special-crossover-3.md). Because sound has no physical form, requests need concrete roles, shared references and links, not "more clang" [185](../episodes/notes/185-giving-feedback-for-sound-effects.md), [222](../episodes/notes/222-using-other-songs-for-audio-reference.md). Then approve whatever serves the game, even if it isn't what you imagined [185](../episodes/notes/185-giving-feedback-for-sound-effects.md).

## Principles

### AUD-01 · Mix every sound by how relevant it is to the player

**Why:** Sounds play nearly all the time, so each needs a deliberate loudness, flashiness and discernibility. Judging by the physical size of an action gives the wrong answer; what counts is the sound's importance to the game and to the player character. Match-deciding events and direct feedback come first, ambience last [204](../episodes/notes/204-balancing-ambient-sounds.md).

**Do:** List every sound and rank it by relevance, then set volumes in that order. Smash's ladder, loudest first: KO; hits given or taken, and perfect shields; special-move activations and voices, with explosions between hits and specials; dodges, shield blocks and ledge grabs; attack swings that don't connect, and dashes; landings; jumps; walking; ambience. Within a tier, scale by strength. Balance voices carefully, because clear speech cuts through other sounds. Where channels or voices are limited, decide ahead of time what yields [239](../episodes/notes/239-famicom-and-nes-audio.md).

**Watch out:** Every sound equally loud, and chatter or ambience masking hits [107](../episodes/notes/107-voice-recording.md).

> "The more relevant a sound is to the player, the louder that sound should be." ([32](../episodes/notes/032-strike-a-balance-with-sound-effects.md), ≈00:36)

*Sakurai's example:* The KO sound is Smash's loudest because it's crucial to push and pull and decides the match; Link's footsteps and his sword swing are both quiet, which raises the question of which comes first. · Episodes: [32](../episodes/notes/032-strike-a-balance-with-sound-effects.md), [204](../episodes/notes/204-balancing-ambient-sounds.md), [107](../episodes/notes/107-voice-recording.md), [239](../episodes/notes/239-famicom-and-nes-audio.md)

### AUD-02 · Make impact sounds hit instantly and clear out fast

**Why:** Players need a reaction to their attack right away, so an impact sound must never feel late. Echo makes a sound feel stronger, but Smash can have four or eight players, and overlapping tails make it impossible to tell where each sound starts. Everything gets buried in noise.

**Do:** Give hit sounds a short attack that reaches a strong level almost immediately (it can't literally start at peak volume and still sound right). Keep the reverb tail short. Trim jab sounds to the shortest length that still reads as consecutive hits. For heavy hits with hitstop, let the main body of the sound finish during the freeze, before action resumes. Review in the busiest mix, not solo. Make sure the punch survives TV speakers that lose bass [54](../episodes/notes/054-listen-in-various-environments.md), and check home theater setups, which can make impact sounds arrive late [8](../episodes/notes/008-down-with-lag.md).

**Watch out:** Sounds that are "good" in isolation but wrong for the game. Long, echoing tails impress solo and muddy the mix.

> "Hence, my policy is: 'strong attack, light reverb.'" ([167](../episodes/notes/167-strong-attack-light-reverb.md), ≈02:35)

*Sakurai's example:* Smash impact waveforms, loud at the start with a short tail, revised from the sound team's longer pre-check versions. · Episodes: [167](../episodes/notes/167-strong-attack-light-reverb.md), [8](../episodes/notes/008-down-with-lag.md), [32](../episodes/notes/032-strike-a-balance-with-sound-effects.md), [54](../episodes/notes/054-listen-in-various-environments.md)

### AUD-03 · Treat audio as essential, never as bolt-on polish

**Why:** He knows masterpieces with bad graphics, and even some without coherent game essence, but none with bad audio. When people think back on a game they loved, its music and sound effects often come to mind first. Even old games with nothing but simple noise-based effects had well-constructed audio.

**Do:** Schedule and budget sound alongside graphics from the start. At kickoff, list audio changes for every character, as he did with 70 to 100 planned changes per Smash Ultimate fighter across graphics, audio and moves [160](../episodes/notes/160-super-smash-bros-ultimate.md). Use what new hardware allows, as Melee used streaming playback for orchestral tracks [40](../episodes/notes/040-super-smash-bros-melee.md). Tie presentation to music: in Kirby's Dream Land each stage begins right as its musical intro ends [4](../episodes/notes/004-kirbys-dream-land.md). Review sound with the same seriousness as visuals, controls and fun [96](../episodes/notes/096-the-family-computer-and-the-nes.md).

**Watch out:** Treating mobile or handheld players' muted play as proof that audio doesn't matter; he counts those as exceptions where good audio would still have added more.

> "Even more than the game itself, its visuals, or its characters— audio is what hits you right in the heart." ([257](../episodes/notes/257-no-masterpiece-has-bad-audio.md), ≈01:33)

*Sakurai's example:* Kirby's Dream Land's Sound Test, intro-timed stage starts and victory dance [4](../episodes/notes/004-kirbys-dream-land.md). · Episodes: [257](../episodes/notes/257-no-masterpiece-has-bad-audio.md), [4](../episodes/notes/004-kirbys-dream-land.md), [40](../episodes/notes/040-super-smash-bros-melee.md), [96](../episodes/notes/096-the-family-computer-and-the-nes.md), [160](../episodes/notes/160-super-smash-bros-ultimate.md)

### AUD-04 · Answer every input and every feat with sound

**Why:** If the player acts and the game doesn't react, it feels lacking [104](../episodes/notes/104-dont-be-unresponsive.md). Swings, dashes and footsteps exist in the mix as feedback for button presses, and landings as reactions [32](../episodes/notes/032-strike-a-balance-with-sound-effects.md). Items and progress are expected in any game and aren't there specifically to tell players they did well, so praise must be added deliberately, shaped to heighten what the player already feels [95](../episodes/notes/095-praise-the-player.md).

**Do:** Give every accepted press a clear signal, sound included. Keep input sounds audible even near the bottom of the mix. Add crowd reactions tied to skill: cheers for big launches and KOs, a subtle "oh!" when someone barely grabs a ledge. Let spectators clap along to victory music. Consider rumble that follows the frequency of impact sounds, which he experimented with on Switch HD Rumble [55](../episodes/notes/055-modern-quality-of-life-features.md).

**Watch out:** Praise that's only item drops, or one generic announcement for everything.

*Sakurai's example:* Smash's crowd gasp at near-misses; Kirby Air Ride's spectators clapping to the dance music at the end of a stage. · Episodes: [104](../episodes/notes/104-dont-be-unresponsive.md), [32](../episodes/notes/032-strike-a-balance-with-sound-effects.md), [95](../episodes/notes/095-praise-the-player.md), [55](../episodes/notes/055-modern-quality-of-life-features.md)

### AUD-05 · Exaggerate action sounds; keep material and ambient sounds realistic

**Why:** Fighting games are nothing like actual fighting, and real impacts would sound like a bunch of slaps. Forget that and you drift away from the game essence you want. Realistic sounds have their own power: they give meaning to things simple graphics can't show. Hits that sound painful, like floors that look hard, enrich a game's information because that's how things are in reality [178](../episodes/notes/178-what-is-play.md).

**Do:** Assign each sound category a job: selling impact (exaggerate) or conveying material, weight and place (keep it realistic). A/B a real recorded hit against an exaggerated one. In games with simple visuals, give each material its own sound. Use fairly realistic ambience even under cartoon art [204](../episodes/notes/204-balancing-ambient-sounds.md). Set the overall level of exaggeration against how realistic your visuals are, and unify audio across mixed sources just as you would the art [138](../episodes/notes/138-a-fight-between-live-action-and-animation.md).

**Watch out:** Exaggerated sounds over very realistic visuals, which feel stranger the more realistic the visuals get.

> "If you don't remember right from the start that fighting games are nothing like actual fighting, you'll end up drifting far away from the game essence you want." ([89](../episodes/notes/089-audio-as-fiction-and-non-fiction.md), ≈01:14)

*Sakurai's example:* Street Fighter II's wildly fake impacts, which remind him of Showa-era yakuza films; Minecraft's thunks, which let you feel the weight of wood and stone despite cube graphics. · Episodes: [89](../episodes/notes/089-audio-as-fiction-and-non-fiction.md), [204](../episodes/notes/204-balancing-ambient-sounds.md), [178](../episodes/notes/178-what-is-play.md), [138](../episodes/notes/138-a-fight-between-live-action-and-animation.md)

### AUD-06 · Never leave dead silence; keep ambience quiet but alive

**Why:** Ambient sounds are the lowest priority and must never cover the sounds that matter. But silence kills atmosphere, and games should make players feel they've entered another world, especially in first-person and over-the-shoulder views. A handful of simple sounds can imply a whole world beyond the screen [26](../episodes/notes/026-fun-beyond-game-essence.md). As graphics carry more information, music that stands out too much starts to feel off, and realistic visuals call for a realistic soundscape [69](../episodes/notes/069-game-music-and-ambient-sounds.md).

**Do:** Mix ambience lowest. Stop in every area and just listen; add layers wherever it's silent. Build soundscapes from real-world layers (wind through leaves, feet on dirt, clinking armor), each with appropriate presence. Check ambience on good speakers, where its quality shows. Put extra care into first-person and over-the-shoulder games.

**Watch out:** Ambience so busy it buries the effects that matter.

> "If you stop playing for a second and hear nothing but dead silence, there'll be no sense of atmosphere." ([204](../episodes/notes/204-balancing-ambient-sounds.md), ≈00:46)

*Sakurai's example:* Smash stages played with the volume turned up, revealing how many quiet ambient sounds they contain; a train simulator whose clattering doors and shuffling feet make it feel like people are boarding [26](../episodes/notes/026-fun-beyond-game-essence.md); modern games played with NES-style music, which sounds noisy and off [69](../episodes/notes/069-game-music-and-ambient-sounds.md). · Episodes: [204](../episodes/notes/204-balancing-ambient-sounds.md), [69](../episodes/notes/069-game-music-and-ambient-sounds.md), [26](../episodes/notes/026-fun-beyond-game-essence.md)

### AUD-07 · Finalize sound on cheap TVs and several setups, not studio speakers

**Why:** Small TV speakers lose low frequencies, so impacts hit less hard, and that's the most common setup. Details tuned in a soundproofed studio can become inaudible on everyday speakers. And once you check this way, your reference matters: change headphones and it's hard to stay consistent with everything you checked before.

**Do:** Compare every important sound on headphones, PC speakers and a cheap TV before approving it. Watch sounds whose punch lives in the bass. Check good speakers too, where ambience quality shows [204](../episodes/notes/204-balancing-ambient-sounds.md). Keep the same reference gear for the whole project. Check audio latency separately from video [8](../episodes/notes/008-down-with-lag.md). Check that every audio feature you rely on exists on each hardware version you ship [239](../episodes/notes/239-famicom-and-nes-audio.md).

**Watch out:** Tuning so far for weak speakers that you stray from your creative vision. Aim for a balance that works everywhere.

> "More than anything, I recommend not solely using big studio speakers to finalize sound." ([54](../episodes/notes/054-listen-in-various-environments.md), ≈01:46)

*Sakurai's example:* His order of checks (headphones, PC speakers, then most importantly a cheap TV); the Switch's own speakers rank low because they're already clear and many handheld players play muted. · Episodes: [54](../episodes/notes/054-listen-in-various-environments.md), [204](../episodes/notes/204-balancing-ambient-sounds.md), [8](../episodes/notes/008-down-with-lag.md), [239](../episodes/notes/239-famicom-and-nes-audio.md)

### AUD-08 · Never make sound the only carrier of critical information

**Why:** Many players play handhelds with the sound down or somewhere they can't hear [257](../episodes/notes/257-no-masterpiece-has-bad-audio.md), [54](../episodes/notes/054-listen-in-various-environments.md). During one crossover session a host who couldn't hear the game audio had no idea when enemies were firing [157](../episodes/notes/157-special-crossover-3.md). The reverse holds too: audio and vibration cues can carry information to players who can't easily see it [55](../episodes/notes/055-modern-quality-of-life-features.md).

**Do:** Give every audio warning a visual tell players can react to, like Punch-Out!!'s yellow eyes [157](../episodes/notes/157-special-crossover-3.md). Treat sound as a reinforcing layer for critical events. Offer optional audio cues (a sound near a spot you can jump to or an item you can pick up, combat cues) and vibration cues for players who need them [55](../episodes/notes/055-modern-quality-of-life-features.md).

**Watch out:** Enemy attacks or key story information delivered only through sound.

*Sakurai's example:* The crossover host who couldn't tell when enemies were firing; the traversal and combat audio cues in a modern accessibility menu. · Episodes: [157](../episodes/notes/157-special-crossover-3.md), [257](../episodes/notes/257-no-masterpiece-has-bad-audio.md), [54](../episodes/notes/054-listen-in-various-environments.md), [55](../episodes/notes/055-modern-quality-of-life-features.md)

### AUD-09 · Build the soundtrack on a few memorable themes

**Why:** He thinks too many songs get produced for a single game, and admits he's guilty of it. Huge soundtracks are harder to remember and harder to get excited by.

**Do:** Write one main theme early and plan where its arrangements will play throughout the game. Cut or merge tracks without a melody players could hum. When arranging, keep the main melody intact, so humming the arrangement is like humming the original [127](../episodes/notes/127-arranging-music.md).

**Watch out:** A new, unrelated song for every scene. (For crossover games with huge libraries, see the tension table.)

> "However you do it, a song will have more impact if people can remember its melody." ([69](../episodes/notes/069-game-music-and-ambient-sounds.md), ≈02:34)

*Sakurai's example:* His habit of writing a main theme and arranging it across the game; the Star Wars theme as the reason a familiar melody shouldn't change from film to film [127](../episodes/notes/127-arranging-music.md). · Episodes: [69](../episodes/notes/069-game-music-and-ambient-sounds.md), [127](../episodes/notes/127-arranging-music.md)

### AUD-10 · Match music tempo and mood to the action and the player's feeling

**Why:** Some tempos fit a game's content and others don't. Even among similar fighting games, the right tempo depends on the speed of the action, and different scenes within one game call for their own tempos and rhythms. In general, songs should complement how the player is feeling [69](../episodes/notes/069-game-music-and-ambient-sounds.md).

**Do:** Count BPM by clapping (one clap per second is 60, two is 120) and note a target for each scene. For fast battle music, start from roughly 130 to 170 BPM and treat near-200 tracks as exceptions. Set tempo by your game's actual speed, not its genre. Write one line per scene on what the player should feel, and check the music supports it. If you can afford it, tie tempo to game state.

**Watch out:** One tempo for the whole game.

*Sakurai's example:* Smash battle arrangements, generally a little over 120 BPM and 130 to 170 on average; the first Dragon Quest, whose dungeon music slows the deeper you go, an effective idea he rarely sees because it's hard to implement. · Episodes: [25](../episodes/notes/025-make-the-tempo-match-the-game.md), [69](../episodes/notes/069-game-music-and-ambient-sounds.md)

### AUD-11 · Brief sound work with concrete roles, shared references and examples

**Why:** Audio has no physical form and you can't really approximate it with your voice, so vague notes fail, especially in text over remote work. Not everyone knows every sound, even a real one. A reference track is a quick way to set direction, but composers are often unsure which parts to keep [222](../episodes/notes/222-using-other-songs-for-audio-reference.md). Directors may know what they want without the musical language to say it [127](../episodes/notes/127-arranging-music.md).

**Do:** Decide what feedback and feeling a sound must give before you request it. Describe its role concretely ("strong attack"), compare only to sounds everyone knows ("a jet engine"), and link an example for anything specific. Use onomatopoeia only as an extra clue. Give one reference track and say what to keep and what not to copy. Route musical feedback through a sound director. When notes keep coming back reversed, check that you and the musician mean the same thing by terms like "main melody" [146](../episodes/notes/146-the-original-piece-is-always-right.md).

**Watch out:** Emails that only ask for more "clang" or "boom," and handing over a reference with nothing but "make it like this."

*Sakurai's example:* God Hand's "Gene's Rock-a-Bye," requested in the style of The Ventures without their famous riff [222](../episodes/notes/222-using-other-songs-for-audio-reference.md); the Athena arrangement that kept coming back opposite to his feedback because he and the musicians heard different melodies as the main one [146](../episodes/notes/146-the-original-piece-is-always-right.md). · Episodes: [185](../episodes/notes/185-giving-feedback-for-sound-effects.md), [222](../episodes/notes/222-using-other-songs-for-audio-reference.md), [127](../episodes/notes/127-arranging-music.md), [146](../episodes/notes/146-the-original-piece-is-always-right.md)

### AUD-12 · Approve the sound that serves the game, not the one in your head

**Why:** The goal isn't just "good" sounds but the right type of sounds for your game [167](../episodes/notes/167-strong-attack-light-reverb.md). Delivered sounds may differ from what you imagined and sometimes sound better. A reference can save work or create more, and getting fixated on one closes your mind to other possibilities [222](../episodes/notes/222-using-other-songs-for-audio-reference.md).

**Do:** Judge drafts by whether they fit the game and the scene, not by how closely they match your idea or the reference. Before locking in a reference, list two or three other directions. Balance multi-environment fixes against your creative vision [54](../episodes/notes/054-listen-in-various-environments.md). If an existing song fits your theme exactly, consider licensing it as-is.

**Watch out:** Rejecting a fitting sound only because it doesn't match what you pictured.

*Sakurai's example:* Sakurai approving sounds "plenty different" from his original idea; Ore no Shikabane wo Koete Yuke using the existing song "Hana," because a song about flowers blooming and wilting fits a family cursed with a two-year lifespan [222](../episodes/notes/222-using-other-songs-for-audio-reference.md). · Episodes: [185](../episodes/notes/185-giving-feedback-for-sound-effects.md), [222](../episodes/notes/222-using-other-songs-for-audio-reference.md), [167](../episodes/notes/167-strong-attack-light-reverb.md), [54](../episodes/notes/054-listen-in-various-environments.md)

### AUD-13 · Shape music structure around gameplay timing

**Why:** Music and play share a timeline: a start countdown, a typical match length, a segment of known length, a fight that ends whenever the player wins. When structure fits, music can hit the exact beats and moods of a scene [125](../episodes/notes/125-kid-icarus-uprising.md).

**Do:** Brief composers with gameplay rules: an intro that covers any countdown (or music that starts after it), big dynamic shifts for a dynamic game, a build to a climax at the end, a loop point before or after the intro, and a length that fits a typical match with just a little looping [127](../episodes/notes/127-arranging-music.md). Use fixed-length segments to time music to beats [125](../episodes/notes/125-kid-icarus-uprising.md). Plan cue points where music waits for, or follows, variable-length events [227](../episodes/notes/227-gameplay-as-part-of-a-live-performance.md). Start play when the stage's musical intro ends [4](../episodes/notes/004-kirbys-dream-land.md).

**Watch out:** Loops that restart mid-match and climaxes that land at random.

*Sakurai's example:* Smash's "3, 2, 1, GO!" intro rule and its two-minute target (two and a half in Ultimate); Kid Icarus: Uprising's air battles, whose known length let music be timed to each scene; the PRESS START orchestra following the moment he beat the mid-boss live [227](../episodes/notes/227-gameplay-as-part-of-a-live-performance.md). · Episodes: [127](../episodes/notes/127-arranging-music.md), [125](../episodes/notes/125-kid-icarus-uprising.md), [227](../episodes/notes/227-gameplay-as-part-of-a-live-performance.md), [4](../episodes/notes/004-kirbys-dream-land.md)

### AUD-14 · Ground characters with audible, varied, material-aware footsteps and landings

**Why:** Footsteps are barely audible in many 3D games, which makes characters feel like they're hovering, not walking. Landing is a reaction, so it deserves more presence than the jump. Believable sounds feel best, and one sample on repeat sounds mechanical.

**Do:** Make footsteps clearly audible. Give each character unique footsteps. Match landings and steps to footwear or costume and to the floor material. Record several variations and rotate them. Make landings louder than jumps. Let surface sounds carry material information when the visuals can't [89](../episodes/notes/089-audio-as-fiction-and-non-fiction.md), as part of a full soundscape [69](../episodes/notes/069-game-music-and-ambient-sounds.md).

**Watch out:** One footstep sample for every floor.

*Sakurai's example:* Cloud's landing sounds differ by costume, audible on a good set of speakers; every new Smash fighter got new footsteps. · Episodes: [32](../episodes/notes/032-strike-a-balance-with-sound-effects.md), [89](../episodes/notes/089-audio-as-fiction-and-non-fiction.md), [69](../episodes/notes/069-game-music-and-ambient-sounds.md)

### AUD-15 · Script every vocalization; keep battle voices short, sparse and non-competing

**Why:** Recording a Smash fighter's voice takes about an hour, two at most, against a year or more of work on the fighter, yet those voices go straight into the game and players hear them constantly. Anything not in the script won't be recorded. Long lines or excessive chatter in battle draw too much attention and break immersion.

**Do:** Script every "ha" and "hmph": basics (attacks, jumps, damage reactions) and unique lines (special moves, super moves, reveal lines). Bring reference audio from the original game and prefer the original or current series actor. Keep combat lines short and infrequent, and plan how loud or feral voices overlap. Brief actors and ease them back into the voice. Record banter-heavy casts together when you can. Budget heavy editing after the session. Where possible, deliver dialogue as voice during play so players don't have to stop [211](../episodes/notes/211-advancing-text.md).

**Watch out:** Patching voice recorded in a different room; he re-recorded his own channel voiceover only as a last resort because the new room sounded different [261](../episodes/notes/261-masahiro-sakurai-on-creating-games-finale-special.md).

*Sakurai's example:* Extra care over how Bowser's and Donkey Kong's feral voices overlap; Kid Icarus: Uprising's cast recorded together to capture banter. · Episodes: [107](../episodes/notes/107-voice-recording.md), [32](../episodes/notes/032-strike-a-balance-with-sound-effects.md), [211](../episodes/notes/211-advancing-text.md), [261](../episodes/notes/261-masahiro-sakurai-on-creating-games-finale-special.md)

### AUD-16 · When arranging beloved music, treat the original as players heard it as right

**Why:** Listeners come for one familiar song out of a great many, and he has bought arrangement CDs only to be let down when a piece came out totally different [127](../episodes/notes/127-arranging-music.md). The song players heard while playing is right, however far it strays from musical convention, and arrangers must not take away from each player's memory of it.

**Do:** Keep the main melody intact. Decide which melody is "main" by what players remember from play, including ports they knew. Keep the original's oddities. Mirror structure the hardware forced on the original. Keep separate language versions distinct. When recreating a sound, match how it came out of the original hardware, not just the raw file [54](../episodes/notes/054-listen-in-various-environments.md). Check reviews for mistranscriptions of the original score [127](../episodes/notes/127-arranging-music.md).

**Watch out:** Rewriting a melody because it feels old-fashioned to you.

> "However you cut it, when arranging beloved songs, the original piece is always right." ([146](../episodes/notes/146-the-original-piece-is-always-right.md), ≈06:20)

*Sakurai's example:* Athena's section that hits on-beats the first time and off-beats the second, faithfully reproduced; Psycho Soldier's English arrangement going verse, instrumental, verse, because the original switched to the internal audio processor after the first verse; Ryu's impact sounds, the hardest Smash sound, tuned to how they sounded from the arcade cabinet [54](../episodes/notes/054-listen-in-various-environments.md). · Episodes: [146](../episodes/notes/146-the-original-piece-is-always-right.md), [127](../episodes/notes/127-arranging-music.md), [54](../episodes/notes/054-listen-in-various-environments.md)

### AUD-17 · When voices or channels are scarce, plan what yields and fit timbre to role

**Why:** The Famicom's music and sound effects shared the same five channels, so part of the music often had to cut out whenever an effect played, and composers gave each role the waveform that suited it. The same budgeting applies to any polyphony, memory or storage limit, and features can differ between hardware versions.

**Do:** Decide in advance which music part drops out when an effect needs a voice. Give crisp tones to melodies, mellow tones to bass and noise-like sounds to hits and explosions. Test compression on big audio sets early, before cutting content [160](../episodes/notes/160-super-smash-bros-ultimate.md). Check each hardware variant or region supports the audio features you rely on.

**Watch out:** Discovering late that a regional or hardware version lacks an audio feature, as NES cartridges lacked the pins for expansion audio [96](../episodes/notes/096-the-family-computer-and-the-nes.md).

*Sakurai's example:* The Famicom's two pulse, one triangle, one noise and one DPCM channel, and Super Mario Bros. 3's timpani sample; Smash Ultimate's music library squeezed into a quarter of the space it took in Smash for [160](../episodes/notes/160-super-smash-bros-ultimate.md). · Episodes: [239](../episodes/notes/239-famicom-and-nes-audio.md), [160](../episodes/notes/160-super-smash-bros-ultimate.md), [96](../episodes/notes/096-the-family-computer-and-the-nes.md)

### AUD-18 · Let players reach and choose the music they love

**Why:** Each generation has its own version of a series, and so its own music, which made one song per world feel wasteful [108](../episodes/notes/108-super-smash-bros-brawl.md). Some players would rather enjoy the music from the very start than unlock it [151](../episodes/notes/151-planning-your-games-rewards.md).

**Do:** Where your game celebrates many sources, offer several tracks per world and let players choose. Don't lock music many players want behind progression. Offer options such as how often menu music plays.

**Watch out:** Music used as a grind reward.

*Sakurai's example:* Brawl's My Music; Smash cutting back on music as rewards in recent games; adjustable menu-music frequency after beating Ultimate. · Episodes: [108](../episodes/notes/108-super-smash-bros-brawl.md), [151](../episodes/notes/151-planning-your-games-rewards.md)

### AUD-19 · Showcase game music live with the performance at the center

**Why:** He believes live music can stir emotions you can't find elsewhere. In a concert the performance is the point, and video after video would distract from it. Live, one-time stakes are what made his gameplay-with-orchestra performance thrilling [227](../episodes/notes/227-gameplay-as-part-of-a-live-performance.md).

**Do:** Gather far more candidate songs than slots and narrow them down by listening together. Use game footage only occasionally. Fix each edition's size and the series' total run so it fits beside development. If gameplay joins the show, keep real stakes and plan cue points for variable timing.

**Watch out:** Turning a concert into a video reel.

*Sakurai's example:* PRESS START, ten annual concerts from 2006 with 14 to 16 program pieces (about 60 songs counting medleys); his Kid Icarus: Uprising air battle played live at Intensity 9.0 with zero defense [227](../episodes/notes/227-gameplay-as-part-of-a-live-performance.md); Melee's orchestral tracks, which led to the concerts [40](../episodes/notes/040-super-smash-bros-melee.md). · Episodes: [116](../episodes/notes/116-press-start.md), [227](../episodes/notes/227-gameplay-as-part-of-a-live-performance.md), [40](../episodes/notes/040-super-smash-bros-melee.md)

## Common mistakes

- Setting volume by how big an action looks instead of how much it matters to the player [32](../episodes/notes/032-strike-a-balance-with-sound-effects.md).
- Impact sounds with slow attacks or long reverb tails that turn a busy match into noise [167](../episodes/notes/167-strong-attack-light-reverb.md).
- Literal, realistic combat sounds, or cartoon sounds over photoreal visuals [89](../episodes/notes/089-audio-as-fiction-and-non-fiction.md).
- Dead silence whenever the action stops, or ambience so loud it buries key effects [204](../episodes/notes/204-balancing-ambient-sounds.md).
- Finalizing the mix on studio monitors and never hearing it on a cheap TV [54](../episodes/notes/054-listen-in-various-environments.md).
- Changing reference headphones partway through the project [54](../episodes/notes/054-listen-in-various-environments.md).
- Signaling enemy attacks with sound alone [157](../episodes/notes/157-special-crossover-3.md).
- Producing so many songs that none is memorable [69](../episodes/notes/069-game-music-and-ambient-sounds.md).
- One tempo for every scene, or a genre-default tempo that doesn't fit the game's speed [25](../episodes/notes/025-make-the-tempo-match-the-game.md).
- Arrangements that lose the main melody or "correct" the original's quirks [127](../episodes/notes/127-arranging-music.md), [146](../episodes/notes/146-the-original-piece-is-always-right.md).
- Requests that say only "more clang," and rejecting good sounds because they differ from your idea [185](../episodes/notes/185-giving-feedback-for-sound-effects.md).
- Fixating on one reference track until every draft is a cheap imitation [222](../episodes/notes/222-using-other-songs-for-audio-reference.md).
- Footsteps so quiet characters seem to hover, and one sample repeated on every step [32](../episodes/notes/032-strike-a-balance-with-sound-effects.md).
- Long or constant battle chatter, unscripted grunts and no time budgeted for voice editing [107](../episodes/notes/107-voice-recording.md).

## Review questions

1. Rank every sound by relevance to the player: is the loudest sound always the most important one?
2. In the worst-case scene (most players, most effects), can you hear exactly when each hit starts?
3. Does the mix still work on a cheap TV, and has the playback chain been checked for lag?
4. Stop anywhere and listen: is there dead silence, or ambience burying something important?
5. With the sound off, can a player still see every warning, and does sound carry what it should for players who rely on it?
6. Can players hum the main theme, and does each scene's tempo and mood fit its action and the player's feeling?
7. For each sound, is its job impact or information, and does its realism fit both the job and the visuals?
8. Could the sound designer or composer act on your request alone, and are you judging their work by fit rather than by your mental image?

## Exercises

1. **Build the ladder (1–2 hours).** Export a list of every sound effect in your game. Rank them by relevance to the player using Smash's ladder as a template. Set volumes in that order, then play your busiest scenario and adjust anything that still masks a higher-ranked sound [32](../episodes/notes/032-strike-a-balance-with-sound-effects.md), [204](../episodes/notes/204-balancing-ambient-sounds.md).
2. **Envelope surgery (half a day).** Take your ten most common hit sounds. Shorten their attacks so they peak almost immediately, cut their reverb tails, and trim the rapid-hit ones to the shortest length that still reads as separate hits. Time heavy hits to finish inside hitstop. A/B old and new in a crowded fight [167](../episodes/notes/167-strong-attack-light-reverb.md).
3. **Cheap TV night (1–2 hours).** Play the same 15-minute session on headphones, PC speakers and the cheapest TV you can find. Note every sound that disappears or loses its punch on the TV, and fix the worst five without ruining the headphone mix [54](../episodes/notes/054-listen-in-various-environments.md).
4. **Dead-silence and mute walk (1 hour).** First, stop in every area and listen; list every spot that's silent and add a quiet, realistic layer. Second, play a level muted and list every warning you missed; give each one a visual tell [204](../episodes/notes/204-balancing-ambient-sounds.md), [157](../episodes/notes/157-special-crossover-3.md).
5. **BPM and hum sheet (1 hour).** Clap-count the BPM of every track and write it next to the scene it plays in, along with one line on what the player should feel there. After a playtest, ask testers to hum the main theme. If nobody can, plan arrangements of one theme instead of new songs [25](../episodes/notes/025-make-the-tempo-match-the-game.md), [69](../episodes/notes/069-game-music-and-ambient-sounds.md).
6. **Rewrite three requests (30 minutes).** Take three sound or music requests you've sent. Rewrite each with a concrete role, a comparison everyone knows, a link, and for music a reference track with "keep" and "don't copy" notes [185](../episodes/notes/185-giving-feedback-for-sound-effects.md), [222](../episodes/notes/222-using-other-songs-for-audio-reference.md).
