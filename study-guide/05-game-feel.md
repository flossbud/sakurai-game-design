# 05 · Game Feel: Impact, Response and Control

> To Sakurai, game feel is a conversation between the player's hands and the screen. The player sends a signal; the game has to answer at once, clearly, and with the right weight. Almost all of it comes down to small, concrete details measured in frames: a stop when a hit lands, a shake that dies away, a pose that changes the instant you press, a jump that falls faster than it rises, a camera that lets you see how high you went. Each one is hard to notice alone. Together they decide whether a game feels great or bland. Because feel lives in time, he also treats lost time as a defect: display lag, slow fades and long death sequences damage feel as surely as a missing hit stop.

## The big ideas

**Feel is a two-way signal.** Players take in information through their eyes and ears and send it back with their hands, and "somehow, you can feel feedback with your hands" ([19](../episodes/notes/019-giving-weight-to-buttons.md)). How heavy a button feels comes from what happens on screen: speed, inertia, jump physics, lag and frame rate. So the first job is to answer every press on the next frame, with a pose change, a sound or a highlight, and then to protect that answer all the way to the player's eyes. Response time a game earns can be thrown away by a TV's image processing or a slow hit sound ([8](../episodes/notes/008-down-with-lag.md), [49](../episodes/notes/049-making-lead-ins-instant-and-impactful.md), [104](../episodes/notes/104-dont-be-unresponsive.md), [167](../episodes/notes/167-strong-attack-light-reverb.md)).

**Impact is built in layers.** Hit stop is the foundation: freeze both fighters when a hit lands, and the hit gets heavier ([3](../episodes/notes/003-stop-for-big-moments.md)). On top of it Sakurai stacks shake (the victim more than the attacker, and never the hitbox), strength scaling with a per-move multiplier, pose blending, screen shake presets, flashes and slow motion for the biggest moments ([47](../episodes/notes/047-eight-hit-stop-techniques.md), [103](../episodes/notes/103-screen-shake.md)). Weight also comes from what happens after the hit: strong moves leave you open for longer, and knockback carries the payoff ([77](../episodes/notes/077-follow-throughs-make-the-impact.md), [145](../episodes/notes/145-knockback-in-super-smash-bros.md)).

**Frames are the unit of everything.** Distance = time × speed, and games measure time in frames ([5](../episodes/notes/005-frame-rates.md)). Sakurai writes specs in frames, trains himself to feel them by counting "1-and-2-and," and expects to retune every value when the tempo, mode or platform changes ([76](../episodes/notes/076-learn-to-count-frames.md), [160](../episodes/notes/160-super-smash-bros-ultimate.md)). Most of the numbers he gives are proportions and anchors (a hit about a quarter of the way into the move, about 15 frames for a Smash Attack's charge-up), not universal constants.

**Respect intent and time.** The critical layer of feel is trust. A deliberate input must never be misread (a step attack that walks you off a ledge feels like a canceled input), an attack that looks like it hits must hit, and the player's time must not be wasted on slow transitions or long retries ([102](../episodes/notes/102-behavior-at-ledges.md), [225](../episodes/notes/225-always-keep-attack-collision-in-mind.md), [246](../episodes/notes/246-speedy-screen-transitions.md), [117](../episodes/notes/117-make-retries-quick.md)).

## Principles

### FEEL-01 · Make every button press visibly register the moment it happens
**Why:** Play is two-way, and the signal from the hands is vital; an action that gets no reaction feels lacking. Lag from networks and TVs can never be fully removed, but you can still tell the player right away that the input arrived. Impact sounds must never feel late. **Do:** On the first frame after an attack input, jump to a clearly different pose instead of easing out of idle. Give every accepted press an unambiguous cue. Let several buttons confirm in menus, and let any press in a cutscene bring up a skip prompt. Count the frames from press to first visible change and treat that count as a design value. **Watch out:** Smooth ease-outs from standby, dead buttons, and ambiguous responses that leave players unsure whether a press registered.
*Sakurai's example:* In Smash Ultimate, the pose changes drastically the moment you press a Smash Attack, then holds and shakes while you charge; Zero Suit Samus's neutral attack comes out one frame after input. · Episodes: [19](../episodes/notes/019-giving-weight-to-buttons.md), [36](../episodes/notes/036-breaking-down-attack-animations.md), [49](../episodes/notes/049-making-lead-ins-instant-and-impactful.md), [104](../episodes/notes/104-dont-be-unresponsive.md), [124](../episodes/notes/124-games-during-my-childhood.md), [167](../episodes/notes/167-strong-attack-light-reverb.md)

### FEEL-02 · Stop the action at every big moment: hits, boss defeats and deaths
**Why:** Whether hit stop is there "will completely change how heavy a hit feels." Stops work for defeat as well as success: when players die without noticing, "Wait, I got hit?" only tells them something went wrong, which is a missed opportunity. Stops are relatively easy to implement and belong in any genre. **Do:** Freeze attacker and target when a hit lands. On a boss defeat, stop and flash the screen ("boss stop"). When the player is hit or dies, pause long enough to show the point of impact. Layer stop, shake and slow motion for the biggest moments. **Watch out:** Treating stops as minor polish, and deaths the player never saw happen.
*Sakurai's example:* Kirby's Adventure stops and flashes the screen when a boss falls; Fantasy Zone stops the screen for a second when you're hit; in Smash, a finishing blow stops the screen completely. · Episodes: [3](../episodes/notes/003-stop-for-big-moments.md), [47](../episodes/notes/047-eight-hit-stop-techniques.md), [36](../episodes/notes/036-breaking-down-attack-animations.md), [62](../episodes/notes/062-attack-poses.md), [142](../episodes/notes/142-damage-animations.md), [225](../episodes/notes/225-always-keep-attack-collision-in-mind.md), [131](../episodes/notes/131-hit-marks.md)

### FEEL-03 · Hunt down input lag across the whole display and audio chain
**Why:** A game with great response time can still be ruined by display delay. At E3 2018 the Smash Ultimate setup lagged badly enough to ruin the controls, and nobody had noticed. **Do:** Before every demo, playtest or capture session, press jump in an instantly responsive 2D game on that exact setup. Use Game Mode or Low Latency Mode, or turn every picture enhancement off. Check audio separately. Read latency specs as frames (16.67 ms is one frame at 60 fps). Rule out the display before you retune any timing. **Watch out:** Assuming someone else already tested, judging lag with a 3D game, and retuning the game to compensate for a slow monitor.
*Sakurai's example:* Mid-way through a recorded retro session he swapped to his low-lag "Mr. Sakurai Presents" monitor because his shots kept missing; more image post-processing means more lag. · Episodes: [8](../episodes/notes/008-down-with-lag.md), [156](../episodes/notes/156-special-crossover-2.md), [49](../episodes/notes/049-making-lead-ins-instant-and-impactful.md), [108](../episodes/notes/108-super-smash-bros-brawl.md), [19](../episodes/notes/019-giving-weight-to-buttons.md), [252](../episodes/notes/252-crt-displays.md)

> "The recording setup probably contributed to the issue, but one thing was clear: many people don't recognize lag when they see it." ([8](../episodes/notes/008-down-with-lag.md), 01:19)

### FEEL-04 · Make attacks that look like they connect actually connect
**Why:** In a fast game with coordinate-based movement, hitboxes mapped exactly to the sword or fist make attacks miss, and players get more and more stressed. Hits that look like they should land but don't, or that keep hitting after the swing, feel broken. **Do:** In fast games, make collision wider than the visible weapon and make every attack's range cross the median line where opponents stand. During hit stop, shake only the rendered model. Give the early hits of multi-hit moves fixed knockback. Test strings against every reaction stance. When a hit feels off, fix the hitbox and the animation together. **Watch out:** Pixel-exact hitboxes, hitboxes that shake with the model, and combos that only work at one damage level.
*Sakurai's example:* Mario's Super Jump Punch uses fixed knockback on every hit but the last, so the whole move connects at any damage; a neutral-attack combo once missed because the target's stance change moved its top node back. · Episodes: [225](../episodes/notes/225-always-keep-attack-collision-in-mind.md), [165](../episodes/notes/165-odds-and-ends-of-supervising.md), [47](../episodes/notes/047-eight-hit-stop-techniques.md), [145](../episodes/notes/145-knockback-in-super-smash-bros.md), [142](../episodes/notes/142-damage-animations.md), [62](../episodes/notes/062-attack-poses.md), [253](../episodes/notes/253-odds-and-ends-of-supervising-sora-edition.md)

### FEEL-05 · Keep every wait and transition hasty, but don't cut it out entirely
**Why:** "It's a sin for a game to make players wait." Slow fades into long black screens rob players of their time, and it's almost impossible to make a transition too fast, but having none at all feels jarring. A wait that looks like someone dropped the ball is what irritates. **Do:** Time every fade, black screen and non-interactive stretch, and trim each until it is hasty but still reads as a transition. Hide unavoidable loading in an in-world moment or a themed wipe; otherwise show a simple, on-theme indicator at once. Make cutscenes skippable and pausable. **Watch out:** Presentation timed to development-build load times, which will change, and loading icons stamped over a transition that already hides the load.
*Sakurai's example:* Resident Evil's doors hid loading while building fear, and opened more slowly when zombies waited behind them; Smash Ultimate uses random wipes into the victory screen. · Episodes: [246](../episodes/notes/246-speedy-screen-transitions.md), [73](../episodes/notes/073-say-no-to-slow.md), [78](../episodes/notes/078-loading-screens.md), [180](../episodes/notes/180-let-them-skip-let-them-pause.md), [104](../episodes/notes/104-dont-be-unresponsive.md)

### FEEL-06 · Make failure hit hard for about a second, then hand back control
**Why:** Interest cools fast between "Ugh, I blew it!" and getting control again, and quick retries raise tolerance for difficulty. The loss should still be felt. **Do:** Make the failure effect flashy and impactful but short (about a second, depending on genre), then return control. Turn any remaining steps into ones the player advances. Don't respawn players into enemy fire, and keep some cost so respawning isn't mindless. **Watch out:** Long death animations and loads, and infinite respawns right where the player died.
*Sakurai's example:* He gives no specific game here; the closest is ep. 3's Kirby's Adventure, where the action stops briefly when Kirby loses a life. · Episodes: [117](../episodes/notes/117-make-retries-quick.md), [3](../episodes/notes/003-stop-for-big-moments.md), [73](../episodes/notes/073-say-no-to-slow.md)

### FEEL-07 · Specify, feel and verify every timing in frames
**Why:** Frames are the unit of time and the basis for all movement, because speed and distance follow from time. Nothing counts frames for you, and even correct specs can be broken by code or data errors, which only a trained sense catches. **Do:** At 60 fps, 60 frames is a second. Clap "1-and-2-and-3-and": each beat is about 30 frames and each half beat about 15. Anchor on moves you know and estimate from 0/15/30. Write specs as frame numbers and per-frame speeds, and lock processing speed before final tuning. **Watch out:** "Quick" and "slow" in specs, and frame counts nobody on the team can feel.
*Sakurai's example:* His NES and SNES games counted time in screen interrupts, so the 50 fps PAL versions ran at 5/6 speed; Meteos got harder than he intended when processing speed changed late. · Episodes: [5](../episodes/notes/005-frame-rates.md), [76](../episodes/notes/076-learn-to-count-frames.md), [36](../episodes/notes/036-breaking-down-attack-animations.md), [14](../episodes/notes/014-assigning-animations.md), [68](../episodes/notes/068-put-parameters-in-brackets.md), [84](../episodes/notes/084-units-of-speed.md), [74](../episodes/notes/074-meteos.md)

### FEEL-08 · Sell hit stop with shake, scaling and blending, not a bare freeze
**Why:** In Smash, hit stop always includes some shake. Each technique is hard to spot on its own, but together they make the game much better. Scaling plus a multiplier lets a sweet spot feel heavier than its damage, and lets guest characters feel like their own games. **Do:** Shake the victim more than the attacker, and only their models. Shake horizontally on the ground and in all directions in the air, decaying over the precomputed stop length. Scale the stop by strength × a per-move multiplier, longer for lightning and shorter in multiplayer. Shake more when the camera is far. Blend the victim into the damage pose over about four frames, keep a sword's swing creeping forward, and let hit marks keep animating. **Watch out:** Shaking the hitbox, and vertical shake on grounded 3D characters, which pushes their legs through the floor.
*Sakurai's example:* Marth's sword tip gets a much bigger stop than its base; Ryu's stops look bigger and Kazuya's smaller at equal damage, matching their source games. · Episodes: [47](../episodes/notes/047-eight-hit-stop-techniques.md), [131](../episodes/notes/131-hit-marks.md), [142](../episodes/notes/142-damage-animations.md), [167](../episodes/notes/167-strong-attack-light-reverb.md), [179](../episodes/notes/179-blade-trails.md)

> "As a general rule, hit stop in Smash Bros. is never just a stop— there's always a bit of 'shake,' too." ([47](../episodes/notes/047-eight-hit-stop-techniques.md), 00:44)

### FEEL-09 · Front-load the hit and make strong attacks pay for it in recovery
**Why:** Lead-ins should be quick for responsiveness, but if you want push and pull, strong attacks must leave the character proportionally vulnerable afterward, or the game feels unrefined. Landing a slow, risky move is a bigger feat and deserves more damage and knockback. **Do:** Make the hit register about 1/4 to 1/3 of the way into the move (1/5 for fast ones), and keep standard attacks under one second. Lengthen recovery as power rises. To weaken a move without erasing it, lengthen its follow-through or shrink its hitbox. **Watch out:** Strong moves that are safe, and nerfs that strip a move's identity when its recovery could change instead.
*Sakurai's example:* Even Smash Attacks with big openings almost never pass the one-second mark; the quick neutral attack and the slow Smash Attack are rewarded differently. · Episodes: [77](../episodes/notes/077-follow-throughs-make-the-impact.md), [36](../episodes/notes/036-breaking-down-attack-animations.md), [238](../episodes/notes/238-how-good-were-the-players-actions.md), [247](../episodes/notes/247-amplify-both-strengths-and-weaknesses.md), [49](../episodes/notes/049-making-lead-ins-instant-and-impactful.md)

### FEEL-10 · Return control when players expect it, using cancel frames
**Why:** Without cancel frames, control returned only once the character was fully back in standby, which made the Smash games through Melee feel a little heavy or rigid. The cancel timing has to match when people expect to have control back. **Do:** Mark a cancel window late in each recovery; blend from the current pose into whatever the player inputs; playtest the timing. **Watch out:** Windows so early that recovery stops meaning anything.
*Sakurai's example:* His attack breakdowns mark where the hit registers, where the move ends and where cancel frames end the recovery. · Episodes: [36](../episodes/notes/036-breaking-down-attack-animations.md), [77](../episodes/notes/077-follow-throughs-make-the-impact.md), [194](../episodes/notes/194-animation-blending.md)

### FEEL-11 · Shape the jump's rise, fall and air control to your game's goal
**Why:** The right jump depends on the goal of your game, and "even with the same trajectory, adjusting a move's speed can drastically change how it feels." **Do:** Build the jump from an upward velocity plus downward acceleration every frame, with a maximum fall speed. Use separate rise and fall gravity, and choose air control on purpose. Make slams fall faster than they rise. Keep a short, uniform pre-jump windup where jump and up-attacks share a direction, and consider a burst jump that rescues late reactions. Each project, ask whether you need a jump at all. **Watch out:** A default jump reused across games, and a camera that hides how high the character went (FEEL-14).
*Sakurai's example:* Super Mario Bros.' gentle rise and sharp fall; Metroid's floaty jump for aiming; Ghosts 'n Goblins' fixed arc, which turns every jump into a plan; Zangief's Spinning Pile Driver falls faster than it rises from Street Fighter II Turbo onward. · Episodes: [37](../episodes/notes/037-jump-physics.md), [193](../episodes/notes/193-game-essence-in-action-games.md), [65](../episodes/notes/065-falling-sells-the-feeling-of-flight.md), [61](../episodes/notes/061-dont-anchor-your-center-point.md)

### FEEL-12 · Sell big impacts with screen shake tuned to the camera
**Why:** A game without screen shake can feel bland, yet too much makes the screen hard to read, and in 3D a camera shake barely moves distant objects. **Do:** Author a few distinct presets rather than scaled copies of one shake. Start strong and decay with a little randomness. Shake the look-at point as much as the camera or more. Scale by camera distance and field of view. **Watch out:** Screens shaken until unreadable, and a distant center point that barely moves.
*Sakurai's example:* Smash's small shake is vertical only, medium adds horizontal, large shakes more; Flat Zone's tight field of view made the usual shake feel too big, so it was retuned. · Episodes: [103](../episodes/notes/103-screen-shake.md), [3](../episodes/notes/003-stop-for-big-moments.md), [56](../episodes/notes/056-flash-blast-and-smoke.md), [47](../episodes/notes/047-eight-hit-stop-techniques.md), [223](../episodes/notes/223-flashing-effects.md)

> "Even a tiny amount of shake can give attacks a drastically different impact." ([103](../episodes/notes/103-screen-shake.md), 03:07)

### FEEL-13 · Tune on-screen response so each character has its own weight
**Why:** The same button feels "lighter" or "heavier" depending on speed, inertia, jump physics, lag and frame rate. Control feel can be fun by itself, like an RC car, and differences in speed, vulnerability and animation flair make characters feel wildly different in the hands. Inertia is also a dial on risk: more of it raises game essence but narrows appeal. **Do:** Decide each character's intended weight and tune to it; compare characters with identical inputs; run them on one shared behavior and express their differences as parameters; set stick tilt sensitivity per console. **Watch out:** Assuming feel comes from the hardware.
*Sakurai's example:* Toying with a Family BASIC sprite that had a little inertia taught him what he later used on Kirby's Dream Land; every Kirby Air Ride machine shares one behavior and differs only by parameters. · Episodes: [19](../episodes/notes/019-giving-weight-to-buttons.md), [26](../episodes/notes/026-fun-beyond-game-essence.md), [118](../episodes/notes/118-family-basic.md), [124](../episodes/notes/124-games-during-my-childhood.md), [96](../episodes/notes/096-the-family-computer-and-the-nes.md), [148](../episodes/notes/148-more-game-essence-means-less-broad-appeal.md), [59](../episodes/notes/059-kirby-air-ride.md), [108](../episodes/notes/108-super-smash-bros-brawl.md)

### FEEL-14 · Frame the camera for the gameplay, not just to follow the character
**Why:** With the center point anchored to the character, it's very hard to tell how high the character is jumping, and dodges lose their impact. **Do:** Track the center point less, or not at all, on the Y axis. Let the character drift off center on dodges and drifts. Look ahead in side-scrollers. In co-op scrollers, make one player the camera's lead and warp stragglers back. When someone is knocked flying, stop scrolling, then gently recenter once they settle. For touch aiming, let a swipe give the camera inertia and a tap stop it. **Watch out:** Anchoring by default; an anchored center fits only cases like precise, realistic third-person racing.
*Sakurai's example:* Super Mario 64's Mario rises on screen when he jumps; Kirby Air Ride's machine slides off center during a drift so players really feel the input; Kirby Super Star's Helpers Space Jump back to the lead. · Episodes: [61](../episodes/notes/061-dont-anchor-your-center-point.md), [20](../episodes/notes/020-kirby-super-star.md), [100](../episodes/notes/100-development-secrets-of-the-original-kirby.md), [103](../episodes/notes/103-screen-shake.md), [125](../episodes/notes/125-kid-icarus-uprising.md), [42](../episodes/notes/042-a-small-window-into-the-world.md)

### FEEL-15 · Keep controls simple and physical, and no busier than the game needs
**Why:** By 1996 fighting-game commands had become extremely complicated, so Smash used a stick direction plus a button, which players could pick up intuitively. Reading how far and how fast the stick moves gives the controls real physicality. One button can carry a whole game, and every added input changes the feel. **Do:** Map moves to direction plus button, and use input timing for strong variants. Scale walk speed by tilt and dash on a fast flick. Try the core action on one button first. On new hardware, adopt only the uses that fit. Lower input density for casual players and laggy online play. **Watch out:** Two-step dashes, and features added just because the hardware allows them.
*Sakurai's example:* Smash's flick input ("Tap" on N64); Kid Icarus: Uprising's flick dash and dodge; Brawl skipped many possible Nunchuk and pointer functions on purpose. · Episodes: [27](../episodes/notes/027-super-smash-bros.md), [51](../episodes/notes/051-the-potential-of-one-button.md), [154](../episodes/notes/154-flick-input.md), [125](../episodes/notes/125-kid-icarus-uprising.md), [108](../episodes/notes/108-super-smash-bros-brawl.md), [147](../episodes/notes/147-make-important-elements-bigger.md), [169](../episodes/notes/169-i-want-to-choose-fast.md)

### FEEL-16 · Never let a deliberate input feel canceled or misread
**Why:** If a small forward nudge from an action drops players off a ledge, it feels as if their input was canceled. Instant jumps would make players leave the ground before they could input an Up Smash on the same direction, and flick dashes can trigger by accident. **Do:** Decide ledge behavior move by move, account for momentum, and consider push-to-fall or steering along a nearly parallel ledge. Keep a short, uniform windup where two inputs overlap. Expose sensitivity settings. **Watch out:** One global rule that sends characters off every ledge.
*Sakurai's example:* Fox Illusion stops at the ledge; Falcon Kick goes over unless the ledge is reached late in the move; Egg Roll uses a speed threshold. · Episodes: [102](../episodes/notes/102-behavior-at-ledges.md), [37](../episodes/notes/037-jump-physics.md), [154](../episodes/notes/154-flick-input.md), [104](../episodes/notes/104-dont-be-unresponsive.md)

### FEEL-17 · Build knockback as a tunable payoff with per-attack parameters
**Why:** Where launching is the core, knockback is the heart of the game essence. A launcher should always get at least some reaction, multi-hit moves must connect at every damage level, and a big launch after a hard exchange is visceral fun (ilinx, in Caillois's terms). **Do:** Scale knockback with attack damage and the target's accumulated damage. Give each attack an angle (with an optional modifier toward the attacker's travel), a multiplier, a guaranteed minimum kept in check by scaling, and an optional fixed value; let weight and fall speed shape the arc. Cap launch speed so launches stay readable. **Watch out:** A minimum with no scaling, which makes knockback too big.
*Sakurai's example:* Smash for 3DS slowed launches so they stayed readable on the 3DS's LCD; Kirby's Dream Land's 1990 design document already tied knockback to health; a child playtester of the original game was amazed at how far a Falcon Punch sent its target. · Episodes: [145](../episodes/notes/145-knockback-in-super-smash-bros.md), [178](../episodes/notes/178-what-is-play.md), [238](../episodes/notes/238-how-good-were-the-players-actions.md), [135](../episodes/notes/135-super-smash-bros-for-3ds-wii-u.md), [160](../episodes/notes/160-super-smash-bros-ultimate.md), [100](../episodes/notes/100-development-secrets-of-the-original-kirby.md), [248](../episodes/notes/248-elementary-school-play-testers.md)

### FEEL-18 · Choose your frame rate on purpose and protect it
**Why:** 60 and 30 fps differ both in look and in how smoothly they control. 60 is ideal; 30 is still more than adequate and frees half your rendering budget. For timing-heavy action, Sakurai treats a stable 60 as non-negotiable. **Do:** Pick the target early. If it's 60, pare down content and effects until it holds. Keep game speed independent of how many frames get drawn; a variable frame rate is not slowdown. **Watch out:** Logic tied to a refresh rate that changes by region or platform.
*Sakurai's example:* "I considered 60 FPS gameplay to be an absolute must, so we pared things down" for Smash on 3DS; the NES Ghosts 'n Goblins kept every element but lost its smoothness and creepiness. · Episodes: [5](../episodes/notes/005-frame-rates.md), [135](../episodes/notes/135-super-smash-bros-for-3ds-wii-u.md), [156](../episodes/notes/156-special-crossover-2.md), [99](../episodes/notes/099-an-up-close-look-at-smash-bros-stages.md), [96](../episodes/notes/096-the-family-computer-and-the-nes.md)

### FEEL-19 · Use genre-standard controls, keep series rules stable, and allow remapping
**Why:** De facto standards let you skip explanations and warm up what Sakurai calls the handshake between human and computer. Changing a series' rules each release makes it harder to play, and players want the freedom to set controls their own way. **Do:** Use the genre's standard layout unless you have a deliberate reason not to. Keep fundamentals fixed across a series. Ship remapping with a drawn controller, shared actions and named profiles, and expose sensitivity. **Watch out:** Avoiding standards just so you don't look like a copy.
*Sakurai's example:* Western FPS games share nearly one control scheme; remapping Pac-Land's press-to-run scheme in Arcade Archives made it easy to play. · Episodes: [175](../episodes/notes/175-its-easier-if-they-already-know.md), [139](../episodes/notes/139-guidelines.md), [156](../episodes/notes/156-special-crossover-2.md), [197](../episodes/notes/197-button-settings.md), [55](../episodes/notes/055-modern-quality-of-life-features.md), [154](../episodes/notes/154-flick-input.md), [19](../episodes/notes/019-giving-weight-to-buttons.md)

### FEEL-20 · Retune every feel parameter when tempo, mode or hardware changes
**Why:** Raising Ultimate's tempo meant none of Smash for's parameters could be reused, jumps and knockback included. Fighters dropped into Brawl's adventure mode with their versus specs didn't work. **Do:** Budget a full retune whenever speed changes. Keep a separate movement set for very different modes. Let the weakest screen cap launch and movement speeds. Redo tuning if performance shifts late. **Watch out:** Porting old values just because they worked last time.
*Sakurai's example:* Melee's speed was hard on newcomers (and even gave some competitive players tendinitis), so Brawl was slower; Ultimate sits between Melee and Smash for. · Episodes: [160](../episodes/notes/160-super-smash-bros-ultimate.md), [108](../episodes/notes/108-super-smash-bros-brawl.md), [135](../episodes/notes/135-super-smash-bros-for-3ds-wii-u.md), [74](../episodes/notes/074-meteos.md), [5](../episodes/notes/005-frame-rates.md), [84](../episodes/notes/084-units-of-speed.md)

### FEEL-21 · State speeds in shared units and judge them against body size
**Why:** Specs don't work if the team can't share what a speed means, and the same pixel speed gives different results on different screens. **Do:** Pick one unit per project (pixels per frame for sprites, km/h or distance per frame for real-scale 3D), put it in parameter names, and judge speeds against the character's own size. **Watch out:** Unitless speeds.
*Sakurai's example:* Kirby Air Ride's km/h values look low because Kirby is only about a 20 cm square. · Episodes: [84](../episodes/notes/084-units-of-speed.md), [68](../episodes/notes/068-put-parameters-in-brackets.md), [5](../episodes/notes/005-frame-rates.md)

### FEEL-22 · Treat the screen as a small window and spend it on play
**Why:** A screen fills only about 30 degrees of vision, so space is scarce; realistic views like the cockpit cam shrink the play area, and tiny targets lack impact. **Do:** Compare camera options by how much screen they leave for play, and scale targets up when they read small. **Watch out:** Choosing a view for realism alone.
*Sakurai's example:* Kid Icarus: Uprising's standard enemies are four or five times Pit's size. · Episodes: [42](../episodes/notes/042-a-small-window-into-the-world.md), [125](../episodes/notes/125-kid-icarus-uprising.md), [61](../episodes/notes/061-dont-anchor-your-center-point.md)

### FEEL-23 · Offer comfort toggles instead of stripping presentation for everyone
**Why:** Motion sickness comes from on-screen inertia the inner ear doesn't feel, but a camera with no embellishment looks less entertaining. Too much shake or blur also hides the action. **Do:** Add toggles for footstep shake, vehicle-frame sway and blur strength, and test with people who actually get sick. **Watch out:** Signing off camera comfort with a team that never gets sick.
*Sakurai's example:* The common FPS option to turn off vertical footstep shake can be very effective. · Episodes: [129](../episodes/notes/129-motion-sickness-in-3d-games.md), [192](../episodes/notes/192-motion-blur.md), [103](../episodes/notes/103-screen-shake.md), [223](../episodes/notes/223-flashing-effects.md)

### FEEL-24 · Tune feel by hand, with exposed parameters and fast iteration
**Why:** Feel is found by trying values and playing. Sakurai learned it by tinkering with inertia in Family BASIC, and tuning is where a team iterates most. **Do:** Keep every speed, timing and force in parameters outside the code, named in plain language with units, and push edits to the build in one step. Add debug shortcuts for the setups you test most. Change one value at a time and note how it feels. **Watch out:** Hard-coded values, and conversions that take minutes.
*Sakurai's example:* On the first Smash games each parameter conversion took ten minutes plus a reboot; the Bandai Namco Studios environment converts the Excel parameters with one button press. · Episodes: [118](../episodes/notes/118-family-basic.md), [46](../episodes/notes/046-making-your-game-easy-to-tune.md), [68](../episodes/notes/068-put-parameters-in-brackets.md), [59](../episodes/notes/059-kirby-air-ride.md), [195](../episodes/notes/195-debug-mode.md), [79](../episodes/notes/079-visual-effects-in-slow-motion.md), [102](../episodes/notes/102-behavior-at-ledges.md)

## Where the advice pulls both ways

- **Instant response vs. windups.** Sakurai wants the pose to change the instant you press ([49](../episodes/notes/049-making-lead-ins-instant-and-impactful.md)), yet he keeps a jump windup ([37](../episodes/notes/037-jump-physics.md)) and gives heavy attacks big lead-ins ([49](../episodes/notes/049-making-lead-ins-instant-and-impactful.md), [150](../episodes/notes/150-the-perils-of-interpolation.md)). The resolution: the *first frame* always answers, and the time to the actual hit is a design value. Light, spammable moves can come out on frame one. Heavy moves get a big, readable windup for impact and to cover online lag. A short, uniform windup protects another input that shares the same direction. Responsiveness lives in the startup's first frame; push and pull lives mostly in the recovery ([77](../episodes/notes/077-follow-throughs-make-the-impact.md)).
- **Hold the follow-through vs. give control back early.** He wants follow-throughs held as long as possible ([36](../episodes/notes/036-breaking-down-attack-animations.md)), but also cancel frames that return control when players expect it ([77](../episodes/notes/077-follow-throughs-make-the-impact.md)). Hold the *pose* for the eye, let cancel frames end the *vulnerability*, and blend the exit so it doesn't pop ([194](../episodes/notes/194-animation-blending.md)).
- **Speed vs. readability.** Melee's speed suited top players and spectators but was too harsh on casual players, so Brawl was slowed ([108](../episodes/notes/108-super-smash-bros-brawl.md)). Smash for 3DS slowed launches for the small LCD ([135](../episodes/notes/135-super-smash-bros-for-3ds-wii-u.md)), and Ultimate sped up again for a home console and a more experienced audience ([160](../episodes/notes/160-super-smash-bros-ultimate.md)). Go fast where the player acts. Slow down where they have to understand what happened (launches, deaths, boss kills). Pick the tempo for the audience and the screen.
- **Generous hitboxes vs. honest collision.** Wide collision keeps fast attacks from whiffing ([225](../episodes/notes/225-always-keep-attack-collision-in-mind.md)), but a big gap between visuals and collision feels broken ([165](../episodes/notes/165-odds-and-ends-of-supervising.md)). Widen collision for fairness, keep the animation where the collision is, and fix both together when they disagree. Sakurai accepts some mismatch if it makes the game more fun.
- **More impact vs. a readable, comfortable screen.** Shake, flash and effects are crucial ([103](../episodes/notes/103-screen-shake.md), [223](../episodes/notes/223-flashing-effects.md)), but too much hides the action and makes some players sick ([129](../episodes/notes/129-motion-sickness-in-3d-games.md), [192](../episodes/notes/192-motion-blur.md)). Scale effects to the camera, cap them, and offer toggles rather than removing them for everyone. Busy scenes need less: hit stop is shortened in multiplayer ([47](../episodes/notes/047-eight-hit-stop-techniques.md)) and impact reverb kept light ([167](../episodes/notes/167-strong-attack-light-reverb.md)).
- **60 fps ideal, 30 acceptable, 60 a must.** In general, 30 fps is more than adequate when visuals need the budget ([5](../episodes/notes/005-frame-rates.md)). For a timing-critical action game, Sakurai cuts content to hold a stable 60 ([135](../episodes/notes/135-super-smash-bros-for-3ds-wii-u.md)).
- **Standard controls vs. new ones.** Use de facto standards for what the genre already agrees on ([175](../episodes/notes/175-its-easier-if-they-already-know.md)), and innovate only where a device offers something physical that fits the game, as the flick did for the analog stick ([154](../episodes/notes/154-flick-input.md)). Always let players remap and adjust sensitivity.
- **Fast vs. no transition; disguised vs. honest loading.** Keep a transition but make it hasty ([246](../episodes/notes/246-speedy-screen-transitions.md)). Disguise loading only with presentation that still works once final load times shrink; otherwise show a clean indicator immediately ([78](../episodes/notes/078-loading-screens.md)).
- **Quick retries vs. meaningful failure.** Retries should be fast, but infinite respawns right where you died can make the game feel mindless ([117](../episodes/notes/117-make-retries-quick.md)).

## Common mistakes

- Treating hit stop and screen shake as optional polish, or using one stop length and one shake for every hit.
- Shaking the hitbox along with the model, so attacks that should connect miss.
- Easing out of the idle pose into an attack, so the first frames after a press show nothing new.
- Judging controls on a TV with post-processing on, then "fixing" timing that was only display lag.
- Strong attacks with short recoveries: no push and pull, and the game feels unrefined.
- Returning control only when the character is fully back in standby.
- One default jump with symmetric gravity, plus a camera that tracks the character vertically and hides the jump.
- Pixel-exact hitboxes in a fast game; multi-hit moves whose later hits miss at high or low damage.
- Letting characters walk or step off ledges after small forward nudges.
- Slow fades, long black screens, unskippable cutscenes and long death sequences.
- Carrying old tuning values into a game whose tempo, mode or platform has changed.
- Specs that say "fast" or "slow" instead of frame numbers and units.
- Adding inputs because the hardware has them; command strings for basic moves.
- Screen shake strong enough to hide the action, with no option to turn it off.

## Review questions

1. From each press to the first visible or audible response, how many frames pass, and does every press in every state get a reaction?
2. When a hit lands, a boss falls or the player dies, does the game stop and shake enough to feel the weight and to see what happened?
3. Does every attack that looks like it connects actually connect, at every damage level, from every direction?
4. How many seconds per session is the player not in control, and how long from failure to the next attempt?
5. Can you state each action's startup, hit and recovery in frames, and does the build match by feel?
6. Does each character's movement, jump and knockback feel like its intended weight and serve the game's goal?
7. Does the camera show the motion the player just made and the space they need?
8. Could a genre newcomer pick up the controls in minutes, and can every player change them?

## Exercises

1. **Hit stop A/B (half a day).** Add a freeze on hit with shake on the models only: victim larger than attacker, horizontal on the ground, decaying to zero. Scale the length by damage and add a per-move multiplier on one sweet spot. Record the same exchange with and without and compare in slow motion.
2. **First-frame and lag audit (2 hours).** Capture gameplay at 60 fps (or use the engine's frame counter) and count the frames from each press to the first visible change, for every action. Fix anything that isn't the next frame. Then run the NES-style jump test on every screen your team uses for playtests and set each to its lowest-latency mode.
3. **Frame-counting drill (30 minutes a day for a week).** Practice "1-and-2-and" against a frame counter. Guess the startup and total length of ten of your moves, then check the data. Rewrite one spec page so every timing is a frame number.
4. **Jump lab (half a day).** Implement the basic jump model with separate rise and fall gravity and a maximum fall speed. Make three presets (gentle rise and fast fall, floaty, fixed arc with no air control) plus Smash Ultimate's burst-then-heavy-gravity jump. Play one test level with each and write down which suits your game's goal and why.
5. **Waiting-time audit (2 hours).** Play 15 minutes and log time in control, time not in control, and empty time. Measure death-to-control time. Cut every fade to the fastest version that doesn't feel jarring, and cut the failure sequence to about a second.
6. **Camera and shake pass (half a day).** Reduce Y-axis tracking during jumps, add an off-center offset on dodges, and build small/medium/large shake presets with different axis patterns, scaled by camera distance. Add a toggle for each inertial camera effect.
