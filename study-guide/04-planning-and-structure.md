# 04 · Planning, Structure and Pacing

> Sakurai plans so the expensive mistakes never happen. He fixes one clear concept before production and steers by it, proves the fun in a bare prototype, writes short and decisive proposals, and keeps specs exact enough that nobody can misread them. Then he structures the game around one cost the player pays: their time. Players should be in control and seeing the fun within minutes. Every wait, prologue and unskippable scene has to justify itself, and length should be a by-product of fun rather than a selling point. This chapter covers concepts, proposals and specs, scope and trimming, structure and pacing, tutorials, story and cutscenes. It is written so a small team, even a game-jam team, can use it on day one.

## The big ideas

**A concept is a lighthouse, and the fun is found by building.** Sakurai won't move a project forward until the concept, his lighthouse, is "in clear sight", and he won't change that heading midway, because changing course throws away months of the team's work and gets everyone lost ([187](../episodes/notes/187-staying-true-to-your-concept.md), [29](../episodes/notes/029-spec-changes.md)). He is just as clear that you can't document the finished game up front: proposals miss elements that later become essential, and some fun only shows up in the build ([29](../episodes/notes/029-spec-changes.md), [52](../episodes/notes/052-prep-your-proposals-hot-and-fast.md), [164](../episodes/notes/164-making-sequels.md)). The two fit together. Fix the destination (the concept, the audience, the core rules), prove it in a playable prototype ([27](../episodes/notes/027-super-smash-bros.md), [100](../episodes/notes/100-development-secrets-of-the-original-kirby.md)), and leave the route open: details, numbers and polish get settled while building.

**The proposal is the skeleton, so write it fast and commit.** He writes proposals "hot", in one push, without talking about them until the draft is done ([52](../episodes/notes/052-prep-your-proposals-hot-and-fast.md), [144](../episodes/notes/144-keep-your-internal-pressure-high.md)). Nearly every page is one image and two lines, so even a 200-page deck moves like a comic ([38](../episodes/notes/038-writing-game-proposals.md)). There is no plan B: start from the ideal spec and trim for cost later, as a team ([109](../episodes/notes/109-dont-rely-on-a-plan-b.md)). He avoids pitching a new game as "X, but with Y" ([161](../episodes/notes/161-avoiding-comparison.md)). Then the specs for teammates are exact: every tunable value in plain-language brackets, every speed with a unit, every timing in frames ([68](../episodes/notes/068-put-parameters-in-brackets.md), [84](../episodes/notes/084-units-of-speed.md), [76](../episodes/notes/076-learn-to-count-frames.md)).

**The player's time is the budget you're spending.** "To make players wait is a sin" ([73](../episodes/notes/073-say-no-to-slow.md)). He counts actual playtime, meaning the time the player is in control, and tries to cut everything else: slow transitions, retry delays, unskippable scenes, walking home after an errand ([73](../episodes/notes/073-say-no-to-slow.md), [117](../episodes/notes/117-make-retries-quick.md), [246](../episodes/notes/246-speedy-screen-transitions.md), [64](../episodes/notes/064-good-errands-and-bad-errands.md), [233](../episodes/notes/233-in-game-cutscenes.md)). The opening matters most. Players should understand the fun within three minutes, so open with play, ideally with a climax, and save the backstory ([17](../episodes/notes/017-just-let-them-play.md), [235](../episodes/notes/235-start-with-the-climax.md), [113](../episodes/notes/113-game-writing-is-unique.md)). Teach controls inside real challenges, and only what players don't already know ([85](../episodes/notes/085-making-tutorials-feel-natural.md), [173](../episodes/notes/173-teaching-players-how-to-play.md), [175](../episodes/notes/175-its-easier-if-they-already-know.md)). Treat the total length as a cost to the player and let fun, not padding, produce the hours ([250](../episodes/notes/250-be-mindful-of-the-time-players-are-giving-you.md)).

**Structure is motivation made visible.** Plan rewards while the game is still a plan, and make sure each type suits the concept ([151](../episodes/notes/151-planning-your-games-rewards.md), [75](../episodes/notes/075-consider-rewards-first.md)). Keep the next objective visible and reachable ([23](../episodes/notes/023-keeping-rewards-in-sight.md)). Shape progression like a mountain trail, with peaks and flat stretches rather than one steady incline ([105](../episodes/notes/105-mountain-climbing-is-more-than-just-climbing.md)). Package volume as short, distinct chunks so no single style wears thin ([20](../episodes/notes/020-kirby-super-star.md)). Story serves this structure: it unfolds from the protagonist's view, during play, and never takes away a win the player earned ([113](../episodes/notes/113-game-writing-is-unique.md), [125](../episodes/notes/125-kid-icarus-uprising.md)).

## A small-team plan: decide first, write down, cut

Sakurai's projects are huge, but the order of his decisions scales down to a team of two and a weekend.

**Decide first, before any content:**
1. The one-line concept, naming the audience or the hook (PLAN-01, PLAN-09).
2. The core rules, proven in a bare prototype with nothing else in it (PLAN-02).
3. The hard limits: platform, performance target, size, deadline, and for a jam, the theme and rules (PLAN-22).
4. What happens in the first minute and the first three minutes (PLAN-03, PLAN-08).
5. Which features must ship (including the hands-on tutorial and skip/pause) and which you'll trim first (PLAN-05).

**Write down, on about one page plus a parameter sheet:** the concept line; what the player does in the first minute; the core rules; the reward list and when each reward arrives (PLAN-16); every tunable value as a bracketed phrase with units (PLAN-13); the must-ship and trim lists. Leave out the plan B (PLAN-15).

**Cut, roughly in this order:** anything that doesn't serve the concept; anything that delays first control (logos, prologue, a separate tutorial room); modes or levels that play like another one (PLAN-24); exposition the protagonist doesn't need (PLAN-19); and features whose hidden costs in testing, unique code or upkeep outweigh their fun (PLAN-23). **Protect:** the core feel, the first three minutes, fast retries, and skip and pause.

## Principles

### PLAN-01 · Fix one clear concept before production, then steer by it
**Why:** Production is long, and once fatigue sets in it's easy to forget what was supposed to be fun. Sakurai treats the concept as a lighthouse: change course midway and you get lost, throw away months of art and programming, and demoralize the team. The spec change to avoid above all is one forced because the game isn't fun. Design documents rarely convey the fun, so the director has to hold the heading until the team can feel it in the build. **Do:** Before anyone builds content, write the concept as one statement of appeal (his examples: "the fun of sending opponents flying", "the thrill of sprinting down a straight path"). Don't start production until it's that clear. Use the time before the full team arrives to finish the design documents and win over stakeholders. When doubt sets in, check the decision against the concept, and amplify emergent fun that heads the same way. **Watch out:** Holding the concept doesn't mean defending every spec. Details should change as the build teaches you (PLAN-12); the destination shouldn't.
> Come storm or dark of night, the one thing you should never change is your trajectory toward that light. ([187](../episodes/notes/187-staying-true-to-your-concept.md), 01:03)

*Sakurai's example:* For Brawl, between E3 and the October start of development, he wrote the design documents and pitched each character to its original creator. He finished Ultimate's proposal on the day the last Smash for 3DS/Wii U presentation went out, so the team could move straight on. Kid Icarus: Uprising's story was set before development really kicked off. · Episodes: [187](../episodes/notes/187-staying-true-to-your-concept.md), [29](../episodes/notes/029-spec-changes.md), [132](../episodes/notes/132-the-team-behind-super-smash-bros-brawl.md), [160](../episodes/notes/160-super-smash-bros-ultimate.md), [113](../episodes/notes/113-game-writing-is-unique.md), [261](../episodes/notes/261-masahiro-sakurai-on-creating-games-finale-special.md)

### PLAN-02 · Prove the core fun in a bare prototype before full production
**Why:** Some things only become clear once you build them, and the most expensive surprise is learning mid-production that the game isn't fun. A playable core and concrete visuals let the team move forward with confidence, and they sell the idea to the people who fund it far better than a document can. **Do:** Build only the core rules first. Mock up key characters at their intended on-screen size with their full movesets. For a content project, finish two or three representative pieces at final quality and treat them as a go/no-go test. Pitch moving mechanics with animated mock-ups, not static text. If you have two candidate concepts, prototype both and estimate how long each would take to finish. **Watch out:** Don't pile content onto the prototype before the core is proven, and don't wait for final tools before seeing anything move.
> What you really want to avoid is having to change course because your game's not fun. ([29](../episodes/notes/029-spec-changes.md), 01:10)

*Sakurai's example:* The Smash prototype, "Dragon King: The Fighting Game", had no special moves, dodges or items, yet already played by Smash's rules. Kirby Super Star's Fighter moveset and Final Cutter combo were sketched early in a Famicom dev tool and survived to release. The channel itself began with a three-episode pilot (C03, C11, D03) that proved it could be done and won Nintendo's cooperation. · Episodes: [27](../episodes/notes/027-super-smash-bros.md), [100](../episodes/notes/100-development-secrets-of-the-original-kirby.md), [261](../episodes/notes/261-masahiro-sakurai-on-creating-games-finale-special.md), [29](../episodes/notes/029-spec-changes.md), [74](../episodes/notes/074-meteos.md), [34](../episodes/notes/034-making-a-living-making-games.md)

### PLAN-03 · Hand players control and the core fun within the first minutes
**Why:** The arcade rule: players should understand why a game is fun in the first three minutes, because they judge fast and put a slow game down. Long prologues only delay play. Players want to take control and find out what the game is, so Sakurai starts with the climax and leaves the backstory for later. **Do:** Time a first boot. A newcomer should be playing, and should see the core fun, within three minutes. Open on a dramatic scene the player controls and explain how things got there afterward, for example a slice from later in the story, or a battle fought as other characters. Show early that the enemy means business. Make repeated presses of the confirm button lead straight into the main game. If the game has to open on everyday life, keep that part short and hand over control early. **Watch out:** Telling the story strictly in order often costs you the opening. Trailers fail the same way when they open on logos and story (see the marketing chapter).
> A game that starts slow is often a game that's starting on the wrong foot. ([17](../episodes/notes/017-just-let-them-play.md), 00:46)

*Sakurai's example:* Final Fantasy VII opens like the run-up to a final dungeon instead of explaining why Cloud joined Avalanche. Kid Icarus: Uprising's first chapter throws you into an Air Battle and introduces Medusa in under a minute. Phoenix Wright puts you in a trial at once. In Smash Ultimate, pressing confirm repeatedly takes you from the title screen to fighter select. · Episodes: [17](../episodes/notes/017-just-let-them-play.md), [235](../episodes/notes/235-start-with-the-climax.md), [113](../episodes/notes/113-game-writing-is-unique.md), [125](../episodes/notes/125-kid-icarus-uprising.md), [240](../episodes/notes/240-making-things-clear-even-without-words.md)

### PLAN-04 · Measure and maximize the time the player is in control
**Why:** Games are full of fun experiences, so making players wait to reach them is the one trap that could make nobody love your game. Some waits can't be avoided, but a wait that looks like someone dropped the ball irritates most. The longer the player is in control, the better. **Do:** Time a session and sort it into three buckets: in control; not in control (cutscenes, loading, menus, transitions, forced animations); and empty time with nothing to do. Set targets to shrink the last two. Keep transitions but make them quick ("a hasty speed is just right"). Hide unavoidable loading in presentation, or show a simple loading indicator that fits your world. Shorten the gap between "Ugh, I blew it!" and getting control back: a flashy failure effect of about a second, then control, with no enemies waiting at the respawn point. Once an errand is done, skip the trip home. **Watch out:** Not every pause is waste. A short cutscene can work as a palate cleanser, which is better than keeping players busy every second (PLAN-20).
> This might sound like an exaggeration, but I'll say it anyway: To make players wait is a sin! ([73](../episodes/notes/073-say-no-to-slow.md), 02:45)

*Sakurai's example:* The first Resident Evil's door animations hide loading and build fear at the same time. The first Zelda's edge-of-screen scrolling suited its era but could be smoother and quicker today. · Episodes: [73](../episodes/notes/073-say-no-to-slow.md), [250](../episodes/notes/250-be-mindful-of-the-time-players-are-giving-you.md), [117](../episodes/notes/117-make-retries-quick.md), [246](../episodes/notes/246-speedy-screen-transitions.md), [78](../episodes/notes/078-loading-screens.md), [64](../episodes/notes/064-good-errands-and-bad-errands.md), [180](../episodes/notes/180-let-them-skip-let-them-pause.md)

### PLAN-05 · Plan to trim: cut what you can't finish, lock what you can't lose
**Why:** Few projects go to plan. Sakurai says a director creates but is also responsible for trimming the excess, offloading "the heavy or flammable cargo" to get back on course. Costs peak at the end (testing, marketing, the most people), so a project that can't make it should cut its losses before then. And features treated as extras get cut first under schedule pressure, including ones you'll regret. **Do:** Weigh candidate concepts partly by estimated development time. Keep a visible list of what the *whole game* still needs, not just each person's task list. Decide early which features must ship and which are first to go. When a target like a stable 60 fps is non-negotiable, pare content until it holds. Before entering the expensive final stretch, weigh what's left to spend against what the game can realistically earn. **Watch out:** Don't assume the game will ship just because everyone finishes their assignments. Don't leave a must-have, like the hands-on tutorial, for the end, when it is easiest to cut. (Chapter 10 covers the director's side of trimming.)

*Sakurai's example:* Smash's four-player fighter beat an RC robot adventure that would have needed two years. Kirby Super Star's "Kagero Mansion" scenario was cut for schedule and ROM space. Smash for 3DS was pared down until four players, stereoscopic 3D and every item ran at 60 fps. Ultimate's hands-on technique mode was cut for schedule, which he still regrets. · Episodes: [258](../episodes/notes/258-a-directors-job-is-to-trim.md), [27](../episodes/notes/027-super-smash-bros.md), [110](../episodes/notes/110-canceled-games.md), [20](../episodes/notes/020-kirby-super-star.md), [135](../episodes/notes/135-super-smash-bros-for-3ds-wii-u.md), [160](../episodes/notes/160-super-smash-bros-ultimate.md), [173](../episodes/notes/173-teaching-players-how-to-play.md)

### PLAN-06 · Treat play time as a cost; build fun first and let length follow
**Why:** Before buying, people weigh a game's hours against everything else they could do, so "This game has 300 hours of content!" can put them off. Time spent should be a result of how fun the game is, as with the players who spend hundreds or thousands of hours in Smash. Every new game competes for those hours with every past work and with low-effort phone content. **Do:** Don't plan or market around an hour count. First write down the core fun experiences and the systems that reinforce them, and size the content after that. Prefer dense, varied content to padding. Size a solo mode to an appropriate length. If failure sends players back to the start, plan what they'll see on each retry so the repetition still pays. **Watch out:** Hard restart loops copied from other games don't come with the pull that makes players push on. That pull has to come from your own game's appeal.
> The time players spend in a game is a cost, so it's best to be mindful of the time they're choosing to give you. ([250](../episodes/notes/250-be-mindful-of-the-time-players-are-giving-you.md), 02:31)

*Sakurai's example:* Kirby Super Star became an omnibus of short scenarios because he felt too many games were taking too long to beat. One of Brawl's two concept pillars was a single-player mode that takes an appropriate amount of time to finish. · Episodes: [250](../episodes/notes/250-be-mindful-of-the-time-players-are-giving-you.md), [20](../episodes/notes/020-kirby-super-star.md), [72](../episodes/notes/072-competing-with-the-past.md), [198](../episodes/notes/198-competition-and-abundance.md), [128](../episodes/notes/128-is-your-game-appealing-enough-to-start-over.md), [108](../episodes/notes/108-super-smash-bros-brawl.md), [113](../episodes/notes/113-game-writing-is-unique.md)

### PLAN-07 · Teach by letting them play, and only what they don't already know
**Why:** Nobody wants manual-style paragraphs, which Sakurai calls the worst option, or a detached training room that just makes players impatient for the real game. Players who learn by overcoming real challenges are more engaged. But hand-holding that keeps telling them how to play makes them feel dragged along, and explaining conventions they already know wastes their time. **Do:** Teach each action where progression first needs it: an early real obstacle that requires it, a small enclosed area that can only be left by using a new movement skill, short prompts placed along the opening path right where the action is used. Build a mode that doubles as training. Skip what genre players know (at most, show the attack-button icon when the first enemy appears), but test the opening with genre newcomers. Make prompts read the current button mapping. Lock the hands-on tutorial into the schedule early. **Watch out:** Sakurai admits the conundrum: you must let players play while you teach, yet you don't want them to feel restricted. Fade the guidance as soon as they've shown they've got it.
> Tutorials shouldn't feel like work, or like they're slowing down the beginning of the game— instead, try to weave them into the game's progression itself. ([85](../episodes/notes/085-making-tutorials-feel-natural.md), 02:33)

*Sakurai's example:* An early stage of Donkey Kong Country 3 spells out buttons with bananas. Few FPS games bother saying "fire with R2". Break the Targets! in the first Smash was built to teach the controls, so it differs for each character. Smash itself only has a non-interactive video, which Sakurai calls a compromise. · Episodes: [17](../episodes/notes/017-just-let-them-play.md), [85](../episodes/notes/085-making-tutorials-feel-natural.md), [173](../episodes/notes/173-teaching-players-how-to-play.md), [175](../episodes/notes/175-its-easier-if-they-already-know.md), [242](../episodes/notes/242-being-kind-to-beginners.md), [219](../episodes/notes/219-mandatory-strategies-and-strategic-balance.md)

### PLAN-08 · Open with a constrained slice of the core, then add controls
**Why:** Asking for every skill at once is too much for beginners. Starting Kid Icarus: Uprising with its third-person shooting would have required players to dodge, aim and fire right away. **Do:** Begin with a restricted form of the core action, such as an on-rails segment, a single-screen fight, or the basic verb on its own, and add free movement, dodging and depth systems after that. Check that a novice can make progress with only the simple core. Consider a no-penalty "trial time" for first-timers. **Watch out:** A constrained opening that drags on turns into the detached tutorial you were trying to avoid.

*Sakurai's example:* Uprising opens with a rail-shooter Air Battle (simple shooting, quick scenery changes) before its Land Battles. Kirby's Adventure can be finished just by inhaling enemies and spitting them out. Space Harrier's arcade "trial time" let newcomers die as much as they wanted for a set period. · Episodes: [125](../episodes/notes/125-kid-icarus-uprising.md), [11](../episodes/notes/011-kirbys-adventure.md), [4](../episodes/notes/004-kirbys-dream-land.md), [157](../episodes/notes/157-special-crossover-3.md), [85](../episodes/notes/085-making-tutorials-feel-natural.md)

### PLAN-09 · Name your audience and hook in one line before designing
**Why:** Risk, controls and rewards all depend on who the game is for. Sakurai sets his own taste aside and treats each project as an assignment to fit a genre or goal. Defining the target by experience ("beginners") works better than by age or gender, and the audience should be chosen from a view of the whole market, not from personal likes. **Do:** Write one line that names the target player or the signature mechanic, the way each of his works is captioned ("tailor-made for newcomers"; "Rack up damage, then smash foes off the screen!"). Define the audience by experience or need, note who already serves them, and tune risk to match (see CASE-01 in chapter 14). **Watch out:** Choosing *who* to serve from market data is fine; choosing *what to make* from trend data is not (see Tensions).

*Sakurai's example:* An experienced gamer made Kirby's Dream Land for newcomers, and a man who is bad at puzzle games made Meteos. Uprising targeted hardcore players because Touch! ### PLAN-10 · Offer, and pitch, something players can't get anywhere else
**Why:** Every new work competes with every past work for people's time, so it needs something fresh. If your game is close to another, people may just play the original. Pitching "X, but with Y" invites exactly that question (plus copycat accusations), and a reference means different things to different listeners. Borrowing another game's systems skips the hardest part of design, so the effort you save has to buy extra appeal somewhere else. **Do:** Open the proposal by describing your game's own core experience without naming another game. List the reasons a player would choose yours over the original, many rather than one, and check the list again near the end of development. Form the concept first and use market data only to check its risks. Save "it's like X" for sequels and informal chats with people who share the reference. **Watch out:** Imitation is a fine place to start, especially for a first game. It's a bad place to finish.

*Sakurai's example:* Around the NES-to-SNES transition, research showed RPGs selling, so many Japanese companies crowded into one market with Dragon Quest and Final Fantasy-like games. "Mario" can mean the NES game, Mario Odyssey or just the character, depending on who you ask. · Episodes: [72](../episodes/notes/072-competing-with-the-past.md), [161](../episodes/notes/161-avoiding-comparison.md), [196](../episodes/notes/196-crafting-similar-games.md), [226](../episodes/notes/226-targeting-and-market-research.md), [198](../episodes/notes/198-competition-and-abundance.md), [40](../episodes/notes/040-super-smash-bros-melee.md)

### PLAN-11 · Question every inherited "given" at project kickoff
**Why:** Genres with a long history pile up rules that add nothing to push and pull, and creators take familiar features for granted. Asking why a convention exists shows what it's for and whether you can do better. Novelty that only renames the genre's usual action isn't new. **Do:** At kickoff, list the conventions you inherited from the genre or from your last game (jump physics, enemies to defeat, blocks that vanish, how bosses get beaten) and decide whether each still earns its place. For each new idea, ask whether it changes what the player does or only what it's called. **Watch out:** Control layouts are the exception. De facto standards make a game easier to pick up, so break them only on purpose (see Tensions).
> To some extent, there's no avoiding complexity in games— but it's best to always approach your "givens" as if they aren't givens at all. ([82](../episodes/notes/082-do-competitive-games-have-to-be-complex.md), 03:36)

*Sakurai's example:* Every Smash game has slightly different jump mechanics because he re-examines them each time. For Meteos he asked whether blocks really had to be erased, and made matched blocks blast off into space instead. · Episodes: [82](../episodes/notes/082-do-competitive-games-have-to-be-complex.md), [74](../episodes/notes/074-meteos.md), [86](../episodes/notes/086-game-essence-in-falling-block-puzzle-games.md), [71](../episodes/notes/071-do-we-really-need-enemies.md), [9](../episodes/notes/009-squeeze-and-release.md), [125](../episodes/notes/125-kid-icarus-uprising.md), [219](../episodes/notes/219-mandatory-strategies-and-strategic-balance.md)

### PLAN-12 · Spec enough to start; expect the fun to surface while building
**Why:** Trying to picture and document the whole finished game can take as long as making it, and some things only become clear in the build. Proposals often lack elements that later become essential. Unplanned elements often end up as a core source of fun, and ideas everyone opposed on paper often work once they're built. Polishing is part of making a game fun. **Do:** Fix the concept and core rules, write enough spec to start, and settle the details as implementation reveals them. Schedule polish as fun-making work, not cleanup. When a problem appears, whoever spots it reports it right away with everything they know; lay out every option and decide quickly. **Watch out:** "Spec lightly" doesn't mean "spec vaguely". What you do write must be exact (PLAN-13). Anything other work has to sync to, like story beats timed to fixed-length segments, should be locked early.

*Sakurai's example:* At proposal level, Monster Hunter is a game about hunting monsters and using their materials to make gear, which doesn't sound especially novel; he imagines only a few of its best points were in the proposal. Metal Gear Solid 3's wound-treatment system reportedly had only four supporters on the team at proposal stage. · Episodes: [29](../episodes/notes/029-spec-changes.md), [52](../episodes/notes/052-prep-your-proposals-hot-and-fast.md), [187](../episodes/notes/187-staying-true-to-your-concept.md), [164](../episodes/notes/164-making-sequels.md), [158](../episodes/notes/158-the-majority-shouldnt-always-rule.md), [261](../episodes/notes/261-masahiro-sakurai-on-creating-games-finale-special.md)

### PLAN-13 · Write specs nobody can misread: units, frames, bracketed parameters
**Why:** Several people tune parameters later, and terse names like "X-velocity" invite misreading. You won't get far with tuning if you can't explain things clearly. Specs don't work at all if speed can't be conveyed, and the same raw values meant different things on hardware with different pixel counts. **Do:** Keep tunable numbers out of code, in a table designers can edit. In specs, write each tunable value as a plain-language phrase in brackets with a placeholder, under the rule that anything in brackets becomes a parameter. Keep a list pairing each phrase with its current value. Pick one unit of speed for the project and put it in every parameter name. Give timings in frames. Keep the spec as a collapsible outline that mirrors the game (character, then moves, then each move's spec). **Watch out:** The bracket notation suits character- and move-specific specs. Handle global values, like general attack power, separately.

*Sakurai's example:* "Initiate movement at [Special startup X-velocity is O]." / "At the same time, decelerate by [Special X-deceleration is O/frame]." ### PLAN-14 · Write proposals hot and fast, one image and idea per page
**Why:** A proposal has to make the people who supply money and talent think "We should definitely do this." Nothing good comes from droning on, and a draft that sits loses its freshness. Single-topic pages move like comic panels, so even 200-plus pages go quickly, and categories show listeners where they are. Talking about an unfinished proposal vents the "internal pressure" that gives it force. **Do:** Itemize the outline, then write the whole proposal in one push without discussing it; consult others once the draft exists. Give each page one screenshot or diagram and two lines of text, grouped into categories and subcategories with visible section markers. Cut graphs, research, grand-vision passages and the plan B. Talk around each slide instead of reading it out. Judge each page by its information, not the page count. If the proposal is rejected, rework how it shows the game's appeal rather than deciding the reviewers lack taste. **Watch out:** The format can't save weak content. What the proposal says still matters most.

*Sakurai's example:* His Super Smash Bros. Ultimate proposal ran over 200 PowerPoint pages, nearly all with one image and two lines of text. · Episodes: [38](../episodes/notes/038-writing-game-proposals.md), [52](../episodes/notes/052-prep-your-proposals-hot-and-fast.md), [12](../episodes/notes/012-presentations-are-all-about-speed.md), [144](../episodes/notes/144-keep-your-internal-pressure-high.md), [74](../episodes/notes/074-meteos.md), [261](../episodes/notes/261-masahiro-sakurai-on-creating-games-finale-special.md)

### PLAN-15 · Commit to one ideal spec; leave Plan B out
**Why:** Writing "or we could do this" hands the decision to someone else. Standing by one idea shows you've thought it through, and it builds experience. The proposal is the skeleton of the game, so start from the ideal and don't bend it out of shape up front for development convenience. **Do:** Before sending a proposal or spec, delete every alternative and keep the option you judge best overall, weighing ease of implementation and appeal yourself. Then ask the team for feedback, and trim for cost together once concrete problems appear. **Watch out:** Committing isn't the same as being deaf. You may end up back at your plan B, but only because a real problem justified it.
> There should always be an ideal spec to start, and from there you can course-correct to maximize cost effectiveness. ([109](../episodes/notes/109-dont-rely-on-a-plan-b.md), 00:54)

*Sakurai's example:* "We could do A. Or we could do B." is the hedge he says won't cut it. He also pushes back on design documents that put the developers' convenience ahead of the player experience. · Episodes: [109](../episodes/notes/109-dont-rely-on-a-plan-b.md), [12](../episodes/notes/012-presentations-are-all-about-speed.md), [52](../episodes/notes/052-prep-your-proposals-hot-and-fast.md), [21](../episodes/notes/021-try-telling-that-to-the-player.md)

### PLAN-16 · Plan rewards at the planning stage, and fit their type to the concept
**Why:** You can't reward players if you have nothing to give them, and a game with no rewards or new developments is drab. Rewards are the main source of a player's investment. Not every type fits every concept, though: in a fighting game where characters grow stronger, the "real" game seems to start only at max level. **Do:** Once the bones of the game exist, list every reward type and roughly when it arrives, and add systems before production if the list is thin. Make sure saved-up currency has appealing things to spend it on. If stat growth would undermine the concept, reward with unlocks, collectibles, options and objectives instead. Let rewards help with the next challenge, and build choice into them. **Watch out:** Don't lock what many players want from the start (music, for example), don't make shareable movies the payoff (PLAN-20), and don't go overboard.

*Sakurai's example:* Smash unlocks fighters by sending challengers for the player to beat, and its coins buy nothing that makes you stronger. Its collectibles moved from costly modeled trophies to stickers and spirits, which needed no models or descriptions. · Episodes: [151](../episodes/notes/151-planning-your-games-rewards.md), [75](../episodes/notes/075-consider-rewards-first.md), [9](../episodes/notes/009-squeeze-and-release.md), [163](../episodes/notes/163-fighter-reveal-videos.md)

### PLAN-17 · Keep the next objective visible and within reach
**Why:** Keeping rewards in sight and giving a clear objective both motivate players, and Sakurai thinks it a shame when a game doesn't keep you thinking about the next reward. Objectives are rewards in their own right. A hint of future developments makes even a short errand worth taking. **Do:** Show progress as current versus required (EXP now, EXP to the next level) and make every action move it. Keep key purchases few, expensive and powerful. Use a goal grid that reveals its neighbors' conditions as each panel clears. At points through a playthrough, ask what the player is working toward right now; if it's unclear or out of reach, add a nearer objective. If the game has no ending, put the goal so far away players can't see it. **Watch out:** Generous loot can kill this. When there's lots of money and lots of minor gear, no single purchase is worth saving for.

*Sakurai's example:* Ys shows your current EXP next to what you need to level up, so you always see how far you've come and how far is left. Faxanadu's few expensive shop items each give a massive boost. Kirby Air Ride's Checklist reveals neighboring conditions as squares clear. Cookie Clicker's exponential growth keeps the end out of sight. · Episodes: [23](../episodes/notes/023-keeping-rewards-in-sight.md), [151](../episodes/notes/151-planning-your-games-rewards.md), [59](../episodes/notes/059-kirby-air-ride.md), [64](../episodes/notes/064-good-errands-and-bad-errands.md), [203](../episodes/notes/203-making-work-games-fun.md), [128](../episodes/notes/128-is-your-game-appealing-enough-to-start-over.md)

### PLAN-18 · Shape progression as peaks and flats, not a steady incline
**Why:** A climb at one steady incline with unchanging scenery is just work. Keeping enemy strength in direct proportion to the player's removes the point of getting stronger. Stress followed by release is a core source of fun, and breathers make the next squeeze land. **Do:** Plot player power and challenge across the whole game and reshape the line into peaks (bosses that demand a strategy and pay off with a clear reward) and flats (an unexpectedly empty area inside a dense zone, a spot to rack up gains, a short scene as a breather). Vary the scenery along the way. In open or multi-path games, don't scale every enemy up with the player. **Watch out:** A boss that is just a beefed-up regular enemy is not a peak.

*Sakurai's example:* His mountain-trail analogy: a real climb has challenges to overcome and flat plains to breeze through, not just rest stops at regular intervals. · Episodes: [105](../episodes/notes/105-mountain-climbing-is-more-than-just-climbing.md), [9](../episodes/notes/009-squeeze-and-release.md), [71](../episodes/notes/071-do-we-really-need-enemies.md), [233](../episodes/notes/233-in-game-cutscenes.md), [15](../episodes/notes/015-game-essence-in-role-playing-games.md)

### PLAN-19 · Write story around play: the protagonist's view, the player's victories
**Why:** Game writing differs from other media in about four ways. Story can get in the way of play; it unfolds from the main character's perspective; the hero almost always needs to win; and comrades can't simply die or leave without upsetting party balance or costing the player items. Story isn't game essence, but it enhances it when it uses interactivity, driving players onward into risk. **Do:** Start with gameplay, not story, and brief players while they play, with an option to turn the dialogue off. Trim long backstory about allies and villains. Never let a cutscene undo a fight the player just won. If a companion must leave, protect the balance and items the player invested. Tie story beats to the challenge ahead, and give errands a mystery tied to your world. **Watch out:** These rules can be broken, and breaking them can make a story memorable. Just do it on purpose, knowing some players will be upset.

*Sakurai's example:* In Kid Icarus: Uprising, Pit runs through a door and play begins; the story unfolds during play like a radio program. His errand rewrite turns "Go buy me some meat!" into a search for a missing girl and a house where strange noises were heard. · Episodes: [113](../episodes/notes/113-game-writing-is-unique.md), [26](../episodes/notes/026-fun-beyond-game-essence.md), [125](../episodes/notes/125-kid-icarus-uprising.md), [64](../episodes/notes/064-good-errands-and-bad-errands.md), [235](../episodes/notes/235-start-with-the-climax.md)

### PLAN-20 · Keep cutscenes lean and purposeful, and never make them the reward
**Why:** Long stretches of fairly irrelevant cutscenes make players impatient, and leaner cutscenes suit modern games. Cutscenes still earn their place when they carry emotion, tell the story more clearly or give a breather. But movies used as rewards for progress end up online at or before launch, so they stop rewarding anyone. **Do:** Review every cutscene. Keep deliberate breathers and beats that play can't carry, and shorten or cut the ones that are just downtime. Treat showcase movies as marketing that everyone can watch. Test any quick-time event with players very carefully before committing to it. **Watch out:** Slowness used to build suspense may not land with today's players.

*Sakurai's example:* Brawl's per-fighter movies were meant to reward progress through the Subspace Emissary, but people posted them online from pre-release copies. He stopped putting movies in the middle of play and turned showcase movies into fighter reveal videos instead. · Episodes: [73](../episodes/notes/073-say-no-to-slow.md), [233](../episodes/notes/233-in-game-cutscenes.md), [163](../episodes/notes/163-fighter-reveal-videos.md), [17](../episodes/notes/017-just-let-them-play.md), [113](../episodes/notes/113-game-writing-is-unique.md)

### PLAN-21 · Let players skip, pause and speed through anything non-interactive
**Why:** A player with no save data isn't necessarily seeing a scene for the first time, and unskippable scenes are off-putting. People now watch video sped up, and everyone wants to enjoy as much as possible in as little time as possible. Players press buttons because they want something from the game. **Do:** Make every cutscene, logo and the credits skippable from the first playthrough, all at once if possible. Make cutscenes pausable. Offer line-by-line skip and fast-forward, or let voiced lines play while the player keeps playing. Have any button press during a cutscene bring up a skip prompt. **Watch out:** A scene that can be skipped but not paused still fails the player whose phone rings.

*Sakurai's example:* He singles out line-by-line dialogue skipping, which jumps straight to the start of the next line. He thinks whoever writes company logo guidelines should treat skippability as a given. · Episodes: [233](../episodes/notes/233-in-game-cutscenes.md), [180](../episodes/notes/180-let-them-skip-let-them-pause.md), [211](../episodes/notes/211-advancing-text.md), [104](../episodes/notes/104-dont-be-unresponsive.md)

### PLAN-22 · Collect every hard limit at kickoff and design inside it
**Why:** Age-rating, platform, storefront, online-service, internal and licensor guidelines must be met or the game can't ship, and when they block an intended feature, players may blame the developer. Hardware and media limits decide what's possible. Discovering any of these after a feature is built wastes the work. **Do:** At kickoff, list every guideline and hard limit (ratings, platform rules, regional rules on depicting gambling, storage size, performance on the weakest target) and check every planned visual and feature against it. Let the weakest platform set shared limits. Budget several rounds of adjustment for region-sensitive features. If you write guidelines yourself, state their purpose and allow justified exceptions. **Watch out:** Limits aren't only subtraction. Chapter 14 shows how often Sakurai turned them into design (CASE-10).

*Sakurai's example:* Smash's spectator mode, where viewers wager points on a match, needed careful, repeated adjustments for regional gambling rules. Ice Climbers were too heavy for the 3DS. Ultimate's cartridge capped the ROM at about 16 GB, and compression fit the music library into a quarter of its old space. A twin-stick peripheral would have added about 5% processor load to an Uprising already at the hardware's limits. · Episodes: [139](../episodes/notes/139-guidelines.md), [120](../episodes/notes/120-the-fun-in-picking-sides.md), [135](../episodes/notes/135-super-smash-bros-for-3ds-wii-u.md), [160](../episodes/notes/160-super-smash-bros-ultimate.md), [125](../episodes/notes/125-kid-icarus-uprising.md), [100](../episodes/notes/100-development-secrets-of-the-original-kirby.md)

### PLAN-23 · Budget the hidden costs each feature drags in
**Why:** Features cost more than building them. Growth and parameter systems multiply the test matrix (a game that takes dozens of hours to finish may need thousands to tens of thousands of hours of testing). Every one-of-a-kind character mechanic needs its own programming. A demo becomes a second build to maintain and test. Online updates mean development has no clean end. Heavy content formats cap how much you can include. **Do:** Before approving a feature, estimate its testing, code and maintenance cost. Schedule programmer time for each unique mechanic, reserve people for post-launch fixes, and plan any demo as a small separate game that needs QA. When volume matters, pick a lightweight format (an image plus a gameplay effect rather than a model and a written description). **Watch out:** Treating DLC and updates as free stretches the schedule until the project feels like it will never end.

*Sakurai's example:* Spirits made testing Ultimate an enormous job. Brawl's adventure mode needed a second set of movement parameters for every fighter. Trophies inflated costs, while stickers and spirits let far more characters in. ### PLAN-24 · Deliver volume as short, distinct chunks with their own rules
**Why:** When too many games took too long to beat, Sakurai built Kirby Super Star as an omnibus of quick scenarios, each a different game with its own rules and theme, so it could offer lots of content without players tiring of any one style. Chunks that play alike lose the benefit. **Do:** Split content into self-contained modes or segments, chosen from a hub, each with a rule no other has. If two end up playing the same, cut one or change one. Use fixed-length segments where you want music or events to land on exact beats. Give competitive sessions short, concrete goals. **Watch out:** One late request can blur a mode's identity. Guard what sets each chunk apart.

*Sakurai's example:* Kirby Super Star's cork board of scenarios included a food-eating racing game and a story-driven adventure. Its first scenario, Spring Breeze, got the copy ability at a producer's request, and Sakurai still wishes it could be played without it, since it no longer stands apart from the next scenario. Smash Run gives players five minutes to collect power-ups before a showdown. ### PLAN-25 · Give every character and option an identity in a word or two
**Why:** Characters that are basically the same are no fun. Pushing each one far from a workable neutral baseline creates strategy and makes them memorable. Options with markedly distinct pros and cons set customization apart, and a feature that could be generic can become individuality instead. **Do:** Define a neutral baseline for your rules, then push each character's parameters well away from it, giving some a one-of-a-kind mechanic with programming budgeted for it. Write a one- or two-word identity for every character, enemy and option, and push further if you can't. Give each option a clear trade-off, and let players test it right away. **Watch out:** Eccentricity that makes something unusable, like a ship that fires everywhere except forward, is pointless.

*Sakurai's example:* Kirby's Gordo is invincible, never attacks and doesn't move; dull alone, it becomes significant through where it's placed. Every Ultimate DLC fighter has its own system. Brawl gave each fighter a unique Final Smash rather than a generic power item. · Episodes: [206](../episodes/notes/206-using-parameters-to-establish-characters.md), [182](../episodes/notes/182-customization-lets-your-imagination-play.md), [108](../episodes/notes/108-super-smash-bros-brawl.md), [74](../episodes/notes/074-meteos.md), [218](../episodes/notes/218-average-and-mediocre-are-the-same-thing.md)

### PLAN-26 · Spend a sequel's head start on purpose, and make it clearly more fun
**Why:** Making the same thing again roughly halves sales, and fans who see the same scale and similar content feel they've already played it. Games need a system, structure and tools before content can be made, so a sequel can spend that head start on expanding the system or on a big lead in content. **Do:** At sequel kickoff, list what carries over (team, tools, assets, hardware architecture) and decide where the saved effort goes. Write down how the sequel is more fun and more impactful than the last game, and list concrete changes for every element. Re-examine inherited rules. If the tempo changes, retune every parameter. **Watch out:** A sequel won't sell on its name if it isn't well made, and a bad experience with one entry costs you the next.

*Sakurai's example:* Melee's concept was simply to power up every element of Smash. Ultimate's "Everyone is here!" was only feasible because the team and tools carried over; he listed 70 to 100 potential changes per fighter. · Episodes: [164](../episodes/notes/164-making-sequels.md), [40](../episodes/notes/040-super-smash-bros-melee.md), [160](../episodes/notes/160-super-smash-bros-ultimate.md), [82](../episodes/notes/082-do-competitive-games-have-to-be-complex.md), [135](../episodes/notes/135-super-smash-bros-for-3ds-wii-u.md)

## Tensions and how they fit

- **Plan everything vs. discover it in the build.** For his channel Sakurai wrote every script before recording ([261](../episodes/notes/261-masahiro-sakurai-on-creating-games-finale-special.md)), and he set Uprising's story before development really kicked off ([113](../episodes/notes/113-game-writing-is-unique.md)). Yet he also warns against documenting the whole game up front, because fun surfaces in the build ([29](../episodes/notes/029-spec-changes.md), [52](../episodes/notes/052-prep-your-proposals-hot-and-fast.md), [164](../episodes/notes/164-making-sequels.md), [158](../episodes/notes/158-the-majority-shouldnt-always-rule.md)). *When each applies:* the more knowable the content, the more you can plan. Scripted talks, story beats that must sync to fixed-length segments, and fixed content counts can all be written in advance. Novel mechanics can't, so fix their concept and core rules and leave the details to prototypes.
- **Never change course vs. cut your losses.** Never changing the concept's trajectory ([187](../episodes/notes/187-staying-true-to-your-concept.md)) sits beside "Don't dig in your heels" on an idea that isn't working ([29](../episodes/notes/029-spec-changes.md)), "get out while you still can" ([110](../episodes/notes/110-canceled-games.md)) and the Kirby Air Ride rebuild on new specs ([59](../episodes/notes/059-kirby-air-ride.md)). *How they fit:* the concept is the destination and the specs are the route. Change routes freely. If the destination itself is unreachable, cancel early, before the costly final stretch, rather than drifting into a different game midway. The Air Ride rebuild kept the game and its assets and replaced the specs and logic.
- **Question every given vs. use the standards.** Re-examining givens drives his originality ([82](../episodes/notes/082-do-competitive-games-have-to-be-complex.md), [74](../episodes/notes/074-meteos.md)). But he also tells you to use de facto control standards so you can skip explanations ([175](../episodes/notes/175-its-easier-if-they-already-know.md)), and to keep a series' basic rules consistent ([139](../episodes/notes/139-guidelines.md)). *How they fit:* question the rules that create push and pull; keep conventions that are pure interface (button layout, piece colors) unless your mechanic needs otherwise, and add remapping either way.
- **Teach by playing vs. leave players free.** Sakurai insists you let players play while you teach ([173](../episodes/notes/173-teaching-players-how-to-play.md), [85](../episodes/notes/085-making-tutorials-feel-natural.md)), yet warns that hand-holding makes them feel dragged along ([242](../episodes/notes/242-being-kind-to-beginners.md)) and calls it "quite the conundrum" ([173](../episodes/notes/173-teaching-players-how-to-play.md)). A boss with one crafted strategy is "almost like a tutorial" ([219](../episodes/notes/219-mandatory-strategies-and-strategic-balance.md)). *Resolution:* teach through the real game, keep each lesson to the moment it's needed, and fade it once the player shows they have it.
- **Trim cutscenes vs. respect them.** He calls needless cutscenes downtime but defends them as palate cleansers, emotional beats and clearer storytelling ([73](../episodes/notes/073-say-no-to-slow.md), [233](../episodes/notes/233-in-game-cutscenes.md)). *Judge each scene by its job*, not by its length alone.
- **Play time is a cost vs. games built to absorb time.** He says length should never be a selling point ([250](../episodes/notes/250-be-mindful-of-the-time-players-are-giving-you.md)), yet admires "work" games whose goal sits out of sight ([203](../episodes/notes/203-making-work-games-fun.md)). *How they fit:* long play is welcome when every minute is fun; what he objects to is length as a target or advertising line, and repetition with nothing new to look forward to ([128](../episodes/notes/128-is-your-game-appealing-enough-to-start-over.md)).
- **Brief proposals vs. 200-page decks.** "Include only what you need" ([52](../episodes/notes/052-prep-your-proposals-hot-and-fast.md)) and "It doesn't have to be short" ([12](../episodes/notes/012-presentations-are-all-about-speed.md)) both sit beside a 200-plus-page Ultimate proposal ([38](../episodes/notes/038-writing-game-proposals.md), [160](../episodes/notes/160-super-smash-bros-ultimate.md)). *Resolution:* brevity is per page. One idea, one image, two lines, no padding; the page count follows from the content.
- **Read the market vs. ignore market research.** He tells you to choose your audience from a top-down view of the market, not your own taste ([112](../episodes/notes/112-branching-tastes.md)), and also not to let targeting and research generate ideas ([226](../episodes/notes/226-targeting-and-market-research.md)). *How they fit:* use the market to decide *who* you're serving, and your concept to decide *what* to make; then use data only to check the risks.

## Common mistakes

- Starting production before anyone can state the concept in one sentence, then changing direction when the game "isn't turning out" ([187](../episodes/notes/187-staying-true-to-your-concept.md)).
- Writing the whole design document before building anything, or defending it against what the build shows ([29](../episodes/notes/029-spec-changes.md)).
- Proposals that hedge with "or we could do this", linger on the grand vision, or are read aloud slide by slide ([109](../episodes/notes/109-dont-rely-on-a-plan-b.md), [52](../episodes/notes/052-prep-your-proposals-hot-and-fast.md), [38](../episodes/notes/038-writing-game-proposals.md)).
- Pitching the game as "X, but with Y" ([161](../episodes/notes/161-avoiding-comparison.md)).
- Specs with unitless numbers, vague timing words and terse parameter names ([68](../episodes/notes/068-put-parameters-in-brackets.md), [84](../episodes/notes/084-units-of-speed.md)).
- Opening with logos, lore and relationships before the first input ([17](../episodes/notes/017-just-let-them-play.md), [235](../episodes/notes/235-start-with-the-climax.md)).
- Manual-style tutorial text, dummy training rooms, or constant prompts that take away discovery ([173](../episodes/notes/173-teaching-players-how-to-play.md), [242](../episodes/notes/242-being-kind-to-beginners.md)).
- Explaining conventions genre players already know, without testing on people who don't ([175](../episodes/notes/175-its-easier-if-they-already-know.md)).
- Slow fades, long death sequences, unskippable or unpausable cutscenes, and treks back to town ([73](../episodes/notes/073-say-no-to-slow.md), [117](../episodes/notes/117-make-retries-quick.md), [180](../episodes/notes/180-let-them-skip-let-them-pause.md), [64](../episodes/notes/064-good-errands-and-bad-errands.md)).
- Selling or planning around an hour count; padding to hit it ([250](../episodes/notes/250-be-mindful-of-the-time-players-are-giving-you.md)).
- Rewards bolted on late, or growth systems that break a game built on a fixed baseline ([151](../episodes/notes/151-planning-your-games-rewards.md)).
- Enemy strength in lockstep with the player's, and bosses that are just bigger enemies ([105](../episodes/notes/105-mountain-climbing-is-more-than-just-climbing.md)).
- Cutscenes that undo the player's win, or movies used as the reward for progress ([113](../episodes/notes/113-game-writing-is-unique.md), [163](../episodes/notes/163-fighter-reveal-videos.md)).
- Leaving the hands-on tutorial as an extra that gets cut at the end ([173](../episodes/notes/173-teaching-players-how-to-play.md)).
- Adding parameter, growth or unlock systems without budgeting their test hours ([136](../episodes/notes/136-bug-testing-never-ends.md)).
- Finding a guideline or hardware limit after the feature is built ([139](../episodes/notes/139-guidelines.md)).

## Review questions

1. Within three minutes of first boot, is the player in control, and has the core fun shown itself?
2. What share of a session is the player in control, and what's the longest stretch without control?
3. Can the director state the concept and the audience in one sentence, and does every current task serve it?
4. Has the core fun been played in a build before content production started?
5. At any moment, what is the player working toward, and can they see how far they have left?
6. If the schedule halved tomorrow, what goes, what is locked, and what hidden costs are we carrying?
7. Could a teammate implement and tune this spec without asking a single question?
8. Does any cutscene or story beat stop play, undo the player's win, or serve as the reward?
9. Described without naming another game, what does ours offer that players can't get anywhere else?

## Exercises

1. **The three-minute test (1 hour).** Find someone who has never played your game. Start a timer at boot and note when they first have control and when they first say or show that they get it. Cut whatever stands before that moment until it falls under three minutes. Then make repeated presses of confirm go straight into play.
2. **The control-time audit (2 hours).** Record a typical session. Tag each stretch as in control, not in control or empty. Total the buckets, cut or shorten the three worst offenders (a slow fade, a death sequence, a walk back), and re-measure.
3. **The one-page plan (half a day).** Write: the one-line concept naming the audience or hook; the first minute of play; the core rules; the reward list with when each arrives; the must-ship list and the trim list. No alternatives allowed. Then turn it into a comic-panel deck (one image, two lines per page) in one sitting, and present it to a teammate in under ten minutes.
4. **The unmisreadable spec (2 hours).** Take one move or mechanic and rewrite its spec with bracketed plain-language parameters, units in every speed and timings in frames. Hand it to a teammate and have them implement it without asking you anything. Every question they need to ask marks a hole in the spec.
5. **Peaks and flats (2 hours).** List your levels or encounters in order. Rate the challenge of each and draw the line. Mark the peaks (each should demand a strategy and pay off) and the flats (breathers, farming spots, an empty room). Fix one stretch that is a steady incline.
6. **Story and cutscene triage (2 hours).** Tag every cutscene as emotional/clarity beat, breather or downtime. Cut or shorten the downtime, check that no scene undoes a player win, and make sure every scene, including the credits, can be skipped and paused.
