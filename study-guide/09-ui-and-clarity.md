# 09 · UI and Clarity

> Sakurai treats the interface as the part of a game where respect for the player is easiest to see, or to miss. Menus, HUDs, help text, text boxes, loading screens and button settings are where a player decides, many times an hour, whether the game is on their side. His stance has two halves. First, clarity is designed for the person who does *not* already know: the newcomer, the player reading in a foreign language, the player with a different controller, body or TV. Second, clarity is not the same as blandness. UI is the wrapping the whole world comes in, so it should carry the game's style, as long as that style never costs the player time or understanding. Get this wrong and players feel lost, slowed down or ignored, however good the game underneath is.

## The big ideas

**The player came to play, so everything between the press and the fun is a cost.** Logos, cutscenes, text crawls, slow fades and five-step menus are all time when the player isn't playing. Sakurai's answer is the same across many episodes: let them skip and pause anything non-interactive, let text move at reading speed, make the confirm button lead straight into the game, and count the inputs to every common choice ([180](../episodes/notes/180-let-them-skip-let-them-pause.md), [211](../episodes/notes/211-advancing-text.md), [240](../episodes/notes/240-making-things-clear-even-without-words.md), [169](../episodes/notes/169-i-want-to-choose-fast.md), [73](../episodes/notes/073-say-no-to-slow.md)). Every press should get a reaction, because "Players press buttons because they want something from the game." ([104](../episodes/notes/104-dont-be-unresponsive.md), ≈01:35)

**Design for the person who doesn't know.** Help text exists for the player who doesn't know what "Options" means, so it must never repeat the word ([229](../episodes/notes/229-adding-help-text-to-menus.md)). Pictures and color are read faster than words, so icons get pictures, sections get colors, and the most-used options get the biggest tiles ([240](../episodes/notes/240-making-things-clear-even-without-words.md), [133](../episodes/notes/133-color-coding-your-game.md), [147](../episodes/notes/147-make-important-elements-bigger.md)). Text has to be sized for each language and read on the real screen ([33](../episodes/notes/033-text-size.md)). His ideal: "Ideally, games should be playable even if you can't read a single word." ([240](../episodes/notes/240-making-things-clear-even-without-words.md), ≈02:12)

**Style is part of the job, not an enemy of clarity.** "Fonts and UI are both a part of a game's worldbuilding. You might even say they're the package that the world is wrapped in." ([111](../episodes/notes/111-menus-define-your-world.md), ≈01:23) Persona's menus are hard to parse, but generic menus would ruin them ([18](../episodes/notes/018-clarity-vs-style.md)). The HUD should be easy to use and fun to look at ([254](../episodes/notes/254-huds.md)): portraits, moving backgrounds and meters that break, but also markers that hide themselves when they'd clutter the screen.

**Let players set the game up their way.** Players arrive with different controllers, bodies, eyes, TVs and habits, so remapping, accessibility options and previews are basic features, not extras ([197](../episodes/notes/197-button-settings.md), [55](../episodes/notes/055-modern-quality-of-life-features.md), [129](../episodes/notes/129-motion-sickness-in-3d-games.md)). Wherever an option would make the game less entertaining for others (less camera shake, less blur, easier fights), offer it as a choice instead of removing the effect for everyone.

**Where episodes pull in different directions:**

- *Clarity vs. style.* Clear UI isn't automatically good UI, and stylish UI isn't automatically bad ([18](../episodes/notes/018-clarity-vs-style.md)). Large text reads better but can stop looking stylish ([33](../episodes/notes/033-text-size.md)); heavy color can make a game look aimed at children ([133](../episodes/notes/133-color-coding-your-game.md)). Settle it per game from its content and audience, then keep a floor: every word legible at real screen size ([111](../episodes/notes/111-menus-define-your-world.md)).
- *Weighted vs. equal menus.* Size items by importance when a few dominate, but keep a plain list when everything is used equally, because weighting costs versatility and makes translation harder ([147](../episodes/notes/147-make-important-elements-bigger.md)).
- *Speed for experts vs. broad appeal.* Advanced shortcuts speed up experts but beginners don't use them, so layer them over a simple default path ([169](../episodes/notes/169-i-want-to-choose-fast.md)).
- *More HUD vs. less.* Japanese games tend to show more and overseas games less, for immersion; Sakurai keeps everything on as a player. Auto-hiding and transparency let you have both ([254](../episodes/notes/254-huds.md)).
- *Fast text vs. the scene you staged.* You want players to appreciate your performances, but they also want to get through. Support both speeds rather than forcing one ([211](../episodes/notes/211-advancing-text.md)).
- *Hide loading or show it.* A transition that hides a load perfectly (Resident Evil's doors) needs no icon. Otherwise show an indicator right away, and never build a presentation around development load times, which will shrink ([78](../episodes/notes/078-loading-screens.md), [246](../episodes/notes/246-speedy-screen-transitions.md)).
- *Fixed controls vs. remapping.* Early Nintendo hardware gained something from fixed schemes, and genre-standard defaults are worth keeping ([55](../episodes/notes/055-modern-quality-of-life-features.md), [175](../episodes/notes/175-its-easier-if-they-already-know.md)). Modern controllers and players are too varied, so ship standard defaults *and* remapping.

## Principles

### UI-01 · Let players skip and pause anything they are not controlling
**Why:** During logos, movies, intermissions and cutscenes nobody is playing. A player with no save data may still have seen the scene before, and phone calls, visitors and dinner interrupt scenes. **Do:** One press skips a whole sequence, credits included, even on a new save. Any press during a cutscene shows a skip prompt. Every cutscene can be paused. If you write logo guidelines, make skippable the default. **Watch out:** Locking skip until a scene has been seen once, or allowing skip but not pause. Online games that keep advancing are the only accepted exception for pausing.
*Sakurai's example:* "When the player's not in control, make sure they can skip what you're showing them—I can't emphasize that enough." ([180](../episodes/notes/180-let-them-skip-let-them-pause.md), ≈01:55) · Episodes: [180](../episodes/notes/180-let-them-skip-let-them-pause.md), [233](../episodes/notes/233-in-game-cutscenes.md), [104](../episodes/notes/104-dont-be-unresponsive.md), [73](../episodes/notes/073-say-no-to-slow.md), [250](../episodes/notes/250-be-mindful-of-the-time-players-are-giving-you.md)

### UI-02 · Let players read and advance text at their own speed
**Why:** People read quickly, and zipping through text feels good. Staged scenes can't match a reader's pace, and viewers now choose their own playback speed on video sites. **Do:** The first press completes the text box and the next advances it. Voiced scenes get a skip-to-next-line as well as skip-all. Where possible, play dialogue during gameplay. **Watch out:** One fixed, slow pace for everyone. Support slow readers too: it is a choice, not a race.
*Sakurai's example:* Line-by-line skipping that jumps straight to the start of the next line, which he praises as effortless ([211](../episodes/notes/211-advancing-text.md)). Kid Icarus: Uprising tells its story during play like a radio program, with an option to turn it off ([125](../episodes/notes/125-kid-icarus-uprising.md)). · Episodes: [211](../episodes/notes/211-advancing-text.md), [233](../episodes/notes/233-in-game-cutscenes.md), [180](../episodes/notes/180-let-them-skip-let-them-pause.md), [125](../episodes/notes/125-kid-icarus-uprising.md)

### UI-03 · Ship in-game button remapping in every game, however simple
**Why:** It's a necessity for some players. Console-wide remaps affect every game and can leave actions unmapped, and no default layout suits everyone. **Do:** Draw the controller graphically. Let players cursor onto a button and pick its action from a list, allow several buttons per action, save layouts under profile names, and store them on the controller where possible. Confirm remaps with a long press when directions are assignable, show each action's default, split maps by context, and never let an essential action end up unbound. **Watch out:** Press-the-button-to-assign screens, which can capture the menu's own exit button, and vertical lists that lose track of the layout.
*Sakurai's example:* Smash Bros. graphical controllers, two jump buttons and name-based profiles for guest players ([197](../episodes/notes/197-button-settings.md)). The Last of Us Part I's long-press confirmation and visible defaults ([55](../episodes/notes/055-modern-quality-of-life-features.md)). Remapping Pac-Land's press-A-to-run-right, mash-B-to-run-left scheme ([156](../episodes/notes/156-special-crossover-2.md)). · Episodes: [197](../episodes/notes/197-button-settings.md), [55](../episodes/notes/055-modern-quality-of-life-features.md), [175](../episodes/notes/175-its-easier-if-they-already-know.md), [156](../episodes/notes/156-special-crossover-2.md), [85](../episodes/notes/085-making-tutorials-feel-natural.md)

### UI-04 · Answer every button press, and accept confirm on several buttons
**Why:** Play is a two-way flow, and an action with no reaction feels lacking. If L also confirms, players can even play one-handed. **Do:** Accept confirm on the main button plus another, put one- or two-action games' actions on several buttons, show a skip prompt for presses in cutscenes, and give every accepted press a clear sound or highlight. Audit each game state for dead buttons. **Watch out:** Ambiguous feedback that leaves players unsure the press registered. Silence is fine only when it is deliberate.
*Sakurai's example:* L as an extra confirm button ([104](../episodes/notes/104-dont-be-unresponsive.md)); two jump buttons in Smash ([197](../episodes/notes/197-button-settings.md)). · Episodes: [104](../episodes/notes/104-dont-be-unresponsive.md), [197](../episodes/notes/197-button-settings.md)

### UI-05 · Make the game playable without reading a single word
**Why:** Pictures are recognized faster than text and parsed by intuition, and many players won't read your language. **Do:** Put a picture or character on every major icon and a color on every section. Set default cursors so pressing confirm over and over from the title reaches the main mode, tutorial included. Test the menus in a language you can't read. **Watch out:** Text-only menus, and default cursors parked on Options or Exit. The path often happens by accident; set it on purpose.
*Sakurai's example:* Mashing confirm reaches Smash mode's fighter select in Ultimate, the first Air Ride stage in Kirby Air Ride, and the first scenario with its tutorial in Kirby Super Star ([240](../episodes/notes/240-making-things-clear-even-without-words.md)). · Episodes: [240](../episodes/notes/240-making-things-clear-even-without-words.md), [133](../episodes/notes/133-color-coding-your-game.md), [147](../episodes/notes/147-make-important-elements-bigger.md)

### UI-06 · Size and lay out text for every language and the real screen
**Why:** Kanji need bigger but fewer characters, English runs longer at smaller sizes, and French longer still. English-only layouts leave translations too small, and developers who check on the real handheld spot small text fast. **Do:** Leave room per language, read every screen in every language on the smallest real display, mock labels in the longest language early, and offer a font-size option where space allows. **Watch out:** Approving text on a big dev monitor. At the other extreme, text so large it stops looking stylish.
*Sakurai's example:* Japanese, English and French text compared ([33](../episodes/notes/033-text-size.md)). Smash's icon menus were hard to fit in wider languages ([147](../episodes/notes/147-make-important-elements-bigger.md)). · Episodes: [33](../episodes/notes/033-text-size.md), [147](../episodes/notes/147-make-important-elements-bigger.md), [55](../episodes/notes/055-modern-quality-of-life-features.md), [111](../episodes/notes/111-menus-define-your-world.md)

### UI-07 · Write help text and names in plain words, never the item's name
**Why:** Menu items are one or two words that often don't say what they do, and help text exists for people who don't know the term. **Do:** Show a one-line description of the highlighted item. Rewrite it in different words, cut it down to the key meaning, and translate meaning rather than words. Name modes by what the player does, and name moves in input order. **Watch out:** "Go to the Options", which he says happens quite often, and jargon like "config" or "key repeat".
*Sakurai's example:* "This is important, so I'll say it again: don't explain using the same words!" ([229](../episodes/notes/229-adding-help-text-to-menus.md), ≈00:54). Options becomes "Adjust a variety of game settings", and "Play Solo" beats "1P Mode" ([229](../episodes/notes/229-adding-help-text-to-menus.md)). Players see "Up Special" while the file label is SpecialHi ([22](../episodes/notes/022-name-files-logically.md)). · Episodes: [229](../episodes/notes/229-adding-help-text-to-menus.md), [22](../episodes/notes/022-name-files-logically.md)

### UI-08 · Offer comfort and accessibility options, grouped, previewed and safe
**Why:** Players differ in needs, bodies and screens. Removing presentation for everyone makes the game less entertaining, so offer options. A stuck player who gives up gets nothing from your challenge. **Do:** Build a dedicated Accessibility menu that links options from other menus and has presets. Put difficulty-lowering assists behind an extra unlock step. Include full subtitle options, a screen reader, colorblind and high-contrast modes, motion-sickness toggles (head-bob off, locked cockpit frame, center dot), blur strength, flick sensitivity, a cutscene-vibration toggle, and difficulty changes at any time. **Watch out:** Signing off camera comfort with a team that never gets motion sick, and assists that switch on by accident.
*Sakurai's example:* The Last of Us Part I's options suite ([55](../episodes/notes/055-modern-quality-of-life-features.md)); FPS footstep-shake toggles ([129](../episodes/notes/129-motion-sickness-in-3d-games.md)); blur sliders ([192](../episodes/notes/192-motion-blur.md)); adjustable flick sensitivity in Smash Ultimate ([154](../episodes/notes/154-flick-input.md)). · Episodes: [55](../episodes/notes/055-modern-quality-of-life-features.md), [129](../episodes/notes/129-motion-sickness-in-3d-games.md), [192](../episodes/notes/192-motion-blur.md), [154](../episodes/notes/154-flick-input.md), [213](../episodes/notes/213-better-than-not-being-able-to-beat-the-game.md), [214](../episodes/notes/214-a-world-with-less-color.md)

### UI-09 · Make choosing fast by cutting steps to the most common choices
**Why:** Of all inputs, menu selection is the one Sakurai most wants to be quick. Tapping an analog stick over and over is unsatisfying, and clicky, tactile steps make even selection feel good. **Do:** Count the inputs to frequent choices and cut them. Support crisp D-pad steps, layer optional shortcuts over a simple default path, offer equip-strongest, and try any new input device (wheel, touch, gyro) as a way to select faster. **Watch out:** Expert-only shortcut systems as the main path, and shoehorning in every function of a new peripheral.
*Sakurai's example:* Since the GameCube and Melee he has proposed a clicky scroll wheel, then called a jog dial, for fast menu selection ([169](../episodes/notes/169-i-want-to-choose-fast.md)). · Episodes: [169](../episodes/notes/169-i-want-to-choose-fast.md), [147](../episodes/notes/147-make-important-elements-bigger.md), [182](../episodes/notes/182-customization-lets-your-imagination-play.md), [240](../episodes/notes/240-making-things-clear-even-without-words.md), [154](../episodes/notes/154-flick-input.md)

### UI-10 · Size menu elements by how often players use them
**Why:** Size shows priority and gives the fastest access to what matters. The GameCube controller's big A button follows the same idea. **Do:** Rank options by use, make the top ones the biggest tiles, and shrink the rare ones. **Watch out:** Weighting options that are actually used equally, which costs versatility, and icon labels that won't fit in translation.
*Sakurai's example:* Icon menus of varying size from Meteos onward, including Smash ([147](../episodes/notes/147-make-important-elements-bigger.md)). · Episodes: [147](../episodes/notes/147-make-important-elements-bigger.md), [18](../episodes/notes/018-clarity-vs-style.md), [44](../episodes/notes/044-distinguishing-between-major-and-minor-elements.md)

### UI-11 · Settle clarity versus style per game, and organize menus like drawers
**Why:** "A game's UI is like a drawer. When you pull it open, you want things to be organized in a sensible way." ([18](../episodes/notes/018-clarity-vs-style.md), ≈01:06) Feature-packed games need drawers on top of drawers, and crafting games drown players in materials. **Do:** Decide early where the UI sits between clarity and style, plan the window hierarchy to fit the content, and review every menu for visual guidance, element size, hierarchy, brightness and saturation, and typeface. **Watch out:** Assuming the clearest UI is automatically best, and replacing a signature style with generic menus.
*Sakurai's example:* Persona 5's stylish but hard-to-parse menus; Minecraft players building warehouses as item folders ([18](../episodes/notes/018-clarity-vs-style.md)). · Episodes: [18](../episodes/notes/018-clarity-vs-style.md), [111](../episodes/notes/111-menus-define-your-world.md), [133](../episodes/notes/133-color-coding-your-game.md), [254](../episodes/notes/254-huds.md)

### UI-12 · Keep the HUD informative without crowding the play area
**Why:** A HUD is useful but crowds the screen, and tracking markers would be an eyesore if always visible. Displays may crop the edges: CRTs showed only about 490 of 525 scanlines. **Do:** Auto-hide markers based on distance and situation, fade panels when a character moves behind them, keep essentials out of the edges, and offer HUD size and hiding options. **Watch out:** HUD elements that cover the characters they track.
*Sakurai's example:* Smash's self-hiding player cursors and semi-transparent HUD ([254](../episodes/notes/254-huds.md)). Kirby's Adventure enlarged its bottom panel, which shrank the play area, made characters read bigger and showed the copy ability's picture ([11](../episodes/notes/011-kirbys-adventure.md)). · Episodes: [254](../episodes/notes/254-huds.md), [252](../episodes/notes/252-crt-displays.md), [11](../episodes/notes/011-kirbys-adventure.md), [55](../episodes/notes/055-modern-quality-of-life-features.md)

### UI-13 · Put navigation information where the player is already looking
**Why:** Sakurai often feels he is just playing on the mini-map while the rendered world goes unseen. "It's important to consider where players will look while they're playing your game. And it's even more important to anticipate what they'll be thinking." ([119](../episodes/notes/119-maps-are-game-screens-too.md), ≈02:02) **Do:** Watch where eyes go in playtests. Try a translucent full-screen map with a map-only button. Put guidance in the world itself (corner markers, terrain that leads), and offer optional aids like destination arrows and traversal audio cues. **Watch out:** Making players open and close a full map over and over.
*Sakurai's example:* Mystery Dungeon's translucent map overlay and Forza's corner guidance ([119](../episodes/notes/119-maps-are-game-screens-too.md)); The Last of Us Part I's navigation aids ([55](../episodes/notes/055-modern-quality-of-life-features.md)). · Episodes: [119](../episodes/notes/119-maps-are-game-screens-too.md), [55](../episodes/notes/055-modern-quality-of-life-features.md), [42](../episodes/notes/042-a-small-window-into-the-world.md)

### UI-14 · Show loading honestly and in your world's style, never timed to dev builds
**Why:** A wait is easier to accept when the game shows it is loading, and a wait that feels like a mistake irritates most. Load times usually shrink before release. **Do:** Build one minimal, world-styled indicator that appears instantly for any duration. Use load-hiding transitions only if they still work with shorter loads, and keep transitions short but present. **Watch out:** Plain "Now Loading..." text, and an icon placed over a transition that already hides the load.
*Sakurai's example:* The first Resident Evil's doors hide loading so well that an icon would ruin them ([78](../episodes/notes/078-loading-screens.md), [246](../episodes/notes/246-speedy-screen-transitions.md)). · Episodes: [78](../episodes/notes/078-loading-screens.md), [246](../episodes/notes/246-speedy-screen-transitions.md), [73](../episodes/notes/073-say-no-to-slow.md), [117](../episodes/notes/117-make-retries-quick.md)

### UI-15 · Never let color alone carry information players need
**Why:** If a game relies on color alone, color-blind players can't play it. **Do:** Check color-coded elements with a color-blindness filter, separate merging hues by brightness, and back color up with shape or icon. Offer high-contrast modes. **Watch out:** Darkening pieces until they vanish into the background.
*Sakurai's example:* Meteos blocks checked under a protanopia filter and given big brightness differences ([214](../episodes/notes/214-a-world-with-less-color.md)). · Episodes: [214](../episodes/notes/214-a-world-with-less-color.md), [55](../episodes/notes/055-modern-quality-of-life-features.md), [133](../episodes/notes/133-color-coding-your-game.md)

### UI-16 · Color-code menu sections, one dominant color per screen, fit to tone
**Why:** A color per section tells players where they are without reading, but a mishmash of colors becomes a mess, and heavy color can make a game read as aimed at kids. **Do:** Give each top-level section a color carried into its submenus, keep one dominant color per screen, and set saturation from the game's tone. **Watch out:** A color scheme that fights the game's tone and manner.
*Sakurai's example:* In Smash Ultimate, Smash is red and Spirits is green, all the way down ([133](../episodes/notes/133-color-coding-your-game.md)). · Episodes: [133](../episodes/notes/133-color-coding-your-game.md), [18](../episodes/notes/018-clarity-vs-style.md), [240](../episodes/notes/240-making-things-clear-even-without-words.md)

### UI-17 · Treat fonts, frames and icons as worldbuilding, but keep them legible
**Why:** The same content can target a different audience just by changing font and UI, which is much easier than redesigning characters. **Do:** Mock one screen in several styles and pick one. Theme frames, icons, colors, loading indicators and wipes, budget for a custom font if needed, and check legibility at real size. **Watch out:** Default fonts and neutral "afterthought" menus, and styling so heavy that text stops being legible.
*Sakurai's example:* One options screen shown as slick, cute, horror, flashy, digital, Japanese, Victorian, Chinese and South Asian ([111](../episodes/notes/111-menus-define-your-world.md)). · Episodes: [111](../episodes/notes/111-menus-define-your-world.md), [78](../episodes/notes/078-loading-screens.md), [18](../episodes/notes/018-clarity-vs-style.md), [246](../episodes/notes/246-speedy-screen-transitions.md), [133](../episodes/notes/133-color-coding-your-game.md)

### UI-18 · Show what an option will do before the player commits
**Why:** Customizing feels like a chore unless players can see what it will get them, and numbers alone are less intuitive. **Do:** Draw effects such as attack range, show pros and cons side by side, give settings live previews (a dark and a bright scene for brightness or HDR), and let players test builds right away. **Watch out:** Differences shown only as stat numbers.
*Sakurai's example:* The Last of Us Part I's HUD-size preview window, which he counts as a quality-of-life feature in itself ([55](../episodes/notes/055-modern-quality-of-life-features.md)); short clips beside skill descriptions ([85](../episodes/notes/085-making-tutorials-feel-natural.md)). · Episodes: [182](../episodes/notes/182-customization-lets-your-imagination-play.md), [55](../episodes/notes/055-modern-quality-of-life-features.md), [85](../episodes/notes/085-making-tutorials-feel-natural.md)

### UI-19 · Make large inventories quick to sort, tell apart and equip
**Why:** Players pile up items, choosing slows down, and look-alike icons cause struggle. **Do:** Add sorting, categories, visually distinct icons and an equip-strongest option, which he calls essential. In libraries, let players make folders. **Watch out:** Unsorted lists of near-identical icons.
*Sakurai's example:* Customization screens ([182](../episodes/notes/182-customization-lets-your-imagination-play.md)); Minecraft warehouses ([18](../episodes/notes/018-clarity-vs-style.md)); his wish for folders in digital libraries ([58](../episodes/notes/058-team-digital-or-team-physical.md)). · Episodes: [182](../episodes/notes/182-customization-lets-your-imagination-play.md), [18](../episodes/notes/018-clarity-vs-style.md), [58](../episodes/notes/058-team-digital-or-team-physical.md)

### UI-20 · Keep the next goal and the distance to it on screen
**Why:** Showing current against required tells players how far they have come and how far is left, which keeps them pushing. **Do:** Show current versus required for the next reward and move it on every gain. Consider a checklist grid that reveals its neighbors' conditions. **Watch out:** Hiding how close the next reward is.
*Sakurai's example:* Ys (TurboGrafx-16 version) EXP display ([23](../episodes/notes/023-keeping-rewards-in-sight.md)); Kirby Air Ride's Checklist ([59](../episodes/notes/059-kirby-air-ride.md)). · Episodes: [23](../episodes/notes/023-keeping-rewards-in-sight.md), [59](../episodes/notes/059-kirby-air-ride.md), [200](../episodes/notes/200-presenting-scores.md)

### UI-21 · Make HUD elements react to play and carry character appeal
**Why:** Small fighters gain appeal from portraits facing the audience, moving backgrounds suggest burning spirit, and in 3D it's hard to track whom you've hit. **Do:** Add portraits with UV-scrolling backgrounds, meters that change per ability and break when destroyed, icons for opponents hit, and scores that count up point by point. **Watch out:** Liveliness that costs readability.
*Sakurai's example:* Smash's HUD faces; Kirby Air Ride's breaking meters; Meteos' alien head spinning faster with the Speeder ([254](../episodes/notes/254-huds.md)). · Episodes: [254](../episodes/notes/254-huds.md), [200](../episodes/notes/200-presenting-scores.md), [11](../episodes/notes/011-kirbys-adventure.md)

### UI-22 · Present scores and ranks so improvement feels meaningful to most players
**Why:** Raw positions tell nearly everyone they're losing; nobody is happy to hear they are in x-millionth place. A unit of measurement changes what a score means. **Do:** Pick a unit that fits the fantasy, show a rank that rises with improvement against the whole player base, and hide the world number one. **Watch out:** A plain score counter dropped in by default.
*Sakurai's example:* Global Smash Power and Elite Smash ([170](../episodes/notes/170-the-pros-and-cons-of-leaderboards.md)); Brain Age, Burnout's Crash Dollars and Tecmo's Game Deviation Value ([200](../episodes/notes/200-presenting-scores.md)). · Episodes: [200](../episodes/notes/200-presenting-scores.md), [170](../episodes/notes/170-the-pros-and-cons-of-leaderboards.md)

### UI-23 · Use text effects for emotion, and let translators restyle them
**Why:** In text-heavy games, changing letters and words conveys emotion, and he finds a single text size almost wasteful. Languages emphasize differently. **Do:** Add inline size, color, animation and font tags, stored in the localizable script so translators control them, plus speech-bubble styles and emotion symbols. **Watch out:** Hardcoded effects. Effects may also not suit very serious titles.
*Sakurai's example:* Japanese comics' kakimoji and manpu ([93](../episodes/notes/093-emphasizing-text.md)). · Episodes: [93](../episodes/notes/093-emphasizing-text.md)

## Common mistakes

- Unskippable logos and credits, skip locked until a scene has been watched once, or cutscenes that skip but can't pause ([180](../episodes/notes/180-let-them-skip-let-them-pause.md), [233](../episodes/notes/233-in-game-cutscenes.md)).
- Text that crawls at one fixed speed with no way to finish a box early ([211](../episodes/notes/211-advancing-text.md)).
- Leaving remapping to the console, or press-the-button-to-assign screens that capture the exit button ([197](../episodes/notes/197-button-settings.md)).
- Button prompts hardcoded to default buttons ([85](../episodes/notes/085-making-tutorials-feel-natural.md)).
- Buttons that do nothing, and presses with no clear feedback ([104](../episodes/notes/104-dont-be-unresponsive.md)).
- Help text that repeats the item's name, and jargon such as "config" ([229](../episodes/notes/229-adding-help-text-to-menus.md)).
- Laying out text for English only and approving it on a big dev monitor ([33](../episodes/notes/033-text-size.md)).
- Menus that only work if you can read them ([240](../episodes/notes/240-making-things-clear-even-without-words.md)).
- Stick-tap-tap menus and expert-only shortcut systems ([169](../episodes/notes/169-i-want-to-choose-fast.md)).
- A jumble of colors on one screen, or color as the only cue ([133](../episodes/notes/133-color-coding-your-game.md), [214](../episodes/notes/214-a-world-with-less-color.md)).
- Always-on markers and HUD panels that cover characters ([254](../episodes/notes/254-huds.md)).
- A mini-map so essential that nobody looks at the world ([119](../episodes/notes/119-maps-are-game-screens-too.md)).
- Plain "Now Loading...", or presentation timed to today's slow dev loads ([78](../episodes/notes/078-loading-screens.md)).
- Showing stats only as numbers on customization screens ([182](../episodes/notes/182-customization-lets-your-imagination-play.md)).
- Stripping comfort-related effects for everyone instead of offering toggles, or assists that switch on by accident ([129](../episodes/notes/129-motion-sickness-in-3d-games.md), [55](../episodes/notes/055-modern-quality-of-life-features.md)).

## Review questions

1. If a player wants the fun right now, how many presses and seconds stand in the way?
2. Does every button press, in every game state, get a clear answer?
3. Could someone who can't read the language still reach and start the main mode?
4. Can every player rebind every action, save it, and never lose a button?
5. Where are the player's eyes during play, and is the information there?
6. Is every word readable on the smallest real display, in every language?
7. Does this menu belong to this game's world while staying clear?
8. Can the player see what a choice will do, what they're working toward, and where they stand?

## Exercises

1. **The fresh-save sprint.** Wipe your save, start a stopwatch, and get to real gameplay as fast as possible using only the confirm button. Then try skipping every logo, movie and cutscene with one press, and pausing one cutscene. Fix every place that fails ([240](../episodes/notes/240-making-things-clear-even-without-words.md), [180](../episodes/notes/180-let-them-skip-let-them-pause.md)).
2. **Dead-button audit.** For each state (title, menus, gameplay, pause, cutscene, results), write a table of every controller button and what it does. Give every dead button a sensible reaction or a written reason for silence ([104](../episodes/notes/104-dont-be-unresponsive.md)).
3. **Help-text rewrite.** Export every menu item and its help line. Flag every line that reuses a word from its item's name or contains jargon, and rewrite them so a non-gamer understands each in one read ([229](../episodes/notes/229-adding-help-text-to-menus.md)).
4. **Unreadable build.** Swap in a language you can't read (or replace all strings with placeholder glyphs) and have a teammate navigate to three specific options. Note where they get lost and add pictures, colors or size cues ([240](../episodes/notes/240-making-things-clear-even-without-words.md), [133](../episodes/notes/133-color-coding-your-game.md), [147](../episodes/notes/147-make-important-elements-bigger.md)).
5. **Real-screen read-through.** Put your longest-language build on the smallest display you ship on, sit at normal distance, and read every screen aloud. Log any text you squint at, plus anything at the very edge of the image ([33](../episodes/notes/033-text-size.md), [252](../episodes/notes/252-crt-displays.md)).
6. **Key config teardown.** Build or review your remapping screen against the recipe: graphical controller, cursor assignment, shared actions, profiles, long-press confirm, defaults shown, no unbound actions. Then remap everything and check that every prompt in the game updates ([197](../episodes/notes/197-button-settings.md), [55](../episodes/notes/055-modern-quality-of-life-features.md), [85](../episodes/notes/085-making-tutorials-feel-natural.md)).
