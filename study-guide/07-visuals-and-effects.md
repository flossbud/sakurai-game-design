# 07 · Visuals, Characters and Effects

> Sakurai judges graphics first by whether the player can read the game, and only then by whether they're beautiful. Beautiful still matters, because "graphics multiply the fun of a game" and decide whether anyone picks it up [152](../episodes/notes/152-do-graphics-impact-fun.md). Most of his advice is concrete supervision: make collision terrain outshine the backdrop, keep characters visible inside their effects, mix dark into bright effects, give hit flashes internal contrast, build explosions in phases, check color-coded pieces through a color-blindness filter, make silhouettes read in solid black, and unify mixed art styles so the cast doesn't look like "a fight between live action and animation." Under all of it sits one habit: judge the final frame, at gameplay distance, on the screen players actually use.

## The big ideas

**Readability is the first job of every pixel.** In an action game the player must always know where the ground is, who is who, what just hit and what's coming. So collision surfaces must stand out from scenery [30](../episodes/notes/030-emphasize-objects-with-collision.md), fighters must stay visible inside their own effects [35](../episodes/notes/035-let-your-characters-shine.md), effects must read on any background [16](../episodes/notes/016-make-it-pop.md), and gameplay colors must survive color blindness and grayscale [214](../episodes/notes/214-a-world-with-less-color.md). Readability doesn't win outright, though. He wants stages that sell the game too, and gives gameplay "the slight edge" rather than total victory [30](../episodes/notes/030-emphasize-objects-with-collision.md), [152](../episodes/notes/152-do-graphics-impact-fun.md). Most of his stage reviews are this balance worked out case by case [165](../episodes/notes/165-odds-and-ends-of-supervising.md), [253](../episodes/notes/253-odds-and-ends-of-supervising-sora-edition.md).

**Contrast does the work, not brightness.** The same idea keeps coming back across effects, flashes and environments: pure additive brightness flattens images and vanishes on light backgrounds. Effects need crisp shapes and dark elements [16](../episodes/notes/016-make-it-pop.md), [131](../episodes/notes/131-hit-marks.md). Explosion flashes need black mixed in [56](../episodes/notes/056-flash-blast-and-smoke.md). Hit flashes need shadows, not just added white [223](../episodes/notes/223-flashing-effects.md). In stages, the same material has strong contrast in the foreground and weaker contrast in the background [253](../episodes/notes/253-odds-and-ends-of-supervising-sora-edition.md). And an asset isn't done until it shows the light it reflects [10](../episodes/notes/010-draw-the-light-not-the-asset.md). When something doesn't read, look for missing contrast before adding anything bigger or brighter.

**Characters are established by exaggeration, and the whole team carries them.** Game characters lose much of the information real people give off, so "too much is just right" [91](../episodes/notes/091-exaggerate-to-make-up-for-information-loss.md), [28](../episodes/notes/028-too-much-is-just-right.md). A silhouette should still read as exaggerated when filled in solid black [243](../episodes/notes/243-establishing-characters-through-their-design.md). Manga conventions beat realism when they express more [232](../episodes/notes/232-manga-like-visuals.md). A character's identity, which should fit in a word or two [206](../episodes/notes/206-using-parameters-to-establish-characters.md), also has to show in its effects: Marth's trail emphasizes the tip where his sword is strongest, Ike's heavier blade gets a frenzied trail [179](../episodes/notes/179-blade-trails.md). Everyone working on a character must understand its core essence [243](../episodes/notes/243-establishing-characters-through-their-design.md).

**Judge the whole, in place, on the real screen.** Scale, light, style unity and readability can only be judged in the final composited frame at gameplay distance [215](../episodes/notes/215-final-output.md), [174](../episodes/notes/174-making-things-look-miniature.md), [224](../episodes/notes/224-all-to-destroy-one-wall.md). That frame should be checked on a reference monitor the team has matched [184](../episodes/notes/184-dealing-with-color-disparity.md) and sized for the screen players will actually use [42](../episodes/notes/042-a-small-window-into-the-world.md), [125](../episodes/notes/125-kid-icarus-uprising.md). He directs this by showing, not telling: five-minute retouches, mouse sketches, posed figures [90](../episodes/notes/090-supervising-art-through-retouches.md), [165](../episodes/notes/165-odds-and-ends-of-supervising.md), [253](../episodes/notes/253-odds-and-ends-of-supervising-sora-edition.md).

## Principles

### VIS-01 · Make everything players must read stand out from decoration

**Why:** In an action game players must always know where solid ground is, and in a perspective 3D view collision is hard to recognize. Elaborate backdrops steal attention so easily that this comes up for every new Smash stage. His usual note is "your backdrop's outshining your terrain."

**Do:** Highlight every collision surface in a debug view (his example marks them in red) and check each reads at a glance in the normal view. Push non-colliding scenery back with aerial perspective and lower contrast, then repair the overall look. Check that drop-through platforms, projectiles, costumes and fighters in shadow or vignetting stay visible, and that the floor doesn't drown out projectiles. Desaturate screenshots to see what really stands out. Give dangerous enemy attacks a visual tell players can react to.

**Watch out:** Fixing readability only by fogging the background until the stage looks bad. He wants both, with gameplay given "the slight edge."

> "Make the objects with collision stand out— and give the backdrop less emphasis." ([30](../episodes/notes/030-emphasize-objects-with-collision.md), ≈01:18)

*Sakurai's example:* Mishima Dojo fighters lost in shadow, shown with a desaturated image; the P8 Enderman costume pitched as a game of "find the Enderman"; Hollow Bastion's floor toned down so Greninja's Water Shuriken stays visible; Punch-Out!!'s yellow eyes as a dodge cue. · Episodes: [30](../episodes/notes/030-emphasize-objects-with-collision.md), [165](../episodes/notes/165-odds-and-ends-of-supervising.md), [253](../episodes/notes/253-odds-and-ends-of-supervising-sora-edition.md), [214](../episodes/notes/214-a-world-with-less-color.md), [157](../episodes/notes/157-special-crossover-3.md), [152](../episodes/notes/152-do-graphics-impact-fun.md)

### VIS-02 · Keep characters visible inside their own effects

**Why:** Effects are needed for impact, but characters easily get lost in a sea of them. In 2D you can just draw characters above effects. In 3D, forcing that draw priority creates depth disparity, and lowering the effect's priority instead can leave you seeing nothing but terrain [131](../episodes/notes/131-hit-marks.md). The fix has to live in the effect's shape.

**Do:** Review every large effect with the character inside it, from gameplay camera angles. Leave gaps around the body. Make the parts of a surrounding effect that sit between camera and character lower than the parts behind it. Add outlines to character models. Thin out layered effects and background atmosphere that bury fighters, and send scattering pieces (like hit stars) far enough that they don't vanish behind bodies.

**Watch out:** Forced draw priority in 3D, and "fixing" occlusion by lowering effect priority.

*Sakurai's example:* Terry stays clear inside Power Dunk's crown; Falcon Punch leaves Captain Falcon partly visible; the inner peaks of Chrom's Up Special are a different height from front and back; Sora's down-throw effects were thinned because they hid the fighters. · Episodes: [35](../episodes/notes/035-let-your-characters-shine.md), [131](../episodes/notes/131-hit-marks.md), [253](../episodes/notes/253-odds-and-ends-of-supervising-sora-edition.md), [165](../episodes/notes/165-odds-and-ends-of-supervising.md)

### VIS-03 · Never let color alone carry gameplay meaning

**Why:** In Meteos, the only game of his where color coding really mattered, he treated color-blind playability as essential before calling the game complete. Certain hues look alike to color-blind players, and the same trap hits power-ups and interactive objects in any genre.

**Do:** Run every color-coded element through a color-blindness simulation filter (Photoshop now includes one), including protanopia. Where hues merge, separate them by *brightness*, not just hue, while keeping them bright enough to stand out from the background. Check whole screens in black and white too: backgrounds shouldn't be more prominent than the foreground. Keep each screen to one dominant color theme rather than a mishmash [133](../episodes/notes/133-color-coding-your-game.md).

**Watch out:** Darkening pieces until they vanish into the background. Checking colors one at a time when elements look different in each level.

> "If your game is primarily built around color, solely relying on that will make it so that players with color blindness won't be able to play." ([214](../episodes/notes/214-a-world-with-less-color.md), ≈00:24)

*Sakurai's example:* Meteos blocks, which change look on every planet, checked with a protanopia filter and redesigned with significant brightness differences. · Episodes: [214](../episodes/notes/214-a-world-with-less-color.md), [133](../episodes/notes/133-color-coding-your-game.md), [223](../episodes/notes/223-flashing-effects.md)

### VIS-04 · Give every character a silhouette that reads even in solid black

**Why:** Of all the ways he knows to make an imaginary character look cool, the silhouette is the one he stresses most. Characters are often small on screen and seen mostly from one angle, so an ambiguous outline, or one too close to another character's, fails. Exaggerated forms are also what make a character *theirs*.

**Do:** Fill each character and key pose in solid black and compare the cast side by side. Exaggerate the signature features (hair, ribbons, gloves, head size, limb thickness). Check hit and damage poses from the game's main viewpoint with all four limbs readable, mirrored for both screen sides [62](../episodes/notes/062-attack-poses.md), [142](../episodes/notes/142-damage-animations.md). Break symmetry and twist torsos [106](../episodes/notes/106-posing-suggestions.md). Brighten extremities so movement reads: he colored a Mii outfit's socks white for exactly this reason [158](../episodes/notes/158-the-majority-shouldnt-always-rule.md).

**Watch out:** Realistic proportions that blur together at game size, and symmetric front-facing poses.

> "Their form should be exaggerated, even when they're just solid black." ([243](../episodes/notes/243-establishing-characters-through-their-design.md), ≈01:38)

*Sakurai's example:* Nakoruru's emphasized hair, ribbon, gloves and legs; Sonic's large head and skinny but exaggerated limbs; Devil Kazuya's too-rounded wing silhouette sharpened; Sora's back jump reworked for a more energetic silhouette. · Episodes: [243](../episodes/notes/243-establishing-characters-through-their-design.md), [62](../episodes/notes/062-attack-poses.md), [142](../episodes/notes/142-damage-animations.md), [106](../episodes/notes/106-posing-suggestions.md), [165](../episodes/notes/165-odds-and-ends-of-supervising.md), [253](../episodes/notes/253-odds-and-ends-of-supervising-sora-edition.md), [158](../episodes/notes/158-the-majority-shouldnt-always-rule.md)

### VIS-05 · Give effects contrast with crisp shapes and dark accents, not glow alone

**Why:** Additive blending adds an effect's colors to the background, pushing it toward white. Layering it gives decent results from simple visuals and nothing more: the effect lacks impact and contrast, often looks too bright, and disappears on light backgrounds. Keeping the whole game dark to show effects off still falls short.

**Do:** Build every effect around solid polygons or simple images as the focal point, with additive glow only as support. Mix dark elements into bright effects (black accents, a dark backdrop behind a hit mark). Test each effect on your brightest and darkest backgrounds. When processing is scarce, mix white and black pixels so an effect shows on any color [244](../episodes/notes/244-the-limitations-of-particle-effects.md). In any design, pair opposites, like straight lines in a curvy shape.

**Watch out:** Effects made only of bright elements; darkening the game instead of fixing the effect.

> "Don't let additive blending do all the work for you!" ([16](../episodes/notes/016-make-it-pop.md), ≈00:48)

*Sakurai's example:* Smash Ultimate builds all its effects around crisp shapes for a clean, anime-style look; Sora's Blizzaga got dark elements because it vanished on bright stages. · Episodes: [16](../episodes/notes/016-make-it-pop.md), [131](../episodes/notes/131-hit-marks.md), [253](../episodes/notes/253-odds-and-ends-of-supervising-sora-edition.md), [56](../episodes/notes/056-flash-blast-and-smoke.md), [244](../episodes/notes/244-the-limitations-of-particle-effects.md)

### VIS-06 · Fit each hit mark to its hit: size, place, layers and duration

**Why:** Hit marks exist for clear feedback and fun, so they should be flashy in proportion to the impact. They must appear where the attack and target hitboxes actually met, or they look wrong, and a pulled-back camera with large hitboxes makes that tricky. Quick repeated hits need short marks. During hitstop the characters freeze but the mark can keep animating, which is free time for detail.

**Do:** Work through his seven-point checklist (see the table below): flashiness matches impact, visual balance, correct position, a variety of elements, characters not eclipsed, fitting duration, contrasting brightness. Layer a main flash, a shockwave behind the target, an upward spray, and material extras like sparks for machines. Keep durations short, even for big hits. Adapt borrowed effects to your own camera distance and hit frequency: Smash couldn't reproduce Tekken 7's lavish effects for fast repeated hits [244](../episodes/notes/244-the-limitations-of-particle-effects.md).

**Watch out:** One generic spark for every hit, marks floating where nothing connected, and cartoony marks pasted unchanged onto realistic characters.

*Sakurai's example:* Smash Ultimate's hit marks in slow motion; the effect during hitstop that shows the flight path the hit player can slightly adjust, an effect that grew out of the game's own rules; Sora's hit stars sent flying farther so they don't disappear behind Donkey Kong. · Episodes: [131](../episodes/notes/131-hit-marks.md), [244](../episodes/notes/244-the-limitations-of-particle-effects.md), [253](../episodes/notes/253-odds-and-ends-of-supervising-sora-edition.md), [179](../episodes/notes/179-blade-trails.md)

### VIS-07 · Flash for impact and status, with internal contrast and fixed color meanings

**Why:** A full-screen flash adds impact the way screen shake does, because it covers so much area. A character flash emphasizes a status change. He calls both crucial. But adding white or one color evenly lowers contrast, because the same amount lands on dark and light areas and they average out.

**Do:** Mix shadows or dark elements into hit flashes so the character keeps internal contrast. Give each status one flash color and keep it consistent across the cast. Use rim lighting for states like invulnerability. Scale flash strength with camera distance so a close-up doesn't flash most of the screen. Keep flashes to a single brief moment, keep full-screen flashing mild, and check one-frame flashes on real LCDs. Freeze and flash the screen for the biggest moments, such as a boss defeat [3](../episodes/notes/003-stop-for-big-moments.md).

**Watch out:** A pure white flash, and full-strength flashing when the camera is zoomed in, which makes too much of the screen flash.

> "If you simply brighten a character when they get hit, they'll vanish into the background like this." ([223](../episodes/notes/223-flashing-effects.md), ≈01:55)

*Sakurai's example:* Smash Ultimate's shadowed hit flash, built from a mockup he drew; white flashes for invulnerability while getting up; a yellow flash for the window after a throw when the character can't be grabbed again; rim lighting for certain damage types. · Episodes: [223](../episodes/notes/223-flashing-effects.md), [3](../episodes/notes/003-stop-for-big-moments.md), [56](../episodes/notes/056-flash-blast-and-smoke.md)

### VIS-08 · Treat effects as essential and study them frame by frame

**Why:** Effects pack a lot of information into an instant, so in play they fly by unnoticed. Remove them and the game looks plain. The same game can feel quite different with different effects.

**Do:** Build debug toggles for slow motion, hiding the background, switching each display element on and off, and making model parts translucent [217](../episodes/notes/217-transparency-features.md). Record your effects and reference games' effects and step through them frame by frame. A/B a key scene with effects off. Give swings, punches and kicks trails or blur [179](../episodes/notes/179-blade-trails.md). Add celebratory flourishes aimed at the player who scored, like the confetti in Ultimate's KO explosion [95](../episodes/notes/095-praise-the-player.md).

**Watch out:** Skimping on effects because they're on screen for only an instant.

*Sakurai's example:* His long montage of Smash Ultimate effects in slow motion with backgrounds disabled. · Episodes: [79](../episodes/notes/079-visual-effects-in-slow-motion.md), [179](../episodes/notes/179-blade-trails.md), [95](../episodes/notes/095-praise-the-player.md), [217](../episodes/notes/217-transparency-features.md)

### VIS-09 · Let each character's look express its essence and its mechanics

**Why:** Characters must be well established, which means everyone assigned to one understands its core essence and draws it out further. It isn't something one person can do alone. A character should be describable in a word or two [206](../episodes/notes/206-using-parameters-to-establish-characters.md), and the visuals are where that identity, and the rules behind it, become visible.

**Do:** Write a short core-essence statement per character and share it with everyone who touches that character. Make effects carry what each move is about: an emphasized trail edge where a sword's tip is strongest, a frenzied trail for a heavy blade, no fade-out for a blade that emits light [179](../episodes/notes/179-blade-trails.md). Show state changes on the body: status flashes [223](../episodes/notes/223-flashing-effects.md), or Kirby drawn slightly plumper and recolored when he holds a copy ability [115](../episodes/notes/115-the-basics-of-pixel-art.md). When a hidden rule matters to play, consider an effect that shows it [131](../episodes/notes/131-hit-marks.md). Look for functional additions that also add character appeal, as copy hats did for Kirby [20](../episodes/notes/020-kirby-super-star.md).

**Watch out:** A design that matches the reference but misses who the character is. Sakurai gives no separate rule for *drawing a weakness*; his examples express strengths and identity, and the balance chapter covers keeping peaks and valleys distinct.

*Sakurai's example:* Marth's tip-emphasized trail, Ike's frenzied trail, Travis's beam katana trail with no fade; Ultimate's hitstop direction effect. · Episodes: [243](../episodes/notes/243-establishing-characters-through-their-design.md), [206](../episodes/notes/206-using-parameters-to-establish-characters.md), [179](../episodes/notes/179-blade-trails.md), [131](../episodes/notes/131-hit-marks.md), [115](../episodes/notes/115-the-basics-of-pixel-art.md), [20](../episodes/notes/020-kirby-super-star.md), [223](../episodes/notes/223-flashing-effects.md)

### VIS-10 · Prefer expressive exaggeration over literal realism

**Why:** Polygon characters lose much of the information real people give off, and no part of them truly exists, so too much is just right [91](../episodes/notes/091-exaggerate-to-make-up-for-information-loss.md). Manga and anime conventions often express a character better than realism, and some are even cheaper to produce. And you can't fix what you never notice, so question the conventions you inherited.

**Do:** Push expressions and signature features past where they feel done, judged at in-game size [166](../episodes/notes/166-facial-animations.md). Try manga conventions: a mouth on the side of the face in profile, animated by swapping cels instead of moving a jaw. For squash and stretch through narrow gaps, try shape animation. Review models from the angles players actually see and question inherited anatomy.

**Watch out:** Rejecting a technique only because it isn't realistic. (See the tension table: this is about your own expression, not about overriding a source fans love.)

*Sakurai's example:* Mega Man Legends' profile mouths; Sonic's side-of-face mouth; Kirby's and Jigglypuff's mouths remodeled for Ultimate because the realistic profile was less expressive; Wolf's damage face pushed to suit a wolf's jaw. · Episodes: [232](../episodes/notes/232-manga-like-visuals.md), [91](../episodes/notes/091-exaggerate-to-make-up-for-information-loss.md), [166](../episodes/notes/166-facial-animations.md), [243](../episodes/notes/243-establishing-characters-through-their-design.md), [28](../episodes/notes/028-too-much-is-just-right.md)

### VIS-11 · Size characters and targets for the screen they're played on

**Why:** A screen takes up only about 30 degrees of the player's vision, so a target of realistic size can be a speck. A big screen with a tiny target lacks impact as entertainment, and on a handheld it forces players to stare at a small area. Large targets also forgive beginners' slightly off-target attacks.

**Do:** Decide how much of the screen play and targets should fill before fixing the camera and field of view. Scale enemies up when they look tiny: Kid Icarus: Uprising's standard enemies are four or five times Pit's size. In beginner games, make bosses big enough that slightly off-target attacks still hit [100](../episodes/notes/100-development-secrets-of-the-original-kirby.md). When characters are small, carry their appeal elsewhere too, as Smash does with HUD portraits [254](../episodes/notes/254-huds.md), and push their exaggeration [91](../episodes/notes/091-exaggerate-to-make-up-for-information-loss.md).

**Watch out:** A camera chosen for realism (like a racing cockpit view) that leaves little screen for play.

*Sakurai's example:* Kid Icarus: Uprising's oversized enemies; Whispy Woods built big for Kirby's newcomers. · Episodes: [42](../episodes/notes/042-a-small-window-into-the-world.md), [125](../episodes/notes/125-kid-icarus-uprising.md), [100](../episodes/notes/100-development-secrets-of-the-original-kirby.md), [254](../episodes/notes/254-huds.md), [91](../episodes/notes/091-exaggerate-to-make-up-for-information-loss.md)

### VIS-12 · Unify mixed art styles around one visual concept

**Why:** Every artist on a team has to produce the same style, whatever their specialty. Characters imported as-is from different sources produce an incoherent style. Unification gets more expensive as visual fidelity rises, but it's a necessary step.

**Do:** Before production, have your strongest artist make a few image boards (world, scenery, characters, items) and build every model and the lighting from them [130](../episodes/notes/130-unifying-visual-style.md). When styles mix, meet in the middle with his five adjustments: slightly paler primaries that respond to light, texture added to flat cartoon characters and eased on realistic ones, small proportion tweaks, the same refinement pass for everyone, and believability (how would this work if the character really existed?). Apply the same check to animation, effects and audio. Vet any new technique before adopting it mid-project.

**Watch out:** Each artist in a personal style; official designs dropped in unchanged; a trendy technique adopted mid-project that doesn't suit how the game is built.

> "Don't make it look like a fight between live action and animation." ([138](../episodes/notes/138-a-fight-between-live-action-and-animation.md), ≈03:43)

*Sakurai's example:* Mario's denim overalls; Bayonetta's adjusted proportions, which look different side by side but not in the game; Donkey Kong's and Yoshi's eyelids closing instead of comic-book pupils; Meta Knight's mask slit reshaped to sit beside realistic fighters [100](../episodes/notes/100-development-secrets-of-the-original-kirby.md); Kirby Super Star's mid-project switch to CG backgrounds, which didn't suit Kirby's pieced-together terrain [20](../episodes/notes/020-kirby-super-star.md). · Episodes: [130](../episodes/notes/130-unifying-visual-style.md), [138](../episodes/notes/138-a-fight-between-live-action-and-animation.md), [100](../episodes/notes/100-development-secrets-of-the-original-kirby.md), [20](../episodes/notes/020-kirby-super-star.md)

### VIS-13 · Draw the light an asset reflects, not just the asset

**Why:** Everything we see is light reflected into our eyes, so a red thing doesn't simply look red. A tree with a brown trunk and green leaves isn't finished, even if lighting is someone else's job. Cheap techniques also leave room for more objects on screen than heavy per-object shaders.

**Do:** Make lighting part of an asset's definition of done. For repeated assets, shine colored light down for contrast within the group, brighten spots that would catch more light, vary hue slightly across copies, and add atmosphere that does more than flat fog. Enrich flat backgrounds with contrast and hue-shifted light [90](../episodes/notes/090-supervising-art-through-retouches.md). Use post-processing to blend hues across the scene [215](../episodes/notes/215-final-output.md).

**Watch out:** Copy-pasted assets with identical colors, and bright primaries that look the same under any light.

### VIS-14 · Judge visuals on the final composited frame at gameplay distance

**Why:** Drawing each material's texture well on its own won't make the image cohesive [174](../episodes/notes/174-making-things-look-miniature.md). On modern hardware, what matters is how everything comes together: the post-processed frame, blended hues and reflections. Many flaws (even spacing, square outlines, mismatched proportions, wrong scale) show only in the whole frame or from the gameplay camera.

**Do:** Approve art in the game with post-processing on, not in a model viewer. Review scale and lighting on full screenshots. Look at assets both up close and from gameplay distance [224](../episodes/notes/224-all-to-destroy-one-wall.md). Line up mixed characters in game, not against their source art [138](../episodes/notes/138-a-fight-between-live-action-and-animation.md). Step back from details regularly. On high-end projects, give one person ownership of post-processing and final output.

**Watch out:** Polishing props one at a time and getting lost in minor details.

*Sakurai's example:* The same stage in Melee, where every object has its own distinct color, and in Ultimate, with blended hues and reflections off the water; the Mishima Dojo wall that only looked square and evenly spaced from far away. · Episodes: [215](../episodes/notes/215-final-output.md), [174](../episodes/notes/174-making-things-look-miniature.md), [224](../episodes/notes/224-all-to-destroy-one-wall.md), [138](../episodes/notes/138-a-fight-between-live-action-and-animation.md), [205](../episodes/notes/205-making-the-imaginary-feel-real.md), [63](../episodes/notes/063-get-the-sense-of-scale-right.md)

### VIS-15 · Spend detail where players actually look

**Why:** Console hardware isn't as powerful as people think, so you have to separate major elements from minor ones. Players mostly watch the center of the screen and the playable surface. How much an environment means depends on how closely the gameplay makes them scrutinize it [152](../episodes/notes/152-do-graphics-impact-fun.md).

**Do:** Rank assets by how much they're seen: the area in front of the camera and the stage surface first, off-screen foundations last. Budget each model by its on-screen size, but raise it for models also shown up close elsewhere. Put environment detail where cautious play makes players look, and where the camera reveals more background [165](../episodes/notes/165-odds-and-ends-of-supervising.md). Build LOD only when the gain is worth the labor. Build stage art to the worst-case load.

**Watch out:** Detail nobody will ever see. Small playful touches are fine if they meet deadlines and don't cost performance.

*Sakurai's example:* Car tires in the backdrop of Inkling's stage that actually turned, toned down because no player would ever see them; Ultimate's stages built to hold 60 fps with eight fighters on Switch [99](../episodes/notes/099-an-up-close-look-at-smash-bros-stages.md). · Episodes: [44](../episodes/notes/044-distinguishing-between-major-and-minor-elements.md), [152](../episodes/notes/152-do-graphics-impact-fun.md), [165](../episodes/notes/165-odds-and-ends-of-supervising.md), [99](../episodes/notes/099-an-up-close-look-at-smash-bros-stages.md)

### VIS-16 · Raise graphics and gameplay together, because graphics multiply fun

**Why:** Game environments are mirages you could walk straight through until gameplay gives them presence: when you die often, you scrutinize every corner. In the other direction, on storefronts with an endless lineup, a banner decides whether anyone looks at your game. Calling visuals irrelevant to gameplay doesn't help if nobody buys the game [30](../episodes/notes/030-emphasize-objects-with-collision.md).

**Do:** Build environment art alongside the mechanics and match its density to how closely play makes players look. Treat key art and store banners as critical. When readability and beauty conflict, keep both and give gameplay the slight edge.

**Watch out:** Writing graphics off as irrelevant to fun.

*Sakurai's example:* Dark Souls and Sekiro, where frequent deaths give every object presence, versus the same graphics at Sonic's speed; Steam banners. · Episodes: [152](../episodes/notes/152-do-graphics-impact-fun.md), [30](../episodes/notes/030-emphasize-objects-with-collision.md), [99](../episodes/notes/099-an-up-close-look-at-smash-bros-stages.md)

### VIS-17 · Direct art by showing, and approve whatever meets the goal

**Why:** Palette, contrast, mood and shape are hard to put into words, and showing communicates more clearly than telling, especially over email. His retouches take about five minutes, ten at most, which beats explaining. But a retouch is direction, not final art, and his goal isn't to make every asset match his personal ideal [224](../episodes/notes/224-all-to-destroy-one-wall.md).

**Do:** Send a quick paintover, retouched screenshot, mouse sketch, posed figure or density reference next to the original, with one line on what and why. Make notes concrete: which axis, which spacing, which repeated pattern. Flag tiny flaws as soon as you find them [253](../episodes/notes/253-odds-and-ends-of-supervising-sora-edition.md). State each asset's goal in one sentence (for the dojo wall: it must look like boards that were really broken) and approve anything that meets it, even if it differs from your image. If several rounds don't converge, ship it. ### VIS-18 · Stage explosions as flash, blast and smoke, then let them finish

**Why:** A single burst of fire is tempting and flat. The instant flash tells players something intense happened; the crisp blast is the key part; smoke and a natural conclusion show where it happened. Clearing the smoke instantly would be better for pure gameplay, but a shorter explosion lacks impact.

**Do:** Follow the four-phase recipe in the table below. Keep the hit active for only part of the blast, not the whole visual. Make ground variants whose blast spreads upward and whose shockwave hugs the floor. Add a screen flash or shake only where it suits that explosion. Consider small gusts of wind around explosions [205](../episodes/notes/205-making-the-imaginary-feel-real.md).

**Watch out:** A pure white flash, which disappears on white backgrounds.

*Sakurai's example:* Star Force's five-frame explosion, flash to smoke to gone, which he calls very well made for 1984 [156](../episodes/notes/156-special-crossover-2.md); Smash's standard explosion; Young Link's bombs; extra smoke so Sora's Final Smash explosion reads [253](../episodes/notes/253-odds-and-ends-of-supervising-sora-edition.md). · Episodes: [56](../episodes/notes/056-flash-blast-and-smoke.md), [156](../episodes/notes/156-special-crossover-2.md), [253](../episodes/notes/253-odds-and-ends-of-supervising-sora-edition.md), [205](../episodes/notes/205-making-the-imaginary-feel-real.md)

### VIS-19 · Sell scale through materials, saturation, atmosphere and density, not shape

**Why:** A huge object and its plastic model share shape and color. CG objects naturally look like scale models (more saturated, glossier, less affected by ambient light, flatter in material), and big things looking too small is the common failure. Shallow depth of field and high saturation are exactly how photos are made to look miniature [174](../episodes/notes/174-making-things-look-miniature.md). Effects fail the same way: a flame as big as a character often looks like a candle [201](../episodes/notes/201-making-effects-the-right-size.md).

**Do:** Fix materials before shape: enlarge texture loops and painted detail, lower saturation on realistic assets, shrink the gloss area and its strength, match normal maps to real size, add dirt and mixed hues to big mechanical or monotone surfaces. Make saturation highest on the closest objects and lower it with distance. Avoid depth-of-field blur outside very specific spots. Give large effects more loops or smaller particles. Use placement and surroundings to establish size [205](../episodes/notes/205-making-the-imaginary-feel-real.md).

**Watch out:** Making these adjustments before the game's atmosphere is established; he says they come after.

*Sakurai's example:* Off-color tile spots and grass hue variation that betray a small object; Pyra's sword flames given a density reference. · Episodes: [63](../episodes/notes/063-get-the-sense-of-scale-right.md), [174](../episodes/notes/174-making-things-look-miniature.md), [201](../episodes/notes/201-making-effects-the-right-size.md), [205](../episodes/notes/205-making-the-imaginary-feel-real.md)

### VIS-20 · Honor the source's feeling instead of copying it literally

**Why:** A costume can match its reference and still feel wrong, because what fans love is a feeling. He wants the team to honor the source's feeling and do what works best, which sometimes means moving closer to the original and sometimes mixing in newer details. In licensed work, faithfulness and fans' love come first [188](../episodes/notes/188-faithfulness-comes-first-in-licensed-games.md).

**Do:** Get original production material [168](../episodes/notes/168-game-watch-designs.md) and put source screenshots beside your version. Compare palette, saturation, the relative brightness of costume parts, eye shape and signature poses [253](../episodes/notes/253-odds-and-ends-of-supervising-sora-edition.md). Combine the classic design with selected newer details [243](../episodes/notes/243-establishing-characters-through-their-design.md). Stay close where the source shows a lot and invent where it shows little [188](../episodes/notes/188-faithfulness-comes-first-in-licensed-games.md). Don't invent facial range the source lacks; carry emotion through movement [166](../episodes/notes/166-facial-animations.md).

**Watch out:** Copying one source version exactly and missing the character.

> "I don't want us to just copy the originals, but honor the feeling and do what works best." ([165](../episodes/notes/165-odds-and-ends-of-supervising.md), ≈04:51)

*Sakurai's example:* The Mii Nakoruru costume, revised from a literal KOF XIV match; Kazuya's knockdown pose and Final Smash moved toward his original game; CG shots pushed toward FFVII: Advent Children, starting by lowering saturation; Steve, whose only facial animation is a blink. · Episodes: [165](../episodes/notes/165-odds-and-ends-of-supervising.md), [243](../episodes/notes/243-establishing-characters-through-their-design.md), [253](../episodes/notes/253-odds-and-ends-of-supervising-sora-edition.md), [166](../episodes/notes/166-facial-animations.md), [168](../episodes/notes/168-game-watch-designs.md), [188](../episodes/notes/188-faithfulness-comes-first-in-licensed-games.md), [138](../episodes/notes/138-a-fight-between-live-action-and-animation.md)

### VIS-21 · Protect players' eyes: cap glare, flashing, blur and depth of field

**Why:** Bright glowing elements are hard on the eyes [90](../episodes/notes/090-supervising-art-through-retouches.md), and modern regulations keep flashing mild [223](../episodes/notes/223-flashing-effects.md). Visuals that are too fuzzy make it hard to tell what's going on [192](../episodes/notes/192-motion-blur.md), and focusing on something blurry can be physically uncomfortable [174](../episodes/notes/174-making-things-look-miniature.md). Inertia-heavy camera motion makes some players sick [129](../episodes/notes/129-motion-sickness-in-3d-games.md).

**Do:** Raise contrast on lava and fire but cap the brightness of the glowing areas. Keep full-screen flashes brief and mild, and weaker when the camera is close. Use pre-drawn blur textures on spinning parts, keep artificial blur subtle, and offer a blur-strength option. Cut depth of field and focus pulls except where truly needed. Offer toggles for camera embellishments such as footstep shake.

**Watch out:** Depth of field as a cheap atmosphere boost.

*Sakurai's example:* World of Light's magma, kept from getting too bright; a focus shift removed from Sora's fighter reveal; the spinning blur he regrets leaving off Kirby Air Ride's Wheel; the Spirit Train stage's built-in wheel blur. · Episodes: [90](../episodes/notes/090-supervising-art-through-retouches.md), [223](../episodes/notes/223-flashing-effects.md), [192](../episodes/notes/192-motion-blur.md), [174](../episodes/notes/174-making-things-look-miniature.md), [129](../episodes/notes/129-motion-sickness-in-3d-games.md)

### VIS-22 · Check color on matched screens and on the displays players use

**Why:** Each monitor shows color differently, even the same model from the same maker, and room lighting changes perception. For artists and supervisors, any variance ruins a color check. Calibration devices only go so far because games end up on ordinary TVs. Older games were made for CRTs, whose glow and dithering read differently today [252](../episodes/notes/252-crt-displays.md).

**Do:** Put one reference monitor where the whole team can see it and calibrate everyone's monitor by eye against it. Retire monitors that crush shadows or flatten contrast. Light art rooms neutrally. Check flashes on real LCD screens [223](../episodes/notes/223-flashing-effects.md). When porting or imitating retro art, judge it as it looked on a CRT.

**Watch out:** Assuming everyone sees the same colors.

*Sakurai's example:* A Mewtwo image whose right-hand shadow disappeared on one monitor; a company whose moody yellow office lighting made all its art come out bluish. · Episodes: [184](../episodes/notes/184-dealing-with-color-disparity.md), [252](../episodes/notes/252-crt-displays.md), [223](../episodes/notes/223-flashing-effects.md)

### VIS-23 · Make imaginary objects feel real through weight, light, use, placement and air

**Why:** To a creator, game worlds are mirages, decoration without mass. Players feel them as real because visuals, audio, programming and parameters work together; he calls all of it "expression." A regular table and a miniature have the same shape, and only presentation and arrangement tell them apart. Imitating reality also packs in information: floors look hard, windups look powerful [178](../episodes/notes/178-what-is-play.md).

**Do:** For each prop, decide texture, how it catches light, weight, use and placement, not just shape. Add environmental wind so cloth and foliage flutter. Make broken objects irregular and keep debris out of the space characters move through [224](../episodes/notes/224-all-to-destroy-one-wall.md). Do a "top-down" plausibility sweep of every stage [165](../episodes/notes/165-odds-and-ends-of-supervising.md).

**Watch out:** Forgetting invisible forces like airflow.

*Sakurai's example:* Smash's environmental wind and the slight gusts around explosions; the Cloud Sea windmills that once turned against the wind; the Mishima Dojo wall. · Episodes: [205](../episodes/notes/205-making-the-imaginary-feel-real.md), [165](../episodes/notes/165-odds-and-ends-of-supervising.md), [224](../episodes/notes/224-all-to-destroy-one-wall.md), [178](../episodes/notes/178-what-is-play.md)

### VIS-24 · Give each weapon its own trail, and fix the swing until it reads

**Why:** Without blade trails, attacks lose their oomph, so getting trails right has a big effect. But a trail that just traces a fast swing looks angular and can be nearly invisible from the side, so animations usually need correcting too.

**Do:** Build trails as a polygon ribbon behind the blade, like a folding fan, with a translucent fading tail. For swings that finish in a single frame, show one flat trail image and rely on hitstop to hold it on screen. Check each swing from the gameplay camera and change the animation rather than using a plain horizontal slash. Design each trail for its weapon, and extend a similar blur to punches and kicks.

**Watch out:** One generic trail for every sword.

*Sakurai's example:* Lucina's standard blue trail, Marth's emphasized edge, Ike's frenzied trail, Pit's two-color trail, Pyra's flames, Travis's unfading beam katana; Pyra's flame-and-Photon-Saber trail, which he names as probably the hardest one [165](../episodes/notes/165-odds-and-ends-of-supervising.md). · Episodes: [179](../episodes/notes/179-blade-trails.md), [165](../episodes/notes/165-odds-and-ends-of-supervising.md), [192](../episodes/notes/192-motion-blur.md)

### VIS-25 · Get the most out of every particle

**Why:** Particles always cost something, however powerful the hardware, so his teams set rules: as many as possible at the lowest processing cost. Billboards are cheap and ideal for intangible effects such as flashes of light [153](../episodes/notes/153-billboards.md), but look wrong from some angles. Effects are always moving, so making them look quick and fierce matters more than fine detail [201](../episodes/notes/201-making-effects-the-right-size.md).

**Do:** Short lifetimes with higher speed; several particles per billboard; somewhat large billboards; staggered spawn timing and offset positions. Orbit the camera around billboarded objects and switch to models where they look strange. Use Y-axis billboards carefully. When processing is scarce, hand-place effect frames or play random picks from preset patterns. Cheap techniques mean more objects on screen [10](../episodes/notes/010-draw-the-light-not-the-asset.md).

**Watch out:** Copying lavish effects from a game with different hardware, camera distance or hit counts.

*Sakurai's example:* Steel Diver's bubbles packed several to a panel; Kirby's Dream Land's enemy-defeat stars, hand-placed in each frame with no particle code; Smash food items, which are billboards and look odd when the camera rotates. · Episodes: [244](../episodes/notes/244-the-limitations-of-particle-effects.md), [153](../episodes/notes/153-billboards.md), [201](../episodes/notes/201-making-effects-the-right-size.md), [10](../episodes/notes/010-draw-the-light-not-the-asset.md)

### VIS-26 · On small, low-color canvases, give every pixel and color a job

**Why:** With few colors and 16x16 sprites, every choice shows. Odd-sized shapes have a true center line, so a 15x15 circle looks rounder than a 16x16 one. A third color is best spent on softening edges or showing a reflection.

**Do:** Draw round or symmetric sprites at odd dimensions. See how far an outline and one highlight can carry a material before adding colors. Choose edge-softening colors against the real background. Mix light and dark pixels so small effects show on any background [244](../episodes/notes/244-the-limitations-of-particle-effects.md). Design emblems that survive mirroring [181](../episodes/notes/181-flipped-animation.md), and save memory with shared or flipped parts [100](../episodes/notes/100-development-secrets-of-the-original-kirby.md). Remember that retro dithering was designed for CRTs [252](../episodes/notes/252-crt-displays.md).

**Watch out:** Mesh (dither) shading on something meant to read as round; it flattens the shape.

*Sakurai's example:* Kirby's 16x16 Game Boy sprite, which he drew himself; outline plus highlight reads as a bubble, solid black plus highlight as a cannonball; the Maxim Tomato's "M"; Waddle Dee and Waddle Doo sharing a back half. · Episodes: [115](../episodes/notes/115-the-basics-of-pixel-art.md), [244](../episodes/notes/244-the-limitations-of-particle-effects.md), [181](../episodes/notes/181-flipped-animation.md), [100](../episodes/notes/100-development-secrets-of-the-original-kirby.md), [183](../episodes/notes/183-long-ago-numbers-were-used-to-draw-pixel-art.md), [252](../episodes/notes/252-crt-displays.md)

## Common mistakes

- Backdrops that outshine the terrain, and "fixing" it by fogging the whole background until the stage looks bad [30](../episodes/notes/030-emphasize-objects-with-collision.md).
- Effects built only from bright additive layers, which wash out on light stages; or darkening the entire game so they show [16](../episodes/notes/016-make-it-pop.md), [253](../episodes/notes/253-odds-and-ends-of-supervising-sora-edition.md).
- Flashing a hit character pure white, so it vanishes into a light background [223](../episodes/notes/223-flashing-effects.md).
- Forcing characters to draw on top of effects in 3D, or lowering effect priority until terrain hides them [35](../episodes/notes/035-let-your-characters-shine.md), [131](../episodes/notes/131-hit-marks.md).
- Telling gameplay elements apart by hue alone [214](../episodes/notes/214-a-world-with-less-color.md).
- Silhouettes that are ambiguous or resemble another character's [243](../episodes/notes/243-establishing-characters-through-their-design.md).
- A single burst of fire for an explosion, or cutting the explosion short [56](../episodes/notes/056-flash-blast-and-smoke.md).
- Calling an asset done at base colors, or approving assets one at a time in a model viewer [10](../episodes/notes/010-draw-the-light-not-the-asset.md), [215](../episodes/notes/215-final-output.md), [174](../episodes/notes/174-making-things-look-miniature.md).
- Oversaturated, glossy large objects that look like toys; big flames that read as candles [63](../episodes/notes/063-get-the-sense-of-scale-right.md), [201](../episodes/notes/201-making-effects-the-right-size.md).
- Shallow depth of field and heavy motion blur as cheap atmosphere [174](../episodes/notes/174-making-things-look-miniature.md), [192](../episodes/notes/192-motion-blur.md).
- Importing characters from different sources unchanged, so the cast looks like "a fight between live action and animation" [138](../episodes/notes/138-a-fight-between-live-action-and-animation.md).
- Copying a reference literally and missing the character [243](../episodes/notes/243-establishing-characters-through-their-design.md), [165](../episodes/notes/165-odds-and-ends-of-supervising.md).
- Spending effort on details nobody will ever see [44](../episodes/notes/044-distinguishing-between-major-and-minor-elements.md).
- Judging color on unmatched monitors under tinted light [184](../episodes/notes/184-dealing-with-color-disparity.md).
- Long text-only art feedback, or a director trying to retouch everything [90](../episodes/notes/090-supervising-art-through-retouches.md), [165](../episodes/notes/165-odds-and-ends-of-supervising.md).

## Review questions

1. Freeze the busiest frame: can a newcomer tell where the ground is, who is who, what just hit and what's dangerous?
2. Filled in solid black, is every character exaggerated, readable and distinct from the rest of the cast?
3. Through a color-blindness filter and in grayscale, does everything that matters still separate?
4. On the brightest and darkest backgrounds, does every effect and flash still pop?
5. Is each effect as big, as long and as well placed as the event it marks, with the damage window shorter than the visual?
6. In the final frame at gameplay distance, does the scene have believable light and scale?
7. Is the detail where players actually look, and are targets big enough on the real screen?
8. Do all characters and assets look like one game, and do adapted ones feel like their originals?
9. Does each character's look and effects tell the player what it is and what its moves do best?
10. After an hour of play, is anything glaring, strobing, blurry or nauseating, and can players turn it down?

## Exercises

1. **The red-and-gray pass (1 hour).** Add a debug view that paints all collision surfaces one flat color. Take five screenshots of your busiest scenes, desaturate them, and circle every place where background is brighter or more contrasty than terrain or characters. Fix the worst three by lowering background contrast and saturation, then check the color version still looks good [30](../episodes/notes/030-emphasize-objects-with-collision.md), [214](../episodes/notes/214-a-world-with-less-color.md).
2. **Effects on white (2 hours).** Make a test level with a pure white background and one near-black background. Fire every effect and hit flash in your game in both. For each one that washes out, add a crisp focal shape and a dark element; for character flashes, replace additive white with a shadowed flash [16](../episodes/notes/016-make-it-pop.md), [223](../episodes/notes/223-flashing-effects.md), [131](../episodes/notes/131-hit-marks.md).
3. **Silhouette lineup (1 hour).** Render every character, and each one's hit pose, as a solid black shape in a single row. Ask a teammate who hasn't seen them to match silhouettes to names. Exaggerate whatever they confuse [243](../episodes/notes/243-establishing-characters-through-their-design.md), [62](../episodes/notes/062-attack-poses.md).
4. **Color-blind filter on your pickups (1 hour).** Screenshot every color-coded item, team marker and puzzle piece. Run them through a protanopia (and other) simulation filter. Where any pair merges, change brightness until they separate under the filter, without losing them against the background [214](../episodes/notes/214-a-world-with-less-color.md).
5. **Rebuild one explosion in phases (half a day).** Take your most common explosion and rebuild it as flash, blast, smoke and conclusion. Limit the hitbox to part of the blast, then make a ground variant. A/B it against the old one in slow motion with the background off [56](../episodes/notes/056-flash-blast-and-smoke.md), [79](../episodes/notes/079-visual-effects-in-slow-motion.md).
6. **Five-minute retouch round (1 hour).** Pick three assets or screenshots you'd give notes on. For each, spend no more than ten minutes on a paintover that shows the change, and write a one-sentence goal it must meet. Send them instead of a written list, and compare how quickly the fixes come back [90](../episodes/notes/090-supervising-art-through-retouches.md), [224](../episodes/notes/224-all-to-destroy-one-wall.md).
