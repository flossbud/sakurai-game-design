# 02 · Game Essence: Risk, Reward and Push-and-Pull

> "Game essence" is Sakurai's name for the thing that makes play gamelike: **push and pull**, or **risk and reward**. The player takes a risk to earn a reward, and the moment of greatest danger is also the moment they can win something. He treats it as the most powerful tool he has for finding the fun in a game, whatever the genre, and he explains Space Invaders, Super Mario Bros., Tetris, Gradius, Street Fighter II, RPG dungeons and strategy games all in the same terms. The method is to find where risk and reward meet in each verb, put them close together in the right amounts, pay the biggest risks the most, and wrap it all in a rhythm of stress and release. Then remember two limits he insists on. Game essence is only one source of fun, and the more of it you add, the fewer people your game reaches (chapter 03 picks that thread up).

## The big ideas

**Fun lives where danger and opportunity overlap.** Sakurai's founding example is Space Invaders [6](../episodes/notes/006-risk-and-reward.md). Your cannon and the invaders only shoot straight up and down, so when you're far from an invader, nobody can hit anybody. He calls that a *no risk, no reward* state. Slide under an invader and both things change at once: its beams can now reach you, and your laser can now reach it. "Take risks to get rewards. This push and pull is the very core of game essence." Strategy is the same thing seen from the player's side: how you manage those risks to get the reward. Because the invaders move sideways, their real attack range is diagonal, so chasing one from behind puts you in danger for nothing, while intercepting it from the front carries the same risk with the target in your sights. Even the cannon's width is a balance setting, because it decides how much risk you take. In Super Mario Bros. the jump that dodges a Koopa Troopa is also the only way to stomp it, and the shell you kick for points can bounce back and hurt you. His summary: "Risk and reward must be in close proximity, and in the right amounts." [6](../episodes/notes/006-risk-and-reward.md) [148](../episodes/notes/148-more-game-essence-means-less-broad-appeal.md)

**Every verb in every genre has both sides.** He applies the lens everywhere [193](../episodes/notes/193-game-essence-in-action-games.md). Jumping from closer to the ledge clears a bigger gap but risks falling. Dashing is faster but harder to control. Melee hits only work when you're close enough to be hit. Guns let you attack from a distance but cost ammo, and the longer the range, the less ammo you get. A parry pays off only if you wait until the attack is about to land. In shooters, Darius caps your shots on screen so you fire more when you move in close, and Gradius puts power-ups next to terrain that kills you [134](../episodes/notes/134-game-essence-in-shooting-games.md). In Tetris the stack is the risk, and the big multi-line clear demands a tall, dangerous stack [86](../episodes/notes/086-game-essence-in-falling-block-puzzle-games.md). RPG dungeons ask whether you press on for better loot or get out before you lose what you've found [15](../episodes/notes/015-game-essence-in-role-playing-games.md). Strategy games start from a fair mirror and reward the player who changes their setup to win [230](../episodes/notes/230-game-essence-in-strategy-games.md). A fighter with one punch has no push and pull, and it appears once a kick recovers longer than the punch and can be punished on a miss [82](../episodes/notes/082-do-competitive-games-have-to-be-complex.md). Even flight only feels good once falling, the force that opposes it, is part of the game [65](../episodes/notes/065-falling-sells-the-feeling-of-flight.md).

**Under the moment-to-moment play sits a rhythm of squeeze and release.** "Stress on its own isn't fun, but if you really squeeze a player, then whoosh! set them free, it can bring great satisfaction." [9](../episodes/notes/009-squeeze-and-release.md) Most games run a four-step loop: an obstacle causes **stress**, removing it gives **relief**, the reward makes you stronger (**growth**), and the next obstacle becomes beatable (**progress**) [71](../episodes/notes/071-do-we-really-need-enemies.md). Relief, growth and progress are the fun, but none of them can happen without the initial stress, so you can remove enemies but not the stress they provide. Rewards close the loop when they hand players new ways to handle the next squeeze, ideally with a choice in how [9](../episodes/notes/009-squeeze-and-release.md) [75](../episodes/notes/075-consider-rewards-first.md). Losing is part of the rhythm too. Competitive players feel relief when they get defeated, quick restarts sweep disappointment away, and the ideal reaction to a loss is to vow you'll get them next time [97](../episodes/notes/097-dying-comes-as-a-relief.md).

**Treat game essence as a way of thinking, and know its limits.** Because Sakurai understood *why* falling-block puzzles are fun, he could pitch Meteos despite being bad at the genre, and its core idea took five minutes [74](../episodes/notes/074-meteos.md) [86](../episodes/notes/086-game-essence-in-falling-block-puzzle-games.md). His method is deconstruct, analyze, rebuild: find the fun part, explain it in risk-and-reward terms, then build a new mechanic that delivers the same payoff. That's how Kirby Air Ride's one-button charge drift came about [57](../episodes/notes/057-deconstruct-analyze-and-rebuild.md). He's equally clear about the limits. "Game essence isn't the only thing that makes a game fun" [26](../episodes/notes/026-fun-beyond-game-essence.md). Control feel, story, fandom, simulation, building and rhythm can all carry a game, and pushing essence too hard can cost a game its flair or make it feel like a chore [134](../episodes/notes/134-game-essence-in-shooting-games.md). More essence also means less broad appeal [6](../episodes/notes/006-risk-and-reward.md) [148](../episodes/notes/148-more-game-essence-means-less-broad-appeal.md). Every principle below is a dial, and where you set it depends on who the game is for.

## Principles

### ESS-01 · Put risk and reward side by side, in the right amounts
**Why:** Fun comes from entering danger to score. In Space Invaders you can only hit an invader from where it can hit you, and in Super Mario Bros. the highest-risk moment beside a Koopa Troopa is the only moment you can stomp it. **Do:** For each core interaction, map how risk and reward change with distance, position and timing, and make sure the scoring spot is an exposed spot. Attach fresh risk to rewards (a kicked shell that can bounce back), and tie effectiveness to closeness. **Watch out:** Safe positions from which the player can still score, and options that let them clear the screen from safety.
*Sakurai's example:* Darius limits how many shots can be on screen, so hanging back uses them up while closing in lets you fire far more, and close-range rapid fire takes bosses down fast. The Street Fighter II shoryuken is strongest when the opponent is closest. · Episodes: [6](../episodes/notes/006-risk-and-reward.md), [134](../episodes/notes/134-game-essence-in-shooting-games.md), [148](../episodes/notes/148-more-game-essence-means-less-broad-appeal.md), [155](../episodes/notes/155-special-crossover-1.md), [157](../episodes/notes/157-special-crossover-3.md), [159](../episodes/notes/159-the-shoryuken-command.md), [193](../episodes/notes/193-game-essence-in-action-games.md)

### ESS-02 · Give every action a downside that opposes its benefit
**Why:** Danger and opportunity are two sides of the same coin. No motion feels complete until something opposes it, just as player attacks and enemy attacks come as a set. An option that erases risk kills the push and pull, however cool it looks. **Do:** List every verb with its risk and its reward, and add the missing downside wherever there isn't one: edge jumps that risk falling, dashes that cost control or stamina, ranged weapons with less ammo, strong attacks with longer recovery, flight that comes with falling or a requirement to fly. If you keep a risk-erasing option, counterweight it. **Watch out:** Strong attacks with little vulnerability afterward, which make a game feel unrefined, and flight with nothing to crash into.
*Sakurai's example:* A three-way laser in Space Invaders might look cool, but it would only be good design if enemies also shot diagonally or ammo were limited. In his one-punch fighter thought experiment, push and pull only appears when the kick recovers longer than the punch. · Episodes: [6](../episodes/notes/006-risk-and-reward.md), [65](../episodes/notes/065-falling-sells-the-feeling-of-flight.md), [77](../episodes/notes/077-follow-throughs-make-the-impact.md), [82](../episodes/notes/082-do-competitive-games-have-to-be-complex.md), [134](../episodes/notes/134-game-essence-in-shooting-games.md), [193](../episodes/notes/193-game-essence-in-action-games.md)

### ESS-03 · Make the riskiest play pay the most
**Why:** Balanced correctly, risk and reward scale together, and a more intense risk brings a more intense joy when you overcome it. A slow attack that leaves you open is a more commendable hit than a quick jab, so it deserves more. **Do:** Rank your options by the risk they demand (startup time, exposure, accumulated danger, how much is staked) and make payoffs climb with them. "A move with high risk should deliver a big reward." **Watch out:** Underpaying the riskiest variant (he thinks Street Fighter II's heavy shoryuken could have been stronger still), or letting the safe play be the top-scoring play.
*Sakurai's example:* Smash Bros. smash attacks vs. neutral attacks; Tetris multi-line clears that need a tall stack; arcade scoring that pays more for chaining fleeing ghosts or killing escorts first; higher Fiend's Cauldron Intensity paying more hearts. · Episodes: [41](../episodes/notes/041-the-fiends-cauldron.md), [86](../episodes/notes/086-game-essence-in-falling-block-puzzle-games.md), [148](../episodes/notes/148-more-game-essence-means-less-broad-appeal.md), [155](../episodes/notes/155-special-crossover-1.md), [159](../episodes/notes/159-the-shoryuken-command.md), [193](../episodes/notes/193-game-essence-in-action-games.md), [238](../episodes/notes/238-how-good-were-the-players-actions.md)

### ESS-04 · Squeeze players with stress, then release it into growth
**Why:** Games stress you out in order to give relief, and catharsis peaks when players straddle the line between victory and defeat. The loop stress → relief → growth → progress is what most games run on, and only the first step is unpleasant. **Do:** For every section, name the stress, the moment of release, the growth reward and the progress it unlocks, and fix whichever is missing. Use squeeze and release moment to moment too: charge during a risky maneuver, release for the payoff. **Watch out:** Weeding out stress to make a game more comfortable, and releases that lead nowhere.
*Sakurai's example:* Kirby Air Ride's drift: braking into the turn charges power, and releasing at the exit gives a burst of speed. · Episodes: [9](../episodes/notes/009-squeeze-and-release.md), [59](../episodes/notes/059-kirby-air-ride.md), [71](../episodes/notes/071-do-we-really-need-enemies.md), [213](../episodes/notes/213-better-than-not-being-able-to-beat-the-game.md)

### ESS-05 · Understand why something is fun before you copy or rebuild it
**Why:** Copying a fun game won't feel original, and it can lose the very reason the game worked. Knowing the genre's essence let him design in genres he's bad at, and copies of hard-game systems fail without the pull that made the originals work. **Do:** Deconstruct (pick the fun part), analyze (state its risk and reward in one sentence), rebuild (find a new verb with the same payoff). Ask whether your idea changes the concept or only renames the default action. When porting or adapting, carry the proven core over unchanged. **Watch out:** Novelty that's only a new word, like blocks that are "vaporized" instead of erased, and borrowing a boss structure or a permadeath system wholesale.
*Sakurai's example:* "You shouldn't start your design with the idea that blocks have to disappear, but instead consider why it's fun to make those blocks disappear." So Meteos blocks blast off instead. Tetris has game essence and its ancestor Pentomino doesn't. Mushiking's handheld version kept the arcade game's rock-paper-scissors core. · Episodes: [9](../episodes/notes/009-squeeze-and-release.md), [57](../episodes/notes/057-deconstruct-analyze-and-rebuild.md), [74](../episodes/notes/074-meteos.md), [86](../episodes/notes/086-game-essence-in-falling-block-puzzle-games.md), [92](../episodes/notes/092-sodatete-kouchuu-ouja-mushiking.md), [96](../episodes/notes/096-the-family-computer-and-the-nes.md), [128](../episodes/notes/128-is-your-game-appealing-enough-to-start-over.md), [134](../episodes/notes/134-game-essence-in-shooting-games.md), [193](../episodes/notes/193-game-essence-in-action-games.md), [219](../episodes/notes/219-mandatory-strategies-and-strategic-balance.md)

### ESS-06 · Make every rule earn its place by creating a new decision
**Why:** Depth comes from mechanics that create decisions, not from mechanics in general. Long-running genres pile up rules that add nothing, combos that settle a fight after the first hit leave the opponent with nothing to do, and a button you only hold has little essence. **Do:** Build up from a minimal core one mechanic at a time, write down the decision each addition creates, and cut those that create none. At each project's start, list your inherited genre "givens" and re-justify each one. **Watch out:** Mash-only or hold-only mechanics (mashing tires players and favors natural tappers) and rules kept out of habit.
*Sakurai's example:* The one-punch fighter that gains depth through walking, a punishable kick, jumping, guarding and throwing. Smash replaced long combos with accumulated damage so each hit gets a different reaction. Kirby's Egg Catcher: hold to catch eggs, release to dodge bombs. · Episodes: [27](../episodes/notes/027-super-smash-bros.md), [51](../episodes/notes/051-the-potential-of-one-button.md), [82](../episodes/notes/082-do-competitive-games-have-to-be-complex.md), [148](../episodes/notes/148-more-game-essence-means-less-broad-appeal.md)

### ESS-07 · Let reads and clever plays, not raw stats, decide contests
**Why:** Sakurai places game essence mainly in agon, competition, and in his view clever plays, not physical advantage, should decide the winner. Push and pull is strongest when players can read, bluff and counter each other. **Do:** Give both sides counters and information (whiff punishes, guard, throw and attack beating each other, specialties that can be read) and signals that can be faked. Tune so position, timing and reads can overcome bigger numbers. **Watch out:** Outcomes decided by stats, size, or whoever lands the first hit.
*Sakurai's example:* In Mushiking each beetle specializes in one rock-paper-scissors shape, so do you play your strong "paper" or try a surprise? In multiplayer Samurai Kirby, players fake the start signal with their mouths. · Episodes: [27](../episodes/notes/027-super-smash-bros.md), [51](../episodes/notes/051-the-potential-of-one-button.md), [82](../episodes/notes/082-do-competitive-games-have-to-be-complex.md), [92](../episodes/notes/092-sodatete-kouchuu-ouja-mushiking.md), [178](../episodes/notes/178-what-is-play.md)

### ESS-08 · Offer choices with real upsides and real downsides
**Why:** Options with trade-offs broaden game essence in any genre, distinct sides create more strategy than mirror matches, and players feel better about a path they chose. "What downsides will you accept, and what upsides will you gain?" **Do:** Give every weapon, unit, character and route a clear upside and a clear downside on different axes (coverage vs. shot count, cost vs. power, a safe long route vs. a risky shortcut). Build counter cycles. Show the effect, such as attack range, instead of only numbers. **Watch out:** Options that are strictly better than others, or interchangeable.
*Sakurai's example:* Gradius's double cannon covers upward angles but fires only two shots and is weaker straight ahead than the laser. In his strategy-game cycle, infantry lose to tanks, tanks to helicopters, helicopters to anti-air and anti-air to infantry. · Episodes: [15](../episodes/notes/015-game-essence-in-role-playing-games.md), [64](../episodes/notes/064-good-errands-and-bad-errands.md), [134](../episodes/notes/134-game-essence-in-shooting-games.md), [182](../episodes/notes/182-customization-lets-your-imagination-play.md), [218](../episodes/notes/218-average-and-mediocre-are-the-same-thing.md), [230](../episodes/notes/230-game-essence-in-strategy-games.md)

### ESS-09 · Place the best rewards beside the biggest hazards
**Why:** Level layout is where risk and reward become physical. When touching terrain costs a life, a power-up beside it is a gamble. The thrill of flight comes from barely scraping past things, and a game with only sky and nothing to crash into has no tension. **Do:** Put power-ups, valuable enemies and shortcuts next to deadly geometry. Put ground, water and structures near flight routes, and trigger movement powers from the environment. Scale dungeon rewards with depth, and make defeat cost the spoils so retreating is a real choice. **Watch out:** Rewards sitting in safe spots; open sky or space with nothing to crash into.
*Sakurai's example:* Flying under the long bridge in the first Ace Combat; Gradius power-ups beside terrain; Kirby Air Ride machines launching off cliffs and ramps. · Episodes: [15](../episodes/notes/015-game-essence-in-role-playing-games.md), [64](../episodes/notes/064-good-errands-and-bad-errands.md), [65](../episodes/notes/065-falling-sells-the-feeling-of-flight.md), [134](../episodes/notes/134-game-essence-in-shooting-games.md)

### ESS-10 · Let players choose how much risk to take
**Why:** Strategy is how players minimize losses and maximize gains, sometimes by taking a bigger loss for an even bigger gain. Optional risk lets each player set their own stakes, and greed is a strong motivator. **Do:** Offer optional risk with matching rewards: press on or retreat, escalating scores for chaining risky actions, a wager on difficulty, safe versus dangerous routes, partial cover. Map each system for how players can cut losses, raise gains, and trade one for the other. **Watch out:** One fixed level of risk for everyone, and a safe play that also scores best.
*Sakurai's example:* Classic arcade greed: Galaxian flagships worth more after their escorts, crushing enemies with rocks instead of the safe pump attack, and the Fiend's Cauldron, which lets you bet hearts on difficulty. · Episodes: [6](../episodes/notes/006-risk-and-reward.md), [15](../episodes/notes/015-game-essence-in-role-playing-games.md), [41](../episodes/notes/041-the-fiends-cauldron.md), [48](../episodes/notes/048-let-players-cheat-the-system.md), [64](../episodes/notes/064-good-errands-and-bad-errands.md), [155](../episodes/notes/155-special-crossover-1.md)

### ESS-11 · Let skilled players turn last-moment danger into big payoffs
**Why:** The longer you wait, the more you risk, and the more a well-timed answer should pay. Being able to cling to life at the brink invites players to take even greater risks. **Do:** Make parries trigger only at the moment of impact and pay heavily, give dodges a brief window where other actions are harder, add last-chance inputs that let players survive at the edge, and make reversals strongest at point-blank range. **Watch out:** Defensive tools that work just as well used early and safely.
*Sakurai's example:* Later Tetris ports let you rotate and slide pieces after they land. Kid Icarus: Uprising's flick dodge rewards drawing attacks in close before evading. Space Invaders' "Wall of Death" makes the cannon untouchable at the very moment one mistake ends the game. · Episodes: [6](../episodes/notes/006-risk-and-reward.md), [86](../episodes/notes/086-game-essence-in-falling-block-puzzle-games.md), [125](../episodes/notes/125-kid-icarus-uprising.md), [154](../episodes/notes/154-flick-input.md), [159](../episodes/notes/159-the-shoryuken-command.md), [193](../episodes/notes/193-game-essence-in-action-games.md)

### ESS-12 · Give enemies a purpose beyond being defeated
**Why:** Kirby started from the idea that it would be fun to *use* enemies, because of risk and reward and the dual purpose enemies serve. Copy abilities then gave every enemy a meaning and made each encounter something to look forward to. Sizing up an enemy and exploiting its weakness is strategy and game essence at once. **Do:** Give players a simple way to use on-screen enemies, give each type something distinct to take, give enemies cues that hint at their weaknesses, and reward exploiting a weakness with more than damage, such as a combo setup. **Watch out:** Overly complex weakness systems in a casual game.
*Sakurai's example:* Kirby's inhale-and-spit (a tongue grab was the first idea) versus Mega Man, where boss weapons are a reward for winning rather than a use of enemies. Persona's weakness combos. · Episodes: [4](../episodes/notes/004-kirbys-dream-land.md), [11](../episodes/notes/011-kirbys-adventure.md), [15](../episodes/notes/015-game-essence-in-role-playing-games.md)

### ESS-13 · Make bosses demand a strategy, and decide how authored it is
**Why:** Bosses are the peaks of the climb, so they should force a plan, not just hit harder. Authored solutions show crafted thought but feel like tutorials. Free-form fights give freedom but can feel flavorless, with less push and pull. Both are valid, but you have to choose. **Do:** For each boss decide up front between one authored solution, an ideal strategy found by trial and error, and many viable approaches, then reward success clearly. Let aggressive positioning finish the fight faster. **Watch out:** Beefed-up regular enemies, and copying another game's boss structure instead of deriving one from your own systems.
*Sakurai's example:* Nintendo-style bosses where you hit the eye at the right moment and slash the stunned foe, vs. Castlevania's any-weapon freedom; Darius bosses that fall fast to close-range fire. · Episodes: [105](../episodes/notes/105-mountain-climbing-is-more-than-just-climbing.md), [134](../episodes/notes/134-game-essence-in-shooting-games.md), [157](../episodes/notes/157-special-crossover-3.md), [219](../episodes/notes/219-mandatory-strategies-and-strategic-balance.md)

### ESS-14 · Plan rewards early and make players earn them
**Why:** In modern games, progress alone doesn't motivate. Rewards are where player investment comes from, and you can't reward players if you have nothing to give them. A collection that's unlocked from the start has no joy. **Do:** As soon as the bones of the game work, list what every enemy, mission and activity grants, when it arrives, and where it gets used. Gate characters and collectibles behind play, and give casual players a grid of goals to work toward. **Watch out:** Leaving rewards until late, unlocking everything up front, and relying on movies as rewards when they'll be online anyway.
*Sakurai's example:* Smash Ultimate's Spirits would be joyless if unlocked from the start. ### ESS-15 · Turn rewards into growth the player chooses how to use
**Why:** Release is only step one. The best rewards are new ways to relieve the next stress, getting stronger is the most effective reward, and giving players a choice boosts game essence. **Do:** Pair every currency with desirable things to buy, including at least one expensive goal worth saving for. Show current progress against what the next reward needs. Make crafted items feel like profit. **Watch out:** Infinite resources with nothing to spend them on, floods of money and minor gear, and loops where collecting is the only activity.
*Sakurai's example:* Faxanadu's few, expensive, powerful shop items made players scrounge and plan; Ys shows the EXP you have and the EXP the next level needs. · Episodes: [9](../episodes/notes/009-squeeze-and-release.md), [23](../episodes/notes/023-keeping-rewards-in-sight.md), [71](../episodes/notes/071-do-we-really-need-enemies.md), [75](../episodes/notes/075-consider-rewards-first.md), [151](../episodes/notes/151-planning-your-games-rewards.md), [203](../episodes/notes/203-making-work-games-fun.md)

### ESS-16 · Make losing part of the fun
**Why:** Competitive players often feel relief when they're defeated, and anxiety after a kill that doesn't settle the match. When the fun of both defeating and being defeated clicks, there's no better entertainment. **Do:** Keep respawns and rematches short, make the moment of defeat entertaining in itself, and write down how each death motivates the next try. **Watch out:** Assuming competitive fun is only the joy of dominating, and long waits that give disappointment time to set in.
*Sakurai's example:* An overseas study of competitive FPS players, and his ideal reaction to a loss: "I'll get you next time!" · Episodes: [97](../episodes/notes/097-dying-comes-as-a-relief.md), [117](../episodes/notes/117-make-retries-quick.md)

### ESS-17 · Name your game's main source of fun; it may not be game essence
**Why:** Control feel, story, fandom, simulation, building and rhythm can each carry a game, and Caillois adds chance, imitation and vertigo to competition. Work games hook players with almost no push and pull, and licensed games are judged on faithfulness first. **Do:** Name the main source of fun and spend your polish there (fine-tune control feel if control is the fun). Then add risk and reward where it fits: fall deaths and dangerous gathering trips in building games, strict timing in rhythm games, a work-earn-upgrade loop in job games. **Watch out:** Assuming a compelling story alone makes a good game, and loosening judgment until nothing is at stake.
*Sakurai's example:* In beatmania a hair late counts as a miss; a looser Good/Bad system would carry less risk and might be boring. ### ESS-18 · Pay off risk tangibly, after the risky act, without snowballing
**Why:** When he rebuilt drifting, a score bonus gave no tangible benefit and permanent power-ups let skilled drifters run away with the race. In a fighting game, character growth would make the "real" game start only at max level. **Do:** Grant a useful, temporary benefit once the risky action is completed, not while it's happening. Keep permanent, stacking advantages out of competitive play, and reduce rewards when players overpower content. **Watch out:** Score-only rewards for risky play, and boosts that make the leader untouchable.
*Sakurai's example:* Kirby Air Ride's speed burst at the curve's exit; World of Light gives fewer rewards when your Spirits overpower a fight. · Episodes: [57](../episodes/notes/057-deconstruct-analyze-and-rebuild.md), [59](../episodes/notes/059-kirby-air-ride.md), [151](../episodes/notes/151-planning-your-games-rewards.md), [160](../episodes/notes/160-super-smash-bros-ultimate.md)

### ESS-19 · Raise the stakes as play goes on
**Why:** Rising pressure keeps tension climbing, and when it rises because of what the player did, strategy emerges. **Do:** Increase speed or pressure over a session, and tie enemy speed or aggression to how many threats remain, so the order in which players clear threats becomes a choice. **Watch out:** Pressure that stays flat from start to finish.
*Sakurai's example:* Tetris pieces fall faster over time. In Space Invaders the fewer invaders remain, the faster they move, so clear one column at a time from the ends. · Episodes: [86](../episodes/notes/086-game-essence-in-falling-block-puzzle-games.md), [155](../episodes/notes/155-special-crossover-1.md)

### ESS-20 · Leave room for players to beat the system within the rules
**Why:** Getting gains without losses is fun and memorable, and just following the path laid out isn't. Some of the best moments were never planned: he suspects the Wall of Death is a bug. **Do:** When testers find a balance-breaking trick, ask whether it stays inside the rules and is fun to find before patching it. Consider a deliberate, rare, lucrative enemy with high HP or a tendency to flee. **Watch out:** Patching out every exploit by reflex, and letting players rewrite game parameters, which is outside the bounds he means.
*Sakurai's example:* Dragon Quest's metal slime farming; Dark Souls enemies lured off ledges. · Episodes: [6](../episodes/notes/006-risk-and-reward.md), [48](../episodes/notes/048-let-players-cheat-the-system.md), [238](../episodes/notes/238-how-good-were-the-players-actions.md)

### ESS-21 · Give every contest a stake that people care about
**Why:** A contest where only one side can win is far more exciting when you have someone to root for, and ranked results need an incentive to aim higher. **Do:** Let spectators pick a winner before a match (a tiny wager is enough, because investment is the point), show standing that moves with performance, keep both racers' progress visible, and keep real stakes when playing in front of an audience. **Watch out:** Spectator modes with no stake, and ranked play with nothing to climb.
*Sakurai's example:* Smash's spectator wagers; Sakurai and Arino Kacho starting the same retro game at the same moment; Sakurai playing Kid Icarus: Uprising live at Intensity 9.0, where a Game Over would have stopped the concert. · Episodes: [120](../episodes/notes/120-the-fun-in-picking-sides.md), [170](../episodes/notes/170-the-pros-and-cons-of-leaderboards.md), [227](../episodes/notes/227-gameplay-as-part-of-a-live-performance.md), [241](../episodes/notes/241-turning-anything-into-a-competitive-game.md)

### ESS-22 · Praise good play explicitly and give scores a meaning
**Why:** Players expect items and progress from any game, so those don't tell players they did well. A score expressed in a meaningful unit changes what the whole game means. **Do:** Add crowd cheers for big launches, a gasp for near-misses, allies who praise feats, generous labeled bonuses, and celebrations aimed at the player who scored. Present scores in a unit that fits the fantasy. **Watch out:** Treating drops and progress as all the praise players need, and a bare score counter added by default.
*Sakurai's example:* Smash's crowd and KO confetti; Forza's bonuses even for wrecking; Brain Age, Burnout's Crash Dollars, Global Smash Power. · Episodes: [59](../episodes/notes/059-kirby-air-ride.md), [95](../episodes/notes/095-praise-the-player.md), [200](../episodes/notes/200-presenting-scores.md)

### ESS-23 · Vary each session so players adapt instead of converging
**Why:** Shaving tenths of a second has game essence, but it funnels everyone into one method. Randomness and variety make each run different and force players to handle a range of situations. **Do:** Add a mode built for variety, vary probabilities instead of fixing results where repetition goes stale, and where draws are random let players choose among two or three. **Watch out:** Designing only around perfection and time trials.
*Sakurai's example:* Kirby Air Ride's City Trial, whose rules later became Smash Run; Smash's random items. · Episodes: [59](../episodes/notes/059-kirby-air-ride.md), [120](../episodes/notes/120-the-fun-in-picking-sides.md), [128](../episodes/notes/128-is-your-game-appealing-enough-to-start-over.md), [135](../episodes/notes/135-super-smash-bros-for-3ds-wii-u.md), [178](../episodes/notes/178-what-is-play.md), [189](../episodes/notes/189-randomness-spices-games-up.md)

### ESS-24 · Side with what's fun as a game over realism
**Why:** Realism leaves little room for creativity. Real drifting is slower than grip driving, yet making drifting fun makes the game fun, and fighting games that forget they're nothing like real fighting drift from their essence. **Do:** When realism and fun conflict, choose fun. Use realism for readable information (floors that look hard, hits that sound painful), not for rules. **Watch out:** Rules copied from reality just because they're accurate.
*Sakurai's example:* Kirby Air Ride drifting; Smash's exaggerated fighting. · Episodes: [57](../episodes/notes/057-deconstruct-analyze-and-rebuild.md), [89](../episodes/notes/089-audio-as-fiction-and-non-fiction.md), [178](../episodes/notes/178-what-is-play.md)

## When the advice pulls in two directions

- **More essence vs. broader appeal.** Several episodes push for sharper risk and reward [6](../episodes/notes/006-risk-and-reward.md) [159](../episodes/notes/159-the-shoryuken-command.md) [193](../episodes/notes/193-game-essence-in-action-games.md), but Sakurai also says more essence narrows appeal, that you should sometimes hold back from adding it [148](../episodes/notes/148-more-game-essence-means-less-broad-appeal.md), and that pushing it too hard can make a game a chore [134](../episodes/notes/134-game-essence-in-shooting-games.md). The tiebreaker is the audience and how long the game has to last. A game meant for hundreds of competitive matches needs real depth [82](../episodes/notes/082-do-competitive-games-have-to-be-complex.md), while a beginner's game like Kirby's Dream Land deliberately lowers risk and accepts a lower thrill [148](../episodes/notes/148-more-game-essence-means-less-broad-appeal.md). Chapter 03 covers that dial.
- **Game essence vs. other sources of fun.** This chapter treats essence as the core, but he also says some of the stickiest games have almost none [203](../episodes/notes/203-making-work-games-fun.md), and that licensed games should put faithfulness ahead of push and pull [188](../episodes/notes/188-faithfulness-comes-first-in-licensed-games.md). Use essence as a lens, not a mandate. First name what your game's fun actually rests on [26](../episodes/notes/026-fun-beyond-game-essence.md), then add risk where it strengthens that.
- **Authored vs. free-form strategy.** Crafted boss solutions show care but feel like tutorials. Total freedom feels flavorless and has less push and pull [219](../episodes/notes/219-mandatory-strategies-and-strategic-balance.md). He says neither is more correct, so decide per encounter.
- **Fair, proportional rewards vs. surprise.** He wants consequences that match how good the action was [238](../episodes/notes/238-how-good-were-the-players-actions.md), but warns that taking this too far makes a game predictable. He likes rule-breaking, lucrative enemies, and he likes leaving players room to cheat the system [48](../episodes/notes/048-let-players-cheat-the-system.md). Use proportion as the baseline, and keep a few deliberate exceptions.
- **Getting stronger vs. not snowballing.** Character growth is the most effective reward [151](../episodes/notes/151-planning-your-games-rewards.md), but permanent power-ups let skilled players run away with a race [57](../episodes/notes/057-deconstruct-analyze-and-rebuild.md), and growth makes a fighting game's "real" game start at max level [151](../episodes/notes/151-planning-your-games-rewards.md). Growth fits single-player progression. Competitive play should be rewarded with unlocks and temporary payoffs instead.
- **Fun over realism vs. imitation.** Choose fun when rules conflict with reality [57](../episodes/notes/057-deconstruct-analyze-and-rebuild.md), but borrowing reality's look and sound (mimicry) raises how much information a game carries [178](../episodes/notes/178-what-is-play.md).

## Common mistakes

- Letting the player score from a safe spot, or adding a flashy option that erases risk without a counterweight, like a three-way laser in Space Invaders [6](../episodes/notes/006-risk-and-reward.md).
- Adding verbs that have only an upside, like a dash with no cost or flight with nothing to crash into [65](../episodes/notes/065-falling-sells-the-feeling-of-flight.md) [193](../episodes/notes/193-game-essence-in-action-games.md).
- Underpaying the riskiest option, or making the safe play the highest-scoring play [155](../episodes/notes/155-special-crossover-1.md) [159](../episodes/notes/159-the-shoryuken-command.md).
- Removing stress (enemies, danger, timing strictness) to make a game comfortable, which also removes relief, growth and progress [9](../episodes/notes/009-squeeze-and-release.md) [26](../episodes/notes/026-fun-beyond-game-essence.md) [71](../episodes/notes/071-do-we-really-need-enemies.md).
- Copying another game's mechanic, boss structure or permadeath system without knowing why it worked [57](../episodes/notes/057-deconstruct-analyze-and-rebuild.md) [128](../episodes/notes/128-is-your-game-appealing-enough-to-start-over.md) [219](../episodes/notes/219-mandatory-strategies-and-strategic-balance.md).
- Novelty that only renames the genre's standard action [74](../episodes/notes/074-meteos.md).
- Keeping genre "givens" that add complexity but no decision; building on mashing or holding alone [51](../episodes/notes/051-the-potential-of-one-button.md) [82](../episodes/notes/082-do-competitive-games-have-to-be-complex.md).
- Rewards with no use: currency with nothing worth buying, everything unlocked from the start, score bonuses with no tangible benefit [57](../episodes/notes/057-deconstruct-analyze-and-rebuild.md) [71](../episodes/notes/071-do-we-really-need-enemies.md) [75](../episodes/notes/075-consider-rewards-first.md).
- Permanent power-ups that let the leader snowball in competitive play [57](../episodes/notes/057-deconstruct-analyze-and-rebuild.md) [151](../episodes/notes/151-planning-your-games-rewards.md).
- Assuming competitive fun is only about winning, and leaving long dead time after a defeat [97](../episodes/notes/097-dying-comes-as-a-relief.md).
- Treating game essence as the only source of fun, or pushing it until the game loses its flair [26](../episodes/notes/026-fun-beyond-game-essence.md) [134](../episodes/notes/134-game-essence-in-shooting-games.md).

## Review questions

1. At the exact moment the player can score, how exposed are they?
2. What does each verb cost the player when they use it?
3. Is the riskiest option also clearly the most rewarding one?
4. Where are the stress, relief, growth and progress in this section?
5. Can you say in one sentence why this mechanic is fun, in risk-and-reward terms?
6. What new decision does each rule create, and can a smarter read beat a stronger character?
7. What does the player get, what can they do with it, and does it help with the next challenge?
8. After a loss, does the player want another try?
9. What is this game's main source of fun, and is your polish going there?

## Exercises

1. **Verb ledger (1 hour).** Write every player action in a two-column table: risk and reward. Circle any action with an empty risk column and prototype one downside for it, such as recovery, ammo, stamina or exposure [193](../episodes/notes/193-game-essence-in-action-games.md).
2. **Safe-spot hunt (half a day).** Play your build looking only for places or tactics that let you score without being exposed. Fix the worst one by moving the reward next to a hazard, capping something like on-screen shots, or giving enemies an answer [6](../episodes/notes/006-risk-and-reward.md) [134](../episodes/notes/134-game-essence-in-shooting-games.md).
3. **Pay the brave (2 hours).** Rank your attacks, routes or options by how much risk they demand, then by what they pay. Wherever the two rankings disagree, retune until the riskiest option pays the most, and playtest whether the risky choice now gets picked [159](../episodes/notes/159-the-shoryuken-command.md) [238](../episodes/notes/238-how-good-were-the-players-actions.md).
4. **Squeeze map (2 hours).** For three sections of your game, write the stress, relief, growth and progress. Add an obstacle wherever stress is missing, and a meaningful reward wherever growth is missing [9](../episodes/notes/009-squeeze-and-release.md) [71](../episodes/notes/071-do-we-really-need-enemies.md).
5. **Deconstruct, analyze, rebuild (half a day).** Pick a mechanic you love from another game. Write one sentence on why it's fun in risk-and-reward terms, then sketch three different verbs that deliver the same payoff in your game and prototype the best one [57](../episodes/notes/057-deconstruct-analyze-and-rebuild.md) [86](../episodes/notes/086-game-essence-in-falling-block-puzzle-games.md).
6. **Oppose a power (2 hours).** Take one movement or power-up and design its opposing force (falling, slowdown, a requirement to activate) plus one hazard placed so players can thread the needle [65](../episodes/notes/065-falling-sells-the-feeling-of-flight.md).
