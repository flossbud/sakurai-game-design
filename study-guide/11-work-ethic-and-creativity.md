# 11 · Work Ethic, Creativity and a Career in Games

> Sakurai treats game development as a professional craft in service of players, not a hobby made to his own taste. His working life runs on habits rather than moods: start before you feel ready, invent urgency, clear every review the day it arrives, take in many games and put out work on a steady rhythm. His ideas come from defining problems and studying why things work, not from waiting for inspiration. He stays objective about criticism while protecting what makes a game distinctive, and he never burns bridges, because the industry is small. This matters because a team's habits, health and relationships set the ceiling on what it can ship, and because every flaw you let through stays in the game for everyone who plays it.

## The big ideas

**Work for the player, as a professional.** Sakurai says he makes games because it is what he is best at, not because he loves them most, and he treats every project as an assignment: a genre, a goal and an audience to serve. That is how an experienced gamer made Kirby's Dream Land for newcomers, and a self-described weak puzzle player made Meteos ([7](../episodes/notes/007-game-development-isnt-a-game.md), [261](../episodes/notes/261-masahiro-sakurai-on-creating-games-finale-special.md)). The constant in every assignment is the player. Players won't forgive a flaw because development was hard ([21](../episodes/notes/021-try-telling-that-to-the-player.md)). The hardship fades, but the game, flaws included, endures, and every player multiplies the effort you put in ([67](../episodes/notes/067-youll-forget-the-hardship-but-the-game-will-endure.md)). "The job of a game developer is to create games, but the work itself is not a game." ([7](../episodes/notes/007-game-development-isnt-a-game.md), 00:31)

**Habits beat moods.** Almost every Work Ethic episode is a small system for getting work done without waiting to feel like it. Start the moment you notice you're stalling ([83](../episodes/notes/083-just-do-it-already.md)). Give yourself something to run from ([171](../episodes/notes/171-the-power-of-suggestion.md)). Write proposals in one hot push ([52](../episodes/notes/052-prep-your-proposals-hot-and-fast.md)). Answer every review the same day while protecting a block for your own work ([177](../episodes/notes/177-finish-everything-within-the-day.md)). Keep a small, fixed output habit going for years ([98](../episodes/notes/098-how-i-stuck-with-my-column.md), [126](../episodes/notes/126-broad-input-broad-output.md)). The systems are also built to last: his column took about an hour a week and was written without a sense of duty, he warns against overdoing exercise, and he insists he isn't asking anyone to suffer needlessly ([98](../episodes/notes/098-how-i-stuck-with-my-column.md), [24](../episodes/notes/024-exercise-while-you-game.md), [67](../episodes/notes/067-youll-forget-the-hardship-but-the-game-will-endure.md)).

**Ideas come from input and process, not from nothing.** Trying to think of something from nothing leaves you with no starting point ([237](../episodes/notes/237-when-ideas-wont-come.md)). Sakurai defines the problem first, lists many solutions, and follows the threads of the failed ones. He feeds that process by playing many current games to the end and reflecting on them ([126](../episodes/notes/126-broad-input-broad-output.md)), sometimes completely blind ([199](../episodes/notes/199-a-world-without-footprints.md)), by asking *why* other games do what they do instead of copying them ([9](../episodes/notes/009-squeeze-and-release.md), [57](../episodes/notes/057-deconstruct-analyze-and-rebuild.md)), and by keeping a new idea to himself until the draft is written so its "internal pressure" isn't vented ([144](../episodes/notes/144-keep-your-internal-pressure-high.md)). What sets a game apart in the end is a director's personal, detail-obsessed way of seeing ([70](../episodes/notes/070-directors-need-to-be-unique.md)).

**Criticism and people.** Stay objective: echo chambers make you feel right while hiding the people who disagree ([228](../episodes/notes/228-echo-chambers.md)). But don't let outside voices or majority votes sand off a deliberate, "unbalanced" direction ([228](../episodes/notes/228-echo-chambers.md), [158](../episodes/notes/158-the-majority-shouldnt-always-rule.md)). If a pitch is rejected, the fault is in how you showed the appeal, not in the reviewers' taste ([52](../episodes/notes/052-prep-your-proposals-hot-and-fast.md)). And never fight or badmouth anyone: developers keep meeting each other on new teams, and directors do ask about former teammates ([249](../episodes/notes/249-never-get-into-fights.md)). To get into the industry in the first place, make and finish a game yourself, and let its originality show ([34](../episodes/notes/034-making-a-living-making-games.md)).

### When the advice pulls both ways

- **Speed vs. polish.** Writing everything fast and living with the results ([261](../episodes/notes/261-masahiro-sakurai-on-creating-games-finale-special.md)) and writing proposals while your motivation is hot ([52](../episodes/notes/052-prep-your-proposals-hot-and-fast.md)) sit next to the warning that the hardship fades but the game endures ([67](../episodes/notes/067-youll-forget-the-hardship-but-the-game-will-endure.md)). Speed applies to drafts, proposals, scripts, presentations and decisions, where momentum is worth more than perfection. The quality bar applies to what ships in the game. Even the proposal episode says polish is what makes a game fun ([52](../episodes/notes/052-prep-your-proposals-hot-and-fast.md)).
- **Listen vs. hold your course.** [228](../episodes/notes/228-echo-chambers.md) asks you to seek out disagreement *and* to keep your own "unbalanced" direction, and [158](../episodes/notes/158-the-majority-shouldnt-always-rule.md) says the majority shouldn't rule. The way through: listen for *problems* and facts (team concerns, data, actual matches; see [218](../episodes/notes/218-average-and-mediocre-are-the-same-thing.md)), but don't take *direction* from headcount or from whoever is loudest. Adapt to the team's concerns, and resist pressure that would only make the game more ordinary.
- **Personal taste vs. a personal touch.** Don't design for yourself ([7](../episodes/notes/007-game-development-isnt-a-game.md)), and don't pick an audience from your likes and dislikes ([112](../episodes/notes/112-branching-tastes.md)). Yet a director must put a personal touch only they can offer into the game ([70](../episodes/notes/070-directors-need-to-be-unique.md)). Your taste shouldn't decide *who* the game is for or *what genre* it is. Your sensibility decides *how* it differs from every other game in that genre.
- **Weather any hardship vs. protect yourself.** Sakurai says he would put up with any hardship if it meant more people played his game ([67](../episodes/notes/067-youll-forget-the-hardship-but-the-game-will-endure.md)), and he describes a punishing routine of directing all day, channel work from 8 PM and about six hours of sleep ([261](../episodes/notes/261-masahiro-sakurai-on-creating-games-finale-special.md)). He also says exercise makes people more productive, that you shouldn't overdo it, and that routine work done out of obligation takes a heavy toll ([24](../episodes/notes/024-exercise-while-you-game.md), [81](../episodes/notes/081-games-and-eye-strain.md), [98](../episodes/notes/098-how-i-stuck-with-my-column.md)). Copy his systems, not his hours.
- **Keep it sealed vs. speak up now.** [144](../episodes/notes/144-keep-your-internal-pressure-high.md) says to keep a new proposal to yourself until the draft is done. [60](../episodes/notes/060-dont-wait-to-speak-your-mind.md) and [29](../episodes/notes/029-spec-changes.md) say to raise problems the moment you see them. The first covers an idea still being formed; the second covers issues in shared work, where waiting only wastes everyone's time.
- **Imitate vs. be original.** Copying an existing game is fine for a first project, and finishing anything is a feat ([34](../episodes/notes/034-making-a-living-making-games.md), [196](../episodes/notes/196-crafting-similar-games.md)). But an imitative game won't help a job application, and a commercial game needs appeal the original lacks ([34](../episodes/notes/034-making-a-living-making-games.md), [72](../episodes/notes/072-competing-with-the-past.md)). Imitate to learn; be original to get hired and to ship.

## Principles

### CRAFT-01 · Make every call for the player, not for the developers' convenience
**Why:** Players only care whether the game is good, and they won't cut you slack because development was hard. Entertaining players is the one goal every developer shares, and it's also what keeps you going when a project gets tough. **Do:** Flag every decision made for ease of development and ask whether a player would accept it. Keep it only if it's an efficiency trade players won't notice; otherwise look for alternatives with your programmers. When you're lost or unmotivated, ask how the decision in front of you entertains the player. **Watch out:** Specs that put the developers' jobs first, and the belief that players will forgive problems caused by a hard schedule.
*Sakurai's example:* His standard answer to design documents that prioritize the team's convenience, and his rule for losing your way: "If you feel like you've lost your way, just remember: entertain players." ([7](../episodes/notes/007-game-development-isnt-a-game.md), 03:39) · Episodes: [7](../episodes/notes/007-game-development-isnt-a-game.md), [21](../episodes/notes/021-try-telling-that-to-the-player.md), [67](../episodes/notes/067-youll-forget-the-hardship-but-the-game-will-endure.md)

### CRAFT-02 · Treat each project as a professional assignment, not a hobby
**Why:** Once money, sales and numbers are involved it's a job, indie or not, and being a pro means responding to demand. If you understand what makes games fun, you can create any kind of experience whatever your own taste. **Do:** At kickoff, write down the genre, goal and audience the project must serve, and design to that brief. On tasks you don't enjoy, find what makes them interesting and do them well anyway. Give sequels the same full effort as originals. Practice working in styles outside your specialty. **Watch out:** Designing for yourself, or coasting on a series' name.
*Sakurai's example:* Kirby's Dream Land for newcomers and Meteos despite not being good at block puzzles; an artist who loves cute things texturing zombies and roaches every day; artists matching one team-wide visual style. · Episodes: [7](../episodes/notes/007-game-development-isnt-a-game.md), [130](../episodes/notes/130-unifying-visual-style.md), [162](../episodes/notes/162-making-games-as-part-of-a-company.md), [164](../episodes/notes/164-making-sequels.md), [261](../episodes/notes/261-masahiro-sakurai-on-creating-games-finale-special.md)

### CRAFT-03 · Refuse easy-way-out compromises: the hardship fades, the game endures
**Why:** You forget a project's difficulties but never the parts you got wrong, because they stay in the game. If 1,000 people play your game, the impact is 1,000 times your hardship. Most creative works also have to do something extra to stand out. **Do:** Before taking a shortcut, ask whether you'll regret it after release. Make your workflow efficient first, then put the time you save into what players will feel. Pick the special touches that set the game apart and commit to them. **Watch out:** Too many compromises, and also its opposite: Sakurai explicitly says workers shouldn't go through needless hardship. Effort should serve customers, not prove anything.
*Sakurai's example:* In Kirby's Adventure's ending the screen shakes where it was supposed to flash white, a slip he has never forgotten. "You'll forget the hardship, but the game will endure!" ([67](../episodes/notes/067-youll-forget-the-hardship-but-the-game-will-endure.md), 01:20). He drew Melee's first CG movie storyboards over his New Year's holiday ([40](../episodes/notes/040-super-smash-bros-melee.md)). · Episodes: [67](../episodes/notes/067-youll-forget-the-hardship-but-the-game-will-endure.md), [40](../episodes/notes/040-super-smash-bros-melee.md), [164](../episodes/notes/164-making-sequels.md)

### CRAFT-04 · Start now: motivation arrives after your hands are moving
**Why:** Turning a 0 into a 1 is hard, and thinking about it while unmotivated gets you nowhere. Motivation comes once you've started, and steady output is how you improve. The words you tell yourself set your actions, and the people who act are the ones who win. **Do:** Take the first physical step immediately: type the first line, make the first stroke. Close social media, video sites and games first. Psych yourself up out loud, and leave planning tricks for after you've begun. If you want to make games, make one now with whatever you have. **Watch out:** Waiting to feel ready, or waiting for better tools.
*Sakurai's example:* Work as if a bomb in your room will go off unless you start ([83](../episodes/notes/083-just-do-it-already.md)). Family BASIC, bought with six months of allowance, is what showed him he could make games ([118](../episodes/notes/118-family-basic.md)). · Episodes: [83](../episodes/notes/083-just-do-it-already.md), [212](../episodes/notes/212-say-it-and-it-might-come-true.md), [118](../episodes/notes/118-family-basic.md)

### CRAFT-05 · Manufacture urgency, and favor speed over endlessly polishing drafts
**Why:** If you think you can finish "whenever," the work drifts. If you tell yourself you have only a few hours, it gets done. A proposal that sits goes stale, and over-polishing every line costs succinctness. **Do:** Give each task a short self-imposed limit well ahead of the real deadline, and picture milestones as something chasing you. Once the outline is itemized, write the whole draft in one push. Accept small imperfections in proposals, scripts and talks. **Watch out:** Leaving work to the real last day, like summer homework, and ruminating over drafts. This is about drafts and communication, not about lowering the bar for the shipped game (see CRAFT-03).
*Sakurai's example:* He went from last to first in a school race by imagining a three-meter machine chasing him ([171](../episodes/notes/171-the-power-of-suggestion.md)). He wrote the channel's scripts in about a month, roughly 12 a day: "Short and snappy! Speed above all else!" ([261](../episodes/notes/261-masahiro-sakurai-on-creating-games-finale-special.md)). · Episodes: [171](../episodes/notes/171-the-power-of-suggestion.md), [52](../episodes/notes/052-prep-your-proposals-hot-and-fast.md), [261](../episodes/notes/261-masahiro-sakurai-on-creating-games-finale-special.md), [12](../episodes/notes/012-presentations-are-all-about-speed.md)

### CRAFT-06 · Take a lot in: play many current games to the end, reflectively
**Why:** To deliver work on par with modern games you have to know their scope; Sakurai calls this a must. Input you don't reflect on goes in one ear and out the other, and fun is relative to its era, so nostalgia makes a bad yardstick. **Do:** Play as many different current games as you can rather than one at length, and try to see each ending. Note what works and why while you play or watch. Any input form works if you reflect on it. Replay an old favorite next to a new game before declaring old games better. **Watch out:** Sinking every hour into one game, and holding modern games to childhood memories.
*Sakurai's example:* Even when busy he plays many games and always tries to reach the ending, and he tells fans with thousands of hours in his competitive games to play other games too. · Episodes: [126](../episodes/notes/126-broad-input-broad-output.md), [45](../episodes/notes/045-that-was-then-this-is-now.md), [220](../episodes/notes/220-play-testing-keeps-going.md), [198](../episodes/notes/198-competition-and-abundance.md)

### CRAFT-07 · Put a lot out: keep a small, steady, low-pressure output habit
**Why:** You won't succeed without steady output, and regular writing is training. A habit survives for years if each piece costs little time, uses strengths you already have, and isn't done out of duty, because obligation takes a heavy toll over time. **Do:** Commit to a recurring output: a devlog, a column, a daily team report, a daily screenshot. Time-box each installment, publish what you think is good, and before starting a side project, fix its total run and per-edition size. **Watch out:** Working out of obligation or for the sake of the industry, overthinking each installment, and side projects that grow until they compete with development.
*Sakurai's example:* A Weekly Famitsu column for 18 years and 9 months, 640 articles, each usually done in about an hour: "You have to enjoy it, not feel beholden to it." ([98](../episodes/notes/098-how-i-stuck-with-my-column.md), 02:29). ### CRAFT-08 · Clear every review the same day, and protect a block for your own work
**Why:** Every review that waits leaves teammates waiting, and stalling a key member's decision can put a whole team behind. Quick replies make later rounds easier, and putting replies off just makes the next day worse. But staying open to reviews all day breaks a lead's schedule into fragments. **Do:** Take requests through one channel with a fixed subject, first come, first served. Reply the same day, and explain any changes to everyone involved. Set a delivery cutoff so reviews never start late at night, and keep a fixed no-review block for your own tasks. **Watch out:** Overnight backlogs, late deliveries that wreck sleep, and an always-open door.
*Sakurai's example:* Email-only "Review Request" messages, always answered within the day however many pile up ([177](../episodes/notes/177-finish-everything-within-the-day.md)). His channel editors had to deliver by 8 PM after 11 PM deliveries left him short of sleep: "Putting them off is just asking for trouble." ### CRAFT-09 · Define the problem's theme, then list many ideas before pruning
**Why:** Starting from nothing gives you no foothold, so of course the ideas don't come. Volume gets you past the feeling of having nothing, and small variants of failed ideas often lead to the answer. **Do:** Write the problem as a one-line theme. List broad solution types, silly ones included, and note why each fails. For every failure, look for a tweak that fixes it. Keep thinking actively, and prune only once the list is long. **Watch out:** Brainstorming with no defined problem, and throwing out minor ideas too early.
*Sakurai's example:* "How to put out a fire in some ruins": no water nearby; sand is scarce, but dirt might work; a bucket is too small, but a bigger one might do. "Quantity over quality— save the pruning for later." ([237](../episodes/notes/237-when-ideas-wont-come.md), 01:25) · Episodes: [237](../episodes/notes/237-when-ideas-wont-come.md), [140](../episodes/notes/140-masahiro-sakurai-on-creating-games-introduction-video.md)

### CRAFT-10 · Ask why other games work, then rebuild instead of mimicking
**Why:** Copying something fun won't make your game feel original, and if your game is too close to another, players can simply play the original. Working out *why* other games do things raises your odds of making something fresh. **Do:** Before borrowing a mechanic, write down why it works in its source and whether that reason applies to you. Deconstruct the fun, analyze it (usually as risk and reward), and rebuild it as your own mechanic. If you start from an homage, list many reasons a player would pick yours. **Watch out:** A single point of difference used to justify a near-copy.
*Sakurai's example:* Kirby Air Ride's brake-charge-release drift came from deconstructing why drifting is fun, not from imitating other racers ([57](../episodes/notes/057-deconstruct-analyze-and-rebuild.md)). · Episodes: [9](../episodes/notes/009-squeeze-and-release.md), [57](../episodes/notes/057-deconstruct-analyze-and-rebuild.md), [196](../episodes/notes/196-crafting-similar-games.md), [72](../episodes/notes/072-competing-with-the-past.md) · See also: PLAN-10

### CRAFT-11 · Train a keen personal eye, and put your own take into the game
**Why:** The skilled directors Sakurai knows have little in common except a sensibility that latches onto tiny details and sees what others miss, in visuals, game essence, animation and story. They use their own thoughts and feelings to set their games apart, but they balance that quirkiness with the ability to communicate it. **Do:** When reviewing any build or game, write down the small details most people would miss. For each big decision, state your personal take and how it departs from the genre default, and keep the difference on purpose. Explain it until your team can repeat it back. **Watch out:** Copying one standard way to direct, or a vision nobody else can follow.
*Sakurai's example:* In supervision he flags flaws as small as an extraneous mark on a face or hair poking into an eye highlight, as soon as he spots them ([253](../episodes/notes/253-odds-and-ends-of-supervising-sora-edition.md)). · Episodes: [70](../episodes/notes/070-directors-need-to-be-unique.md), [253](../episodes/notes/253-odds-and-ends-of-supervising-sora-edition.md), [228](../episodes/notes/228-echo-chambers.md)

### CRAFT-12 · Notice echo chambers and seek opinions from outside your circle
**Why:** A flood of agreement makes you feel right while the many who disagree stay out of sight, and an insular world hides problems that need solving. If you can't stay objective, your game ends up unbalanced, whether you play it safer or push it further. Online claims about what's strong or weak gain momentum and seem truer than they are. **Do:** When an idea gets nothing but praise, ask people outside that circle what's wrong with it. Before acting on reviews or social reactions, check whether you're only seeing the voices that agree. Settle your own verdict on a game before reading others'. Test spreading claims against data and real matches. **Watch out:** Letting in only the opinions that validate you.
*Sakurai's example:* Despite online talk about strong and weak fighters, Smash Ultimate's worldwide online data put every fighter's win rate between 47.18% and 51.43% ([218](../episodes/notes/218-average-and-mediocre-are-the-same-thing.md)). · Episodes: [228](../episodes/notes/228-echo-chambers.md), [218](../episodes/notes/218-average-and-mediocre-are-the-same-thing.md), [247](../episodes/notes/247-amplify-both-strengths-and-weaknesses.md), [199](../episodes/notes/199-a-world-without-footprints.md)

### CRAFT-13 · Keep your deliberate direction; don't let outside voices sand it off
**Why:** Sometimes your own "unbalanced" direction is what feels best, and a unique vision gives a game its distinctive strength. Decisions made by majority vote converge on the safest, most mediocre ideas, and ideas everyone opposed in planning often turn out fun once built. **Do:** Sort feedback into team concerns you should adapt to and outside pressure that would only make the game more ordinary. Let the person holding the big picture make creative calls, with reasons, and use polls only as input. **Watch out:** Losing the game's identity to outside voices, and the opposite failure: dictating with paper-thin reasoning.
*Sakurai's example:* Metal Gear Solid 3's wound-treatment system reportedly had only four supporters on its team at first. Sakurai polled his team on a Mii Fighter outfit out of curiosity, then made the director's call to color the socks white ([158](../episodes/notes/158-the-majority-shouldnt-always-rule.md)). · Episodes: [228](../episodes/notes/228-echo-chambers.md), [158](../episodes/notes/158-the-majority-shouldnt-always-rule.md), [247](../episodes/notes/247-amplify-both-strengths-and-weaknesses.md), [218](../episodes/notes/218-average-and-mediocre-are-the-same-thing.md) · See also: TEAM-01

### CRAFT-14 · Steer by your concept when fatigue and doubt set in
**Why:** Development is long, and once fatigue sets in it's easy to wonder what was supposed to be fun. The concept is a lighthouse: change course midway and you get lost, and scrapping work wastes months of art and code and demoralizes the team. Fun is hard to see on paper, so many teammates only grasp it once the game is playable. **Do:** State the concept in one clear line before production, and don't start until it's that clear. When in doubt, check decisions against it, and amplify emergent fun only if it heads toward it. In proposals, commit to the ideal spec instead of offering a Plan B, then course-correct with the team. **Watch out:** Changing direction because the game isn't turning out as you expected.
*Sakurai's example:* Teammates have told him after a project shipped that only then did they see what he had been getting at. · Episodes: [187](../episodes/notes/187-staying-true-to-your-concept.md), [109](../episodes/notes/109-dont-rely-on-a-plan-b.md), [7](../episodes/notes/007-game-development-isnt-a-game.md) · See also: PLAN-01, PLAN-15, TEAM-02

### CRAFT-15 · Treat a rejection as your job to show the appeal better
**Why:** A proposal has to convince the people who provide money and talent that the game must be made, so showing the appeal is the presenter's responsibility. A game unlike anything before runs into preconceptions, and searching for new ways to explain it pays off later. Poor results rarely come down to one person. **Do:** When a pitch is rejected, rework how it shows the fun instead of blaming the reviewers. Describe the game on its own terms, not by comparison. If press or partners won't listen, explain it to players yourself. When something underperforms, review the whole chain. **Watch out:** Deciding the other side has no taste, or hunting for a scapegoat.
*Sakurai's example:* When magazines ignored his document explaining the first Smash Bros.' push and pull, he turned it into the Smash Bros. DOJO!! site, hand-coding the HTML himself ([143](../episodes/notes/143-official-websites.md), [245](../episodes/notes/245-smash-bros-dojo.md)). · Episodes: [52](../episodes/notes/052-prep-your-proposals-hot-and-fast.md), [143](../episodes/notes/143-official-websites.md), [245](../episodes/notes/245-smash-bros-dojo.md), [161](../episodes/notes/161-avoiding-comparison.md), [162](../episodes/notes/162-making-games-as-part-of-a-company.md)

### CRAFT-16 · Never fight or badmouth coworkers: the industry is small
**Why:** Developers move between teams all the time, so someone you wronged may turn up on your next project years later, and directors do ask each other about former teammates. Treating people badly gets you excluded, and dislike comes straight back to you. You also shape your workplace's atmosphere: fixating on difficult people steals attention from the supportive ones. **Do:** Raise problems directly as constructive feedback, ideally with a fix attached. Step back from a bad fit instead of escalating. Finish or hand off your work before you leave. Follow partner companies' rules, meet trouble with calm analysis, and phrase directions as requests. **Watch out:** Fights, gossip, leaving on bad terms, abandoning a project to jump to the next job.
*Sakurai's example:* "Unfortunately, the game industry is much smaller than you might think. So, never get into fights!" ([249](../episodes/notes/249-never-get-into-fights.md), 00:47). ### CRAFT-17 · Break in by finishing your own game and letting originality show
**Why:** Making a game yourself is the quickest, if not only, way in. You learn what problems come up and how to solve them, and even designers need to think like programmers sometimes. Many people want to make games, so an imitative idea won't set you apart. **Do:** Start small in any tool you can get (Unity, Unreal Engine, C, JavaScript, visual programming or "Maker" software) and finish it. Have friends play it or release it free online, learn from what they say, then improve it or carry the lessons forward. Send an original game with your applications even if you don't meet every requirement, and enter open proposal calls. **Watch out:** Waiting for ideal tools, and showcasing a copy.
*Sakurai's example:* Family BASIC taught him he could make games ([118](../episodes/notes/118-family-basic.md)). Before entering the industry he won Sega's Associate Planner Certificate No. 004 through a public proposal promotion ([149](../episodes/notes/149-show-and-tell-some-rare-belongings.md)). · Episodes: [34](../episodes/notes/034-making-a-living-making-games.md), [118](../episodes/notes/118-family-basic.md), [196](../episodes/notes/196-crafting-similar-games.md), [149](../episodes/notes/149-show-and-tell-some-rare-belongings.md)

### CRAFT-18 · Keep a new idea sealed until its first draft is written
**Why:** Talking about an idea makes you feel fulfilled, and that releases the "internal pressure" that gives a proposal its force. A document written without sharing a word burns hotter than one compiled from a brainstorm. Posting progress online vents the same pressure. **Do:** Each time you want to tell someone about the idea, write it into the document instead. Hold off on work-in-progress posts. Consult colleagues for weak points once the draft is finished. **Watch out:** Pitching half-formed ideas in conversation, or letting a group brainstorm stand in for your own draft. This does not mean hiding *problems* from the team (see [60](../episodes/notes/060-dont-wait-to-speak-your-mind.md)).
*Sakurai's example:* His balloon and pressure-cooker metaphors. · Episodes: [144](../episodes/notes/144-keep-your-internal-pressure-high.md), [52](../episodes/notes/052-prep-your-proposals-hot-and-fast.md)

### CRAFT-19 · Turn negative thoughts into questions about how to improve
**Why:** Every time you think, say or post a defeatist idea it becomes a little more true, because your words set your attitude. Negative feelings are fine, and used as fuel they can drive more change than positivity. **Do:** Stop voicing or posting lines about there being no point in working. Rewrite each one as a question about what you could change to make the work better, and act on the answer. **Watch out:** Forced positivity that denies the feeling.
*Sakurai's example:* His examples of self-talk to drop are complaints about money, about work being pointless and about life not being fun. · Episodes: [212](../episodes/notes/212-say-it-and-it-might-come-true.md), [83](../episodes/notes/083-just-do-it-already.md)

### CRAFT-20 · Protect your body: exercise a little and fix your workstation
**Why:** People who exercise tend to be far more productive. Screens close to the eyes cause strain, and nothing works as well as changing your environment. A missing desk and chair will cause health problems. **Do:** Get some regular exercise, for example by pedaling an exercise bike through play sessions. Keep about a meter between your eyes and the monitor (if it's under 60 cm, move it back and get a bigger screen). Match brightness to the room, and make sure everyone has a real desk and chair. **Watch out:** Overdoing it, and treating eyedrops or glasses as a substitute for fixing the setup.
*Sakurai's example:* Two-hour exercise-bike sessions, and sore legs from climbing about 500 stairs ([24](../episodes/notes/024-exercise-while-you-game.md)). Severe eye strain during Smash Ultimate ([81](../episodes/notes/081-games-and-eye-strain.md)). ### CRAFT-21 · Give one project all of you, and keep overhead off creative time
**Why:** A project that needs its director's full attention suffers when that attention is split. Independent creators who hire staff often drown in the management needed to keep everyone paid, with no time left for the game. **Do:** Before taking a demanding lead role, decline or finish competing work. Direct one project at a time. Before founding a studio, estimate how much of your week management will eat. **Watch out:** Juggling side agreements, or hiring by default.
*Sakurai's example:* For Brawl he turned down his other projects except Mushiking, which was already under way ([132](../episodes/notes/132-the-team-behind-super-smash-bros-brawl.md)). ### CRAFT-22 · Picture the real people who will play, and let that fuel you
**Why:** Imagining how people will react to specific parts gives you the strength to push on. Actual people will buy what you make, and what children love can shape their tastes for life. Imagining a presentation also organizes your thinking. **Do:** While writing and building, picture specific reactions to specific moments. Mentally present your build to customers and note what you'd change. Share outside players' reactions with the team during hard stretches. **Watch out:** Losing sight of the audience behind the task list.
*Sakurai's example:* An elementary-school playtester shouting with excitement at how far a Falcon Punch launched someone in the original Smash Bros. ([248](../episodes/notes/248-elementary-school-play-testers.md)); Smash Ultimate topping a 2019 CoroCoro Comic survey of children's favorite games ([124](../episodes/notes/124-games-during-my-childhood.md)). · Episodes: [144](../episodes/notes/144-keep-your-internal-pressure-high.md), [67](../episodes/notes/067-youll-forget-the-hardship-but-the-game-will-endure.md), [207](../episodes/notes/207-playing-games-in-front-of-other-people.md), [248](../episodes/notes/248-elementary-school-play-testers.md), [124](../episodes/notes/124-games-during-my-childhood.md)

### CRAFT-23 · Respect the labor behind every game, and judge it by its own goals
**Why:** Every game has its own values, goals and audience, and diversity is what makes games great. Finishing any game means cuts and hardship, and many games never ship. Creators who vanish from view have often found success elsewhere. **Do:** Judge a game by what it set out to do for its players. Approach others' games with gratitude, frame awards as celebration rather than a race, and don't call vanished creators washed up. Give yourself the same freedom to try something new. **Watch out:** Ranking everything on one scale, or treating sales as proof of ingenuity.
*Sakurai's example:* The Game Designers Award, judged on originality by directors of unique games ([13](../episodes/notes/013-game-awards.md)); master-up often leaving a team bruised rather than triumphant ([87](../episodes/notes/087-mastering-up.md)); starting a YouTube channel while still making games ([176](../episodes/notes/176-creators-change-too.md)). · Episodes: [13](../episodes/notes/013-game-awards.md), [87](../episodes/notes/087-mastering-up.md), [198](../episodes/notes/198-competition-and-abundance.md), [176](../episodes/notes/176-creators-change-too.md), [31](../episodes/notes/031-the-price-of-games.md), [110](../episodes/notes/110-canceled-games.md)

### CRAFT-24 · Study your genre's roots, then carve a new path
**Why:** The path today's creators walk was carved by their predecessors. Playing foundational games shows how clever ideas built on one another, and that knowledge is the basis for making your own path. **Do:** Play the landmark games of your genre, note which mechanic each introduced and what it cost, then aim at something none of them did. **Watch out:** Learning a genre only from its latest hits, or retracing an old path instead of extending it.
*Sakurai's example:* Scramble's separate shot and bomb buttons and fuel gauge laid groundwork for Gradius; Moon Patrol had possibly the first parallax scrolling ([155](../episodes/notes/155-special-crossover-1.md)); 1986's jumps in storage capacity ([209](../episodes/notes/209-1986-the-year-of-legend.md)). · Episodes: [155](../episodes/notes/155-special-crossover-1.md), [156](../episodes/notes/156-special-crossover-2.md), [157](../episodes/notes/157-special-crossover-3.md), [209](../episodes/notes/209-1986-the-year-of-legend.md), [45](../episodes/notes/045-that-was-then-this-is-now.md)

### CRAFT-25 · Don't let convenient tools or obvious answers fence in your ideas
**Why:** If you're too tied to convenient systems, it's hard to step away from them, and you start setting mental limits on what's possible. The obvious choice isn't always right. **Do:** Regularly ask which "impossible" ideas are only the limits of a tool or convention. Before adopting a stock solution (a default rig, a raw leaderboard, a template site), sketch one alternative built for your game. **Watch out:** Treating defaults as the edge of the possible.
*Sakurai's example:* Realistic skeleton rigs stop joints at 180 degrees, while great animation pushes past common sense ([236](../episodes/notes/236-the-limitations-of-skeletons.md)); Smash's Global Smash Power in place of a standard leaderboard ([170](../episodes/notes/170-the-pros-and-cons-of-leaderboards.md)). · Episodes: [236](../episodes/notes/236-the-limitations-of-skeletons.md), [170](../episodes/notes/170-the-pros-and-cons-of-leaderboards.md), [143](../episodes/notes/143-official-websites.md)

### CRAFT-26 · Use advice to sharpen your own judgment, not to replace it
**Why:** Sakurai calls his lessons a boost on top of your own experience, skill and way of thinking, and says your mileage may vary. Knowledge only means something once it's used. **Do:** Test each lesson against your own build, keep what fits, and keep making and playing games to build the judgment it depends on. **Watch out:** Following rules blindly, or collecting advice without applying any.

## Common mistakes

- Choosing the easy option for the team and expecting players to forgive it.
- Designing the game you'd want to play instead of the one the assignment and audience need.
- Waiting for motivation, inspiration or better tools before starting.
- Polishing drafts and proposals forever, or the reverse: shipping game flaws because you were moving fast.
- Letting review requests and decisions sit overnight, or leaving yourself open to interruptions all day.
- Playing one game for hundreds of hours and nothing else, or judging every modern game against childhood favorites.
- Taking on routine output out of obligation until it burns you out.
- Talking a half-formed idea to death, or posting it online, before it's written.
- Hearing only agreement and mistaking it for consensus; or caving to loud outside voices and votes until the game is ordinary.
- Changing the concept midway because development fatigue makes the game look less fun.
- Blaming reviewers' taste when a pitch is rejected, or one teammate when a game underperforms.
- Fighting, gossiping or leaving a project on bad terms in an industry where you'll meet everyone again.
- Copying Sakurai's punishing hours instead of his systems; neglecting exercise, sleep and your screen setup.
- Applying to studios with an imitative game, or not applying because you miss one requirement.

## Review questions

- Which decisions in this build were made for our convenience, and would a player accept each one?
- Is the pace we're working at one we could keep for years? What would break first?
- What recent games has each of us finished, and what did we learn from them?
- Who is waiting on me right now, and for how long?
- What in this game could only have come from us?
- Are we hearing real disagreement from outside our circle, and are we still protecting what's distinctive?
- Can everyone state the concept in one line, and does today's work move toward it?
- If every teammate on this project joined my next one, would I be glad?
- What did I actually finish this week?

## Exercises

1. **Convenience audit (1–2 hours).** List ten decisions in your current build that were made because they were easier to build. For each, write whether a player would accept it if they knew. Pick the worst one and spend an hour with a programmer finding an alternative. (CRAFT-01)
2. **Fire-in-the-ruins drill (45 minutes).** Take a design problem you're stuck on and write it as a one-line theme. Each person lists as many solution types as they can, silly ones included, with a one-line "why it fails" for each, then writes a fixing variant for every failure. Prune only at the end. (CRAFT-09)
3. **Same-day review day (one workday).** Each lead answers every request and review the day it arrives, with a cutoff time for deliveries and a no-review block in the morning. At the end of the day, count how many people would otherwise have waited overnight. (CRAFT-08)
4. **Input/output hour (2 hours).** Everyone plays a recent game near your genre for an hour and writes down what works and why. Then each person posts a short report to the team: one screenshot and three to five points. (CRAFT-06, CRAFT-07)
5. **Echo-chamber check (half a day).** Take the feature your team and community praise most. Ask people outside that circle (another team, players of other genres) what's wrong with it. Sort the answers into problems to fix and pressure toward the ordinary, and decide on each one. (CRAFT-12, CRAFT-13)
6. **Lighthouse check (30 minutes).** Each team member writes the game's concept in one line without conferring. Compare the lines, agree on one, then check your last week of tasks against it. (CRAFT-14)
