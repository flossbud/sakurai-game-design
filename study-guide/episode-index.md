## In release order

| # | Episode | Category | Lesson |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | [About This Channel](../episodes/notes/001-about-this-channel.md) | Special | Use outside design lessons as a small boost on top of your own experience, skill and way of thinking, and keep the shared goal in view: making games a little more fun. |
| 2 | [Masahiro Sakurai's Creative Works](../episodes/notes/002-masahiro-sakurais-creative-works.md) | Special | Be able to sum up each game in one line that says who it is for or what its hook is, the way every one of Sakurai's works is captioned here. |
| 3 | [Stop for Big Moments!](../episodes/notes/003-stop-for-big-moments.md) | Design Specifics | Briefly stop the action at your game's big moments (landing a hit, beating a boss, getting hit or dying) so players clearly feel the impact of their success or defeat. |
| 4 | [Kirby's Dream Land](../episodes/notes/004-kirbys-dream-land.md) | Game Concepts | Decide who your game is for and shape its risks to match, as Kirby's Dream Land cut risk on purpose (inhale enemies, fly over pits) to welcome newcomers and relied on fun worth replaying, not punishing difficulty, for long-term value. |
| 5 | [Frame Rates](../episodes/notes/005-frame-rates.md) | Planning & Game Design | Treat frames as your game's unit of time (distance = time × speed), and choose your frame rate on purpose: 60 fps is ideal, 30 fps is an acceptable standard. |
| 6 | [Risk and Reward](../episodes/notes/006-risk-and-reward.md) | Game Essence | Build your core fun from risk and reward placed close together in the right amounts, so the moment of greatest danger is also the moment the player can win something. |
| 7 | [Game Development Isn't a Game](../episodes/notes/007-game-development-isnt-a-game.md) | Work Ethic | Approach game development as a professional job: set your personal taste aside, find the fun in whatever genre or goal you're given, and never lose sight of entertaining players. |
| 8 | [Down with Lag!](../episodes/notes/008-down-with-lag.md) | Grab Bag | Your game's responsiveness is only as good as the player's display and audio setup, so learn to recognize input lag and test and demo on screens set to Game Mode or Low Latency Mode with extra processing turned off. |
| 9 | [Squeeze and Release](../episodes/notes/009-squeeze-and-release.md) | Game Essence | Put players under stress with enemies, obstacles or puzzles, then release it, and reward their success with choices that help them handle the next, bigger squeeze. |
| 10 | [Draw the Light, Not the Asset](../episodes/notes/010-draw-the-light-not-the-asset.md) | Graphics | Don't call an asset finished once it's modeled and textured; finish it by showing the light it reflects in its environment, using cheap techniques like colored light, hue variation and atmosphere. |
| 11 | [Kirby's Adventure](../episodes/notes/011-kirbys-adventure.md) | Game Concepts | When your audience mixes beginners and veterans, layer optional depth on a simple core, as copy abilities sit on top of inhale-and-spit, so novices can finish the game and experts can dig deeper. |
| 12 | [Presentations Are All About Speed!](../episodes/notes/012-presentations-are-all-about-speed.md) | Work Ethic | Keep idea presentations fast-moving: cut the fat, go straight to the heart of the topic, drop justifications and alternatives, and use simple graphics. |
| 13 | [Game Awards](../episodes/notes/013-game-awards.md) | Grab Bag | Treat awards as a celebration for creators and fans rather than a race, judge each game against its own goals and audience, and value originality, not just sales. |
| 14 | [Assigning Animations](../episodes/notes/014-assigning-animations.md) | Animation | Before animation starts, give animators an animation list with every move's length and attack timing (chosen around the game's push and pull) plus photos of a posed figure for key poses, and give direction with all the animators in the room. |
| 15 | [Game Essence in Role-Playing Games](../episodes/notes/015-game-essence-in-role-playing-games.md) | Game Essence | Build an RPG's push and pull from exploration (go deeper for bigger rewards at the risk of losing your spoils) and combat (read enemies and exploit their weaknesses), and match the complexity to how casual your game is. |
| 16 | [Make It "Pop"](../episodes/notes/016-make-it-pop.md) | Effects | Don't rely on additive blending alone; mix crisp shapes and darker elements into your effects so they pop with contrast against any background. |
| 17 | [Just Let Them Play!](../episodes/notes/017-just-let-them-play.md) | Planning & Game Design | Make players understand why your game is fun within the first three minutes by getting to real gameplay fast, in both your trailers and your opening, and saving long story and CG for later. |
| 18 | [Clarity vs. Style](../episodes/notes/018-clarity-vs-style.md) | UI | Treat clarity and style in UI as a trade-off you settle per game: organize menus like a tidy drawer, fit the style to your game's content and setting, and make things as user-friendly as you can. |
| 19 | [Giving "Weight" to Buttons](../episodes/notes/019-giving-weight-to-buttons.md) | Design Specifics | Use what happens on screen (movement speed, inertia, jump physics, lag, frame rate) to decide how "heavy" or "light" the same physical button feels, and give each character its own distinct weight. |
| 20 | [Kirby Super Star](../episodes/notes/020-kirby-super-star.md) | Game Concepts | Meet hard requests with structural ideas, as Kirby Super Star made co-op work by splitting players into a lead and a Helper and offered lots of content without wearing players out by packaging short, distinct scenarios as an "omnibus." |
