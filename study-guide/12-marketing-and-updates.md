# 12 · Marketing, Reveals and Online Updates

> To Sakurai, marketing is half of shipping. Development and marketing multiply each other, so a great game nobody hears about has wasted its effort, and he does far more promotion than most directors: reveal videos, sites he lays out himself, presentations he plays live. His rules are simple and strict. Show the real game, fast, and make every image match how it actually feels. Explain an unusual game's essence to players in the designer's own words, on a schedule they can rely on. Know the market well enough to choose an audience on purpose, but never let research invent the concept. And ship as if no patch will ever come, because updates can't win back players who have already left. This matters more every year: thousands of games launch annually, and each one competes for players' time with everything ever made.

## The big ideas

**Being known is part of the job.** "An unknown game may as well not exist." ([80](../episodes/notes/080-marketing-is-multiplicative.md), 00:25) Sakurai calls development and marketing two wheels on one axle: if one doesn't turn, you don't move ([80](../episodes/notes/080-marketing-is-multiplicative.md), [245](../episodes/notes/245-smash-bros-dojo.md)). He is a director, not a producer, but he asks publishers for the marketing and operations plan, checks every official post, writes reveal-video plots, and makes in-game art with outside use in mind ([80](../episodes/notes/080-marketing-is-multiplicative.md), [234](../episodes/notes/234-directors-and-producers.md), [163](../episodes/notes/163-fighter-reveal-videos.md)). Publicity alone usually isn't enough, which is why demos, sites and showcases exist ([122](../episodes/notes/122-game-demos.md)), and on storefronts with an almost endless lineup, a banner decides whether anyone looks at all ([152](../episodes/notes/152-do-graphics-impact-fun.md)).

**Show the game, honestly and quickly.** Players decide fast whether a game is fun, and they can't buy something when they can't tell what it is ([17](../episodes/notes/017-just-let-them-play.md)). So a trailer should get to gameplay as early as possible, skipping the logos and long intros that viewers habitually skip anyway ([186](../episodes/notes/186-show-the-actual-game.md)). Everything a buyer sees before playing (title, box, screenshots, trailer, demo) has to match how the game actually feels. Otherwise you miss the people who would love it and disappoint the people you fooled ([101](../episodes/notes/101-paint-an-accurate-picture-of-your-game.md), [122](../episodes/notes/122-game-demos.md)). Within that honesty, push every shot bolder: zoom in, change the angle, add interaction, cut to the music ([163](../episodes/notes/163-fighter-reveal-videos.md)).

**Explain it yourself, on a rhythm.** When the first Smash Bros. was dismissed as Nintendo characters beating each other up, magazines ignored Sakurai's explanation of its depth, so he published it himself as the Smash Bros. DOJO!! players' guide ([143](../episodes/notes/143-official-websites.md), [245](../episodes/notes/245-smash-bros-dojo.md)). Later Dojo sites posted one new detail every weekday, in six languages, written by the person who knew the game best ([245](../episodes/notes/245-smash-bros-dojo.md)). Today that role is played by an official home base shaped around the game's content ([143](../episodes/notes/143-official-websites.md)), presentations in which the director plays the game ([207](../episodes/notes/207-playing-games-in-front-of-other-people.md)), and reveal videos built like small films ([163](../episodes/notes/163-fighter-reveal-videos.md)).

**Know the market without letting it design for you, and treat patches as a bonus.** Tastes branch apart as players age, so pick your audience from a top-down view of the market, not your own likes, and describe it by experience level (Kirby was for beginners, not for children) rather than demographics ([112](../episodes/notes/112-branching-tastes.md), [226](../episodes/notes/226-targeting-and-market-research.md)). Your competition is every game a player could spend the same hours on, old ones included, plus their phone ([72](../episodes/notes/072-competing-with-the-past.md), [198](../episodes/notes/198-competition-and-abundance.md), [250](../episodes/notes/250-be-mindful-of-the-time-players-are-giving-you.md)). But research only describes paths already taken, so it can't generate a new idea ([226](../episodes/notes/226-targeting-and-market-research.md)). After launch, fix fatal bugs as a must, treat balance and quality-of-life updates as a bonus weighed against freeing the team, and keep fixes narrow ([231](../episodes/notes/231-online-updates.md), [141](../episodes/notes/141-unexpected-results.md)). No patch helps once players have quit ([231](../episodes/notes/231-online-updates.md)).

### When the advice pulls both ways

- **Know the market vs. distrust market research.** [112](../episodes/notes/112-branching-tastes.md) says the more aware you are of the market, the better you can target it. [226](../episodes/notes/226-targeting-and-market-research.md) says research data can't show what comes next. Market awareness is for choosing and describing *who* the game is for, and for checking risks. The *concept* should come from somewhere new, not from a chart of what already sells.
- **Ship it perfect vs. plan for patches.** [231](../episodes/notes/231-online-updates.md) says to ship in the best possible condition because patches can't recover lost players, while [87](../episodes/notes/087-mastering-up.md), [135](../episodes/notes/135-super-smash-bros-for-3ds-wii-u.md) and [141](../episodes/notes/141-unexpected-results.md) describe planning people for post-release fixes, DLC that stretched a schedule by more than a year, and narrow patches for launch problems. Set the release bar as if no update will come, and staff for updates anyway. They are insurance and a bonus, not part of the plan for the launch build.
- **Listen to fans vs. keep the plan.** A fan survey with about 1.8 million valid votes shaped Smash Ultimate's new fighters ([160](../episodes/notes/160-super-smash-bros-ultimate.md)), and persistent follower requests got their own video ([39](../episodes/notes/039-my-feline-friend-fukurashi.md)). Yet Sakurai wrote every channel script in advance and couldn't take requests ([261](../episodes/notes/261-masahiro-sakurai-on-creating-games-finale-special.md)), and he warns about echo chambers ([228](../episodes/notes/228-echo-chambers.md)). Gather structured input while planning; once a plan is set, don't let comment threads steer it.
- **Keep ideas sealed vs. post constantly.** [144](../episodes/notes/144-keep-your-internal-pressure-high.md) says posting ideas or progress on social media vents creative pressure, but the Dojo posted screenshots and news during Melee's development ([245](../episodes/notes/245-smash-bros-dojo.md)). The first applies to an idea still being formed. The second is planned communication about an announced game whose direction is already set.
- **Skip trailers vs. make trailers essential.** Sakurai closes his eyes at trailers for things he has already decided to see, so he can play blind ([199](../episodes/notes/199-a-world-without-footprints.md)), yet he won't buy a game without a gameplay trailer ([186](../episodes/notes/186-show-the-actual-game.md)). Trailers serve people deciding whether to buy; once someone is sold, the game itself should do the surprising.
- **Honesty vs. surprise.** Misrepresenting a game for surprise is the big no-no, yet Kirby Super Star's Japanese wooden-box cover deliberately looked unrelated to the game ([101](../episodes/notes/101-paint-an-accurate-picture-of-your-game.md)). Indirect marketing is fine when it captures the heart of the game; when in doubt, show the game for what it is.
- **Director-led marketing vs. the director's time.** The director knows the game best and should shape its messaging ([245](../episodes/notes/245-smash-bros-dojo.md)), but the director is also the busiest person on the team, and Sakurai says that with supervision, approvals and marketing on his plate he should never have been entering parameters himself ([135](../episodes/notes/135-super-smash-bros-for-3ds-wii-u.md), [245](../episodes/notes/245-smash-bros-dojo.md)). Delegate production work and keep the director on writing and review.

## Principles

### MKT-01 · Treat making the game known as seriously as making it good
**Why:** Development and marketing are multiplicative: the success of one is proportional to the success of the other. An estimated 10,000+ games were listed on Steam in 2021 alone, so nobody finds a game by chance, and publicity alone usually isn't enough. **Do:** From early in development, plan what the dev team itself will contribute (reveal videos, a site, a demo, presentations) and put it in the schedule. Ask your publisher for the marketing and operations plan while you can still suggest ideas, and plan how each piece of news will reach people scrolling social feeds. **Watch out:** Assuming a good game will be found, and treating marketing as some other department's problem.
*Sakurai's example:* As a director he takes on many producer duties apart from money, from fighter reveal movies to the Smash Bros. DOJO!! site to Mr. Sakurai Presents. · Episodes: [80](../episodes/notes/080-marketing-is-multiplicative.md), [245](../episodes/notes/245-smash-bros-dojo.md), [122](../episodes/notes/122-game-demos.md), [234](../episodes/notes/234-directors-and-producers.md), [31](../episodes/notes/031-the-price-of-games.md), [152](../episodes/notes/152-do-graphics-impact-fun.md)

### MKT-02 · Make every marketing image match how the game actually feels
**Why:** Until people play, they judge by title, box art, screenshots and videos. A mismatch fails to reach the people the game would resonate with, and disappoints the people misled into buying it, like a banana that tastes like an apple. **Do:** Lay your title, cover, screenshots, trailer and demo next to real play and fix whatever sets the wrong expectation. Surprises are fine as long as the overall picture matches. Use indirect packaging only if it captures the game's heart, and show tech demos only if they truthfully reflect how the game runs. **Watch out:** Misleading for the sake of surprise.
*Sakurai's example:* "No matter how much you want to catch your players by surprise, don't paint a picture that doesn't represent your game!" ([101](../episodes/notes/101-paint-an-accurate-picture-of-your-game.md), 00:33). His exception, Kirby Super Star's Japanese wooden-box cover (Shigesato Itoi's idea), looked unrelated but got to the heart of the game. · Episodes: [101](../episodes/notes/101-paint-an-accurate-picture-of-your-game.md), [186](../episodes/notes/186-show-the-actual-game.md), [122](../episodes/notes/122-game-demos.md)

### MKT-03 · Get to real gameplay as fast as possible in every trailer
**Why:** Players judge quickly, and they can't buy a game when they can't tell what kind of game it is. Viewers skip long logos and intros. A trailer will never match a film's cinematics, so its job is to show what only your game offers. Famous series can lean on teasers; new IP, and indie titles especially, can't. **Do:** Treat a gameplay trailer as a required deliverable, including on your store page. Cut opening logos, long movies and character intros, lead with the game's appeal, and push story and world later. If you don't open on raw play, open on footage that clearly shows the systems, or on an honest tech demo. **Watch out:** Store-page trailers with no gameplay at all.
*Sakurai's example:* He won't buy a game without a trailer unless there's a very good reason, and has almost made a habit of skipping trailer openings: "And please, get to the actual game as soon as possible!" ([186](../episodes/notes/186-show-the-actual-game.md), 00:50) · Episodes: [186](../episodes/notes/186-show-the-actual-game.md), [17](../episodes/notes/017-just-let-them-play.md), [12](../episodes/notes/012-presentations-are-all-about-speed.md) · See also: PLAN-03

### MKT-04 · Ship in the best shape possible: patches can't win back players who left
**Why:** However much patches improve a game, they mean nothing to players who have already given up on it. A perfect release is all but impossible for modern games: they are hugely complex, every fix restarts testing so developers barely play the final build, and the market finds one-in-a-thousand bugs. That is a reason to aim high from day one, not an excuse. **Do:** Set the release bar as if no update will ever come. Lock content early enough to play a near-final build, and recheck difficulty on retail hardware after late optimization. After launch, treat player videos and reports as your biggest test pass. **Watch out:** Shipping rough because you plan to fix it later.
*Sakurai's example:* "no matter how much you improve your game through patches, it won't mean anything if your players have already given up on the game." ([231](../episodes/notes/231-online-updates.md), 03:31). Late optimization made Meteos faster and harder than he had tuned it ([141](../episodes/notes/141-unexpected-results.md)). · Episodes: [231](../episodes/notes/231-online-updates.md), [136](../episodes/notes/136-bug-testing-never-ends.md), [141](../episodes/notes/141-unexpected-results.md), [87](../episodes/notes/087-mastering-up.md)

### MKT-05 · Choose your audience from a top-down market view, not your own taste
**Why:** Players' styles have branched far apart, so your own likes and dislikes misread the market. Tastes split with age: the broadest market is the trunk, younger players before everyone finds their branch, while aiming squarely at one branch can make its players fall in love with a game. Age and gender lines are blurry; beginners come in every age and gender. **Do:** Map the player segments that exist now and choose one on purpose. Describe the target by experience level or need. For broad reach, use orthodox, widely appealing design; for a niche, commit to that branch's values. Then set the risk in your rules to fit that audience. **Watch out:** Choosing an audience because it's you, or treating demographic slices as hard walls.
*Sakurai's example:* The original Kirby was designed for beginners, never "for children," yet has enough oomph that advanced players enjoy its controls ([226](../episodes/notes/226-targeting-and-market-research.md)); Nintendo's top sellers are hits with younger generations ([112](../episodes/notes/112-branching-tastes.md)). · Episodes: [112](../episodes/notes/112-branching-tastes.md), [226](../episodes/notes/226-targeting-and-market-research.md), [148](../episodes/notes/148-more-game-essence-means-less-broad-appeal.md), [7](../episodes/notes/007-game-development-isnt-a-game.md) · See also: PLAN-09, BAL-01

### MKT-06 · Don't let market research generate your concept
**Why:** "Targeting and market research data is built on everything that's come before. They don't show the path to what comes next." ([226](../episodes/notes/226-targeting-and-market-research.md), 01:33) Research shows the safe main road everyone else walked; follow it and your work becomes more average and mediocre, and it sells less. Innovators stand alone, but they open the roads others follow. **Do:** Form the concept first and use data to check its risks. When a genre is booming, ask how crowded it will be when you release and what you offer that the others don't. Keep graphs and research out of the pitch and spend that space on what's new. **Watch out:** Chasing what sells now, or deciding direction by what most people already like.
*Sakurai's example:* Around the NES-to-SNES transition, research showed RPGs selling, so many Japanese companies made Dragon Quest and Final Fantasy-like games and all dove into an overcrowded market. · Episodes: [226](../episodes/notes/226-targeting-and-market-research.md), [52](../episodes/notes/052-prep-your-proposals-hot-and-fast.md), [158](../episodes/notes/158-the-majority-shouldnt-always-rule.md), [72](../episodes/notes/072-competing-with-the-past.md)

### MKT-07 · Count every game, old or new, as competition for players' time
**Why:** To a player, an hour on an old title is worth the same as an hour on a new one, and subscription services set back catalogs right beside new releases. Games take many hours and more effort to start than low-barrier phone content, and a giant play-time number can scare buyers away. **Do:** List what your target player could spend the same hours on (current releases, older games, subscription catalogs, phones and social media) and write why they'd pick yours. Measure against the best games in the genre, offer something fresh that can't be found elsewhere, and lead with fun rather than an hour count. **Watch out:** Assuming your only rivals are this year's releases, or advertising 300 hours of content as the main attraction.
*Sakurai's example:* Subscription video services pit every new film against decades of back catalog ([72](../episodes/notes/072-competing-with-the-past.md)); being told up front that a game has 300 hours of content is likely to put you off ([250](../episodes/notes/250-be-mindful-of-the-time-players-are-giving-you.md)). · Episodes: [72](../episodes/notes/072-competing-with-the-past.md), [198](../episodes/notes/198-competition-and-abundance.md), [250](../episodes/notes/250-be-mindful-of-the-time-players-are-giving-you.md), [31](../episodes/notes/031-the-price-of-games.md)

### MKT-08 · When a trailer shot feels good enough, zoom in and go bolder
**Why:** Sakurai's most frequent notes on reveal footage were all ways past adequate: bolder framing, a changed angle of view, more interaction between characters, a balance of serious and comic, and cuts synced to the music. Shallow depth of field and focus pulls make big things read as small. **Do:** Run every shot through those five checks. Remove focus shifts unless a shot truly needs one. Polish faces, eyes and highlights that will be seen up close. **Watch out:** Safe, medium framing that nobody will remember.
*Sakurai's example:* "When you think something's good enough, zoom in and go bolder." ([163](../episodes/notes/163-fighter-reveal-videos.md), 04:38). He had a background-to-foreground focus shift removed from Sora's reveal ([174](../episodes/notes/174-making-things-look-miniature.md)) and Kazuya's eye glint made stronger and sharper ([165](../episodes/notes/165-odds-and-ends-of-supervising.md)). · Episodes: [163](../episodes/notes/163-fighter-reveal-videos.md), [174](../episodes/notes/174-making-things-look-miniature.md), [165](../episodes/notes/165-odds-and-ends-of-supervising.md)

### MKT-09 · Explain an unusual game's essence to players yourself
**Why:** A game unlike anything before runs into preconceptions. People dismissed the first Smash Bros. before release; even Nintendo's sales staff and wholesalers rejected the idea before playing it, and a document explaining its depth sent to magazines had little impact. Searching for new ways to explain pays off later, and the director knows the game and its fun best. **Do:** Write a plain explanation of how the game plays, its core essence and where its push and pull lies, and publish it to players directly as a guide if the press won't run it. Have the person who knows the game best write it or review it, and delegate the production work. **Watch out:** Relying on press coverage alone to explain something new.
*Sakurai's example:* His document "The Secrets of Smash Bros." became the Smash Bros. DOJO!! players' guide, which he hand-coded in an HTML editor; he traces the effort behind Ultimate's fighter reveals back to that setback. · Episodes: [245](../episodes/notes/245-smash-bros-dojo.md), [143](../episodes/notes/143-official-websites.md), [27](../episodes/notes/027-super-smash-bros.md), [234](../episodes/notes/234-directors-and-producers.md) · See also: CASE-15

### MKT-10 · Post updates on a fixed, frequent schedule fans can make a habit
**Why:** People come to a site excited to see something new, and that works well once it becomes a daily routine; something new every day gets read where a mass email doesn't. Official posts speak for the game, so someone who knows it should check every one. **Do:** Release one new detail on a set cadence. Have the director write or review the text and the team handle production such as screenshots. Review every official social and website update before it goes out, shape news for people scrolling feeds, and keep the announcements off your personal account. **Watch out:** Irregular dumps of information, unreviewed posts, and flooding your personal feed, which can rub people the wrong way.
*Sakurai's example:* Brawl's Dojo updated five times a week in six languages. During Melee's brutal development Sakurai updated the site himself and replied directly to comments ([245](../episodes/notes/245-smash-bros-dojo.md)), and today he checks every official Twitter and website update ([80](../episodes/notes/080-marketing-is-multiplicative.md)). · Episodes: [245](../episodes/notes/245-smash-bros-dojo.md), [80](../episodes/notes/080-marketing-is-multiplicative.md), [123](../episodes/notes/123-daily-report-suggestions.md), [143](../episodes/notes/143-official-websites.md)

### MKT-11 · Keep one official home base, shaped around your game's content
**Why:** Social media delivers short bursts, but a game still needs one place that holds all its information, and a site made from a template won't fit every game. **Do:** Run one canonical site alongside a blog and official social accounts. Build its sections from your actual content (characters, modes, techniques, online play, stages, music, events) and add what the game needs. Have someone who knows the design review its layout and every update. **Watch out:** Information scattered across social posts with no home.
*Sakurai's example:* "Instead of just making a site in a standard fashion, you want to find a form that best fits your game." ([143](../episodes/notes/143-official-websites.md), 02:31). Smash Ultimate's site covers fighters, modes, 100+ stages, Pokémon and Assist Trophies, a built-in music player, reveals, a blog, tournaments and photo contests. · Episodes: [143](../episodes/notes/143-official-websites.md), [80](../episodes/notes/080-marketing-is-multiplicative.md)

### MKT-12 · Make storefront art strong enough to earn the click
**Why:** On storefronts with an almost endless lineup, people use a game's banner to decide whether to look at it at all. If the visuals don't draw them in, they won't play, and once a game is in stores the cover is crucial. **Do:** Treat banner, capsule and cover art as critical assets, test whether they make strangers want to click, and keep them honest to the game (MKT-02). **Watch out:** Assuming good gameplay will be found whatever the art looks like.
*Sakurai's example:* Steam's almost endless lineup, where players judge games by their banners ([152](../episodes/notes/152-do-graphics-impact-fun.md)). · Episodes: [152](../episodes/notes/152-do-graphics-impact-fun.md), [101](../episodes/notes/101-paint-an-accurate-picture-of-your-game.md)

### MKT-13 · Triage post-launch work: fatal bugs first, the rest is a bonus
**Why:** Crashing bugs must be fixed. Balance tweaks and quality-of-life features are a free bonus that ties up the director and key staff who could be starting the next game (games-as-a-service may differ). Every fix can create new bugs, core-system changes force wide retesting, and DLC stretches the schedule. **Do:** Rank post-launch work by severity. Plan people for issues that slip past master-up. Prefer narrow, contained fixes, re-run broad checks before each patch, use online and tournament data for balance, and budget DLC as a real schedule extension. **Watch out:** Rushed hotfixes, late rewrites of core AI or difficulty, and treating updates as free.
*Sakurai's example:* When Smash Ultimate's CPU fighters felt too hard at launch, the patch lowered Challenger fights and added a Very Easy option to World of Light instead of touching core CPU code ([141](../episodes/notes/141-unexpected-results.md)). Smash for 3DS/Wii U released in 2014, but DLC kept development going until spring 2016 ([135](../episodes/notes/135-super-smash-bros-for-3ds-wii-u.md)). · Episodes: [231](../episodes/notes/231-online-updates.md), [141](../episodes/notes/141-unexpected-results.md), [136](../episodes/notes/136-bug-testing-never-ends.md), [135](../episodes/notes/135-super-smash-bros-for-3ds-wii-u.md), [160](../episodes/notes/160-super-smash-bros-ultimate.md), [220](../episodes/notes/220-play-testing-keeps-going.md), [87](../episodes/notes/087-mastering-up.md)

### MKT-14 · Budget a demo as its own small game, and release it only when balanced
**Why:** Demos are very effective at getting people to try a game, but a demo is far more work than it looks: two builds to manage, update, tune and QA, spoiler data to strip, and stripping data breaks things. A half-baked demo invites people to write the game off. **Do:** Decide early and schedule the demo as its own deliverable with near-final QA. Keep spoiler content in separable files and check the build for leftovers. If the pre-release build isn't balanced, consider a post-launch demo made from the finished game, and accept the smaller splash. **Watch out:** Something-is-better-than-nothing demos.
*Sakurai's example:* Pre-COVID expo demos were somewhat easier to make because players couldn't keep them; downloadable demos may be more effective than ever. · Episodes: [122](../episodes/notes/122-game-demos.md), [101](../episodes/notes/101-paint-an-accurate-picture-of-your-game.md)

### MKT-15 · Build assets and tools so they double as marketing material
**Why:** Character art may be used outside the game, trailers need models that hold up at higher resolution, and the best footage comes from re-shooting a moment from any angle. **Do:** Make key art and renders at a quality and format marketing can reuse. Keep touched-up model revisions for pre-rendered CG and build trailer sound from the game's own effects. Give the team replay and debug-camera tools (pause, rotate characters, zoom, change the angle). **Watch out:** Assets that only work in-game, forcing marketing to remake them or make do.
*Sakurai's example:* Fighter artwork made with outside use in mind ([80](../episodes/notes/080-marketing-is-multiplicative.md)); reveal gameplay recorded with debug and replay features to find the best angles ([163](../episodes/notes/163-fighter-reveal-videos.md)); the Screenshot of the Day captured with debug-mode controls ([195](../episodes/notes/195-debug-mode.md)). · Episodes: [80](../episodes/notes/080-marketing-is-multiplicative.md), [163](../episodes/notes/163-fighter-reveal-videos.md), [195](../episodes/notes/195-debug-mode.md)

### MKT-16 · Demo by playing: show every key feature in a close match
**Why:** Showing a game directly is probably the most effective form of presentation, you can't demo what you don't understand, and real stakes are what make live play thrilling. **Do:** Before a demo, list everything the footage must show (for a fighter: every special move, the super move and as many regular moves as possible) and keep matches relatively close. Learn the content, check the stage setup, and when playing live, keep the risk real. **Watch out:** One-sided matches, and demoing content you don't know.
*Sakurai's example:* He played every DLC fighter himself in Mr. Sakurai Presents ([207](../episodes/notes/207-playing-games-in-front-of-other-people.md)). At PRESS START 2012 he played a Kid Icarus: Uprising Air Battle live with the orchestra at Intensity 9.0 and zero defense, where a Game Over would have ended the performance ([227](../episodes/notes/227-gameplay-as-part-of-a-live-performance.md)). · Episodes: [207](../episodes/notes/207-playing-games-in-front-of-other-people.md), [227](../episodes/notes/227-gameplay-as-part-of-a-live-performance.md)

### MKT-17 · Present your game on its own terms, not as "X but with Y"
**Why:** If your game is that close to another, people may just play the original, and you invite copycat accusations. References also mean different things to different generations. Only a sequel in the same series should sell itself by pointing at other games. **Do:** Describe the core experience without naming another game and check that people can still picture it. Sum the game up in one line naming its audience or hook. Keep "it's like X" for informal talk with people you know share the reference. **Watch out:** Headlining with a comparison.
*Sakurai's example:* "Mario" can mean Super Mario Bros. on the NES, Mario Odyssey, or just the character ([161](../episodes/notes/161-avoiding-comparison.md)); each of his works summed up in one caption ([2](../episodes/notes/002-masahiro-sakurais-creative-works.md)). · Episodes: [161](../episodes/notes/161-avoiding-comparison.md), [2](../episodes/notes/002-masahiro-sakurais-creative-works.md), [72](../episodes/notes/072-competing-with-the-past.md)

### MKT-18 · Make a sequel's reveal prove it beats its predecessor
**Why:** A sequel that repeats the original can expect roughly half the sales. People buy a sequel based on how well the last one was made and whether the new one looks clearly more fun and impactful; if it's the same scale with similar content, fans feel they've already played it. **Do:** Write in one line how the sequel is more fun and impactful than the last game, and make the reveal show exactly that. If returning players would think they've seen it all, expand the system or content first. **Watch out:** Counting on the series name to sell it.
*Sakurai's example:* Smash Ultimate's headline concept that every fighter returns, possible because the team and tools carried over ([160](../episodes/notes/160-super-smash-bros-ultimate.md)); Melee's concept of powering up everything ([40](../episodes/notes/040-super-smash-bros-melee.md)). · Episodes: [164](../episodes/notes/164-making-sequels.md), [160](../episodes/notes/160-super-smash-bros-ultimate.md), [40](../episodes/notes/040-super-smash-bros-melee.md)

### MKT-19 · Hear fans through deliberate channels, but keep the plan yours
**Why:** Persistent requests show what an audience wants, and a well-run survey can inform big decisions. Replying to fans built a relationship before social media, though risk management leaves less freedom for that today. A plan fixed in advance can't change course on comments, and off-topic or mocking comments mostly mean the point didn't land. **Do:** Gather wishes with structured tools at planning time (a survey that discounts repeat votes). Answer persistent requests with dedicated content. Decide which parts of the plan are open to input and which are fixed, and read reactions with echo chambers in mind (CRAFT-12). **Watch out:** Letting comment threads steer the plan, or ignoring a request your audience keeps repeating.
*Sakurai's example:* A past survey with about 1.8 million valid votes informed Ultimate's new fighters ([160](../episodes/notes/160-super-smash-bros-ultimate.md)); his followers' requests for his cat got their own video ([39](../episodes/notes/039-my-feline-friend-fukurashi.md)); with every channel script written in advance, he couldn't take requests ([261](../episodes/notes/261-masahiro-sakurai-on-creating-games-finale-special.md)). · Episodes: [160](../episodes/notes/160-super-smash-bros-ultimate.md), [39](../episodes/notes/039-my-feline-friend-fukurashi.md), [245](../episodes/notes/245-smash-bros-dojo.md), [261](../episodes/notes/261-masahiro-sakurai-on-creating-games-finale-special.md), [228](../episodes/notes/228-echo-chambers.md)

### MKT-20 · Plan from the start to reach a global audience in their languages
**Why:** Reaching players worldwide at the same time takes coordination with regional partners, and it multiplies the audience for every update. Text written ahead of time also serves subtitles, translation and checks. **Do:** If you want a global audience, plan localized updates and videos from day one, line up regional partners, and write copy and scripts early so translators work from final text. **Watch out:** Treating localization of marketing as an afterthought.
*Sakurai's example:* Brawl's Dojo ran in Japanese, English, Spanish, French, German and Italian with help from Nintendo of America and Nintendo of Europe ([245](../episodes/notes/245-smash-bros-dojo.md)); he planned the channel as Japanese-only, then added English to reach as many people as possible ([261](../episodes/notes/261-masahiro-sakurai-on-creating-games-finale-special.md)). · Episodes: [245](../episodes/notes/245-smash-bros-dojo.md), [261](../episodes/notes/261-masahiro-sakurai-on-creating-games-finale-special.md)

### MKT-21 · Don't lock expensive movies behind progress; they'll be shared anyway
**Why:** People post in-game movies online before a game is even out, so movies meant as rewards for progress stop being rewards. A showcase that isn't tied to progress can be enjoyed by everyone and works as marketing. **Do:** Assume every in-game movie will be uploaded at or before launch. Turn showcase cinematics into marketing, and reward progression with things that keep their value after someone uploads them. **Watch out:** Expensive cutscenes used as the payoff for slogging through a mode.
*Sakurai's example:* Brawl's per-fighter movies in Subspace Emissary were posted online from pre-release copies, so he stopped making movies that cut in during gameplay and made 37 fighter reveals instead ([163](../episodes/notes/163-fighter-reveal-videos.md)). · Episodes: [163](../episodes/notes/163-fighter-reveal-videos.md) · See also: PLAN-20

### MKT-22 · Pick a title that shortens into something memorable
**Why:** Players will shorten your title, and a name that shortens well may stick in memory more easily. A title's words also carry nuance about the game's tone. **Do:** List the short forms players are likely to use and favor names that shorten well; choose words for the tone they imply. **Watch out:** A name that shortens to something forgettable, or carries the wrong tone.
*Sakurai's example:* Super Smash Bros. becomes just "Smash" ([101](../episodes/notes/101-paint-an-accurate-picture-of-your-game.md)); Mr. Iwata picked "Brothers" so the fighting reads as friends settling a little disagreement ([259](../episodes/notes/259-mr-iwata.md)). · Episodes: [101](../episodes/notes/101-paint-an-accurate-picture-of-your-game.md), [259](../episodes/notes/259-mr-iwata.md)

## Common mistakes

- Assuming a good game will be discovered on its own, or leaving marketing until the end.
- Trailers that open with logos, long CG and character intros, or store pages with no gameplay at all.
- Teaser-only reveals for a new IP or an indie game.
- Box art, banners or trailers that promise a different game from the one in the box.
- Settling for adequate trailer framing; adding focus pulls that make big things look small.
- Leaving explanation of a novel game to the press and social chatter.
- Irregular, unreviewed official posts, or announcements flooding the director's personal feed.
- Generic template sites, or information scattered with no home base.
- Choosing an audience by personal taste or by rigid age and gender slices.
- Building the concept from whatever genre research says is selling.
- Measuring yourself only against this year's releases; advertising a giant hour count.
- Shipping rough because patches will fix it; rushed hotfixes that create new bugs; DLC planned as if it were free.
- Treating a demo as a quick slice, leaving spoiler data in it, or releasing it unbalanced.
- Locking expensive cutscenes behind progress when they'll be uploaded before launch.
- Pitching the game as "X but with Y."
- Letting comment threads steer a set plan, or reading only the voices that agree.

## Review questions

- Would a stranger know what kind of game this is within the first seconds of the trailer?
- Does every piece of marketing (title, cover, banner, screenshots, trailer, demo) match how the game actually plays?
- Who exactly is this for, and what else competes for their time?
- Does the dev team own a plan to make this game known, and is it in the schedule?
- Would we ship this build if no patch were ever possible?
- When people misunderstand the game, where do they find the designer's own explanation?
- Is every showcase shot as bold as it can be?
- How do fans' voices reach the team, and which parts of the plan are open to them?

## Exercises

1. **Seconds-to-gameplay audit (1 hour).** Play your current trailer and note the timestamp of the first real gameplay. Recut a version where gameplay, or footage that explains your systems, comes first and story and logos come later. Show both cuts to someone who has never seen the game and ask what kind of game it is. (MKT-03)
2. **Expectation-match test (2 hours).** Show your store page (banner, screenshots, trailer, description) to three people who haven't played. Ask each to describe the game they expect, then have them play for 15 minutes and ask what was different. Fix the biggest mismatch. (MKT-02, MKT-12)
3. **Five-check shot pass (half a day).** Take the three weakest shots in your trailer and redo each one: zoom in and go bolder, change the angle of view, add an interaction, balance a serious beat with a comic one, cut it to the music. (MKT-08)
4. **Write your own "Secrets of" guide (2–3 hours).** In plain language, write one page on how your game plays, its core essence and where its push and pull lies. Post it where players can find it, and plan the next four updates on a fixed weekday schedule. (MKT-09, MKT-10)
5. **Audience and competition sheet (1 hour).** Write your target audience by experience level and need, not age or gender. List ten things that player could spend the same hours on (new games, older games, subscription titles, their phone) and one honest reason they'd choose your game over each. (MKT-05, MKT-07)
6. **Patch triage board (1 hour).** List every known issue and planned update. Mark each as fatal (must fix) or bonus. For each bonus, write who it would tie up, and for each fatal fix, the narrowest change that solves it. (MKT-04, MKT-13)
