# 03 · Balance and Difficulty

> For Sakurai, balancing starts with a question about people: who is this game for? Risk and reward scale together, so every notch of danger you add makes the game more thrilling for some players and shuts out others. He made the first Kirby for newcomers and slowed Brawl for casual players, but aimed Kid Icarus: Uprising at hardcore ones. Whatever the target, the same duties follow. Be kind to beginners. Never let a player get permanently stuck. Get players back into control seconds after they fail. Never treat the dev team's skill as the standard. Between characters and options, keep them distinct instead of sanding them down to average. And make deliberate, stated choices about luck, catch-up and handicaps, so that losing feels like the player's own doing. Balance is how the risk and reward from chapter 02 get fitted to a real audience, and getting it wrong breaks fairness and wastes the time players give you.

## The big ideas

**Difficulty is a dial you set for a specific person.** "As you increase 'game essence,' you reduce a game's broad appeal." [6](../episodes/notes/006-risk-and-reward.md) Sakurai's clearest comparison is Super Mario Bros. against Kirby's Dream Land [148](../episodes/notes/148-more-game-essence-means-less-broad-appeal.md). Mario's heart is getting right next to enemies and turning that extreme risk into a counterattack. Kirby can inhale an enemy while the risk is still low, take up to six hits, spit an attack that outranges the inhale, and fly over pits whenever you like. Shrink the inhale, add one-hit deaths, take away flight or add inertia, and you'd get more push and pull but a narrower audience. "Risk and reward, when balanced correctly, should scale in almost perfect proportion," so Dream Land really is less thrilling than Mario. Yet it does exactly what it set out to do, because a risk that seems minor to an experienced player can be a wall to a newcomer. "We should be kind to beginners." So pick the target first, by experience level rather than age [226](../episodes/notes/226-targeting-and-market-research.md), then set the dials: hitbox size, health, escape tools, inertia, game speed [6](../episodes/notes/006-risk-and-reward.md) [108](../episodes/notes/108-super-smash-bros-brawl.md).

**The developer is the worst judge of difficulty, and the player's patience is the scarcest resource.** Your team knows the game inside out, so tune while handicapped (Sakurai plays one-handed), then make it easier still: "make the game even easier than you think it should be, and it'll be just right." [256](../episodes/notes/256-give-yourself-a-handicap-when-balancing-your-game.md) Players fail anyway, and what happens next decides whether they stay. After their "Ugh, I blew it!" moment, play a punchy failure effect of about a second and hand back control [117](../episodes/notes/117-make-retries-quick.md). Let them change difficulty at any time, and after repeated failure offer an easier setting or a skip, worded so it doesn't humiliate. "No matter how fun tense challenges may be, the moment players give up, it's all over." [213](../episodes/notes/213-better-than-not-being-able-to-beat-the-game.md) His favorite example of difficulty done kindly is Kid Icarus: Uprising's Fiend's Cauldron. Players bet currency on a higher Intensity for bigger rewards, and the game quietly lowers the difficulty after each failure instead of asking whether they'd like Easy mode [41](../episodes/notes/041-the-fiends-cauldron.md).

**Balance between options means keeping their peaks and valleys.** When the Smash team found a fighter too strong, the obvious fix was to shave down its strength and patch its weaknesses to compensate. Sakurai says that usually leads to ruin: fighters start to feel like the same character and the game turns bland [247](../episodes/notes/247-amplify-both-strengths-and-weaknesses.md). His instruction is to amplify shortcomings to offset advantages [218](../episodes/notes/218-average-and-mediocre-are-the-same-thing.md), and when a move must be nerfed, to touch a secondary parameter (a longer vulnerable follow-through, a smaller hitbox) and leave its defining power alone. "When there are lots of possible actions or moves, don't make the balance mediocre! Keep your peaks and valleys!" [247](../episodes/notes/247-amplify-both-strengths-and-weaknesses.md) Distinctness and fairness can coexist. Smash Ultimate's 87 fighters fell between 47.18% and 51.43% in global online win rates. Even so, he treats feel, not the numbers, as the final measure [218](../episodes/notes/218-average-and-mediocre-are-the-same-thing.md).

**Fairness is a choice you make out loud.** How much should the game help the player who is behind? "Games where the person with the most skill keeps winning every single time just aren't fun," so casual games need catch-up systems like Mario Kart's [202](../episodes/notes/202-handicap-systems.md). Smash wanted results to reflect skill, so it gives losing players no stat buffs and lets items, stages and Final Smashes stir things up instead. Luck can spice up repeated play, but no loss should come down to luck alone, because players need to feel responsible for their own failures [189](../episodes/notes/189-randomness-spices-games-up.md). CPU opponents should react like humans and attack like players, not defend like machines [221](../episodes/notes/221-computer-controlled-players.md). For mismatched friends there are per-player handicaps, asymmetric fail conditions, and the most effective tool of all: easing off by hand [157](../episodes/notes/157-special-crossover-3.md) [202](../episodes/notes/202-handicap-systems.md).

## Principles

### BAL-01 · Pick the target player first, then set how much risk to allow
**Why:** More game essence means less broad appeal, and who you target should follow from the game's design and concept. There's no one right answer, only a deliberate one. **Do:** Write down the target player by experience (beginners span every age and gender), decide how much push and pull they want, and set the risk dials to match. If you go hardcore, make it a stated goal. **Watch out:** Tuning to your own skill, assuming more depth is always better, and defining the audience by age or gender.
*Sakurai's example:* Kirby's Dream Land was made deliberately simpler than Mario. Melee's speed was hard on newcomers, so Brawl was slowed for casual Wii players. Kid Icarus: Uprising went hardcore because casual players were already served on the DS. · Episodes: [4](../episodes/notes/004-kirbys-dream-land.md), [6](../episodes/notes/006-risk-and-reward.md), [41](../episodes/notes/041-the-fiends-cauldron.md), [108](../episodes/notes/108-super-smash-bros-brawl.md), [125](../episodes/notes/125-kid-icarus-uprising.md), [148](../episodes/notes/148-more-game-essence-means-less-broad-appeal.md), [213](../episodes/notes/213-better-than-not-being-able-to-beat-the-game.md), [226](../episodes/notes/226-targeting-and-market-research.md)

### BAL-02 · Be kind to beginners: neutralize threats early and forgive small slips
**Why:** A risk that's minor to you can stop a newcomer cold, and getting people to play at all is hard enough. Being hit or missing a jump is a small mistake, so losing a life for it is too harsh. **Do:** Let beginners stop threats while risk is still low, give them a forgiving health pool and an escape from the steepest penalties, make bosses big enough that near-misses still connect, and give first-timers a window where death costs little. Keep a real penalty for real failure. In versus play, let newcomers have fun before they're flattened. **Watch out:** One-hit deaths, heavy inertia, or wiping every power-up on death in a game meant for newcomers.
*Sakurai's example:* Kirby's flight exists because losing a life for a missed jump felt too harsh (falling still costs a life), and Whispy Woods is huge so off-target attacks still hit. Space Harrier's *trial time* let arcade players die freely for a set period. · Episodes: [4](../episodes/notes/004-kirbys-dream-land.md), [100](../episodes/notes/100-development-secrets-of-the-original-kirby.md), [148](../episodes/notes/148-more-game-essence-means-less-broad-appeal.md), [156](../episodes/notes/156-special-crossover-2.md), [157](../episodes/notes/157-special-crossover-3.md), [242](../episodes/notes/242-being-kind-to-beginners.md)

### BAL-03 · Never let a player get permanently stuck
**Why:** Once players give up, the challenge is worth nothing. In RPGs you can grind past a wall, but in action games getting stronger isn't enough, and everyone has a breaking point. A game that forces a restart because difficulty can't be changed mid-game has a fundamental design flaw. **Do:** Let players change difficulty at any time without losing progress. After repeated failure, offer an easier setting or a skip that counts as cleared. Provide a near-guaranteed floor, and explicitly teach newcomers how to turn difficulty down. If the shipped game proves too hard, patch in an easier option. **Watch out:** An impossible spike after dozens of hours, and an easy end of the difficulty system that nobody explains.
*Sakurai's example:* Uprising's Intensity 0.0 is nearly invincible, but players weren't told clearly enough that going below the default 2.0 lowers difficulty. Smash Ultimate's patch eased Challenger fights and added Very Easy to World of Light. · Episodes: [41](../episodes/notes/041-the-fiends-cauldron.md), [55](../episodes/notes/055-modern-quality-of-life-features.md), [141](../episodes/notes/141-unexpected-results.md), [213](../episodes/notes/213-better-than-not-being-able-to-beat-the-game.md)

### BAL-04 · Don't tune difficulty to your team; handicap yourself, then go easier
**Why:** If your best player can barely squeak by, you've proven only that the game can be beaten by people who know it inside out. Even balancing for the median team member leaves it hard, and players won't spend all their time on your game. **Do:** Run difficulty passes one-handed, with lower health or stricter conditions. Set difficulty well below what feels right to the team, then lower it once more. Use outside testers from the very start, and treat each as fresh only once. **Watch out:** Concluding that players will persist because other hard games are popular, or because you can scrape by yourself.
*Sakurai's example:* His own one-handed tuning; elementary-school play testers who came with friends and snacks and reacted honestly. · Episodes: [128](../episodes/notes/128-is-your-game-appealing-enough-to-start-over.md), [148](../episodes/notes/148-more-game-essence-means-less-broad-appeal.md), [248](../episodes/notes/248-elementary-school-play-testers.md), [256](../episodes/notes/256-give-yourself-a-handicap-when-balancing-your-game.md)

### BAL-05 · Get players back in control within seconds of failing
**Why:** Interest cools fast in the gap between "Ugh, I blew it!" and regaining control, and quick retries raise players' tolerance for difficulty. Watching the game move on its own doesn't count; they need to be in control. **Do:** Measure death-to-control time and cut it: a flashy, punchy failure effect of about a second, short loads, button-advanced text. Respawn players where enemies can't hit them immediately. **Watch out:** Going so far that respawning right where you died, forever, makes the game feel mindless.
*Sakurai's example:* He states it as a general rule; in competitive FPS games, quick restarts leave no time to feel upset. · Episodes: [97](../episodes/notes/097-dying-comes-as-a-relief.md), [117](../episodes/notes/117-make-retries-quick.md)

### BAL-06 · Keep every option's peaks and valleys; never average them out
**Why:** Balancing purely on strength trims advantages and patches weaknesses until everything is average, and average is mediocre. Without individuality there's no point to a big roster. Divided opinions about which option is best are a sign of health, and players should settle the argument in a match. **Do:** When something is too strong, exaggerate its weaknesses before cutting its signature strength, and don't soften its weaknesses in the same patch. Reject changes that make an option more average. Push interchangeable options apart, including the moves in a single-player game. **Watch out:** Backing away from distinctive designs because opinions snowball on social media.
*Sakurai's example:* Mario vs. Mario has no balance problems but limited push and pull. Smash fighters differ in movement speed, vulnerability and animation flair. · Episodes: [19](../episodes/notes/019-giving-weight-to-buttons.md), [218](../episodes/notes/218-average-and-mediocre-are-the-same-thing.md), [220](../episodes/notes/220-play-testing-keeps-going.md), [247](../episodes/notes/247-amplify-both-strengths-and-weaknesses.md)

### BAL-07 · Fix balance with the narrowest change that keeps the signature intact
**Why:** One attack has a dozen components (power, speed, duration, windup, follow-through, range, hit size, animation speed, knockback, combo ability, shield damage, shield recovery), so it can be weakened without erasing its character. Changing a mechanic's context often works better than removing it, and late patches to core systems force wide re-testing. **Do:** Mark the defining parameter and adjust a secondary one. For an overpowered ability, shrink the play space, confine key fights or slow it down before cutting it. After launch, ease specific encounters or add an easier option rather than rewriting core AI. **Watch out:** Flattening the stat players love about an option.
*Sakurai's example:* On the NES, Kirby's flight could bypass nearly anything, so he enlarged the info panel, kept mid-boss fights to one screen and slowed flight instead of removing it. · Episodes: [11](../episodes/notes/011-kirbys-adventure.md), [77](../episodes/notes/077-follow-throughs-make-the-impact.md), [141](../episodes/notes/141-unexpected-results.md), [247](../episodes/notes/247-amplify-both-strengths-and-weaknesses.md)

### BAL-08 · Scale every penalty and reward to how good or bad the action was
**Why:** Players are happiest when they're compensated correctly. A big penalty for a truly bad choice can be fun, but ask whether the player can ever redeem themselves, and at what cost. Failure should still cost something, or retries feel mindless. **Do:** For each reward, penalty and damage value, ask how good or bad the triggering action really was. Give irreversible choices an explicit way back. Check that a death which costs players their build still leaves them a realistic recovery. **Watch out:** Rationalizing every consequence until the game becomes predictable. Polish out only what feels unreasonable.
*Sakurai's example:* Gradius resetting every power-up on death makes players want to quit; story departures that take a party member and the gear invested in them upset party balance. · Episodes: [4](../episodes/notes/004-kirbys-dream-land.md), [113](../episodes/notes/113-game-writing-is-unique.md), [117](../episodes/notes/117-make-retries-quick.md), [156](../episodes/notes/156-special-crossover-2.md), [238](../episodes/notes/238-how-good-were-the-players-actions.md)

### BAL-09 · Shape difficulty like a mountain trail, not a steady ramp
**Why:** If player and enemy strength rise in lockstep, progress is a constant incline, and that's just work. Real climbs have peaks to overcome and flats to breeze through. Enemies that scale with the player remove any reason to get ahead. **Do:** Plot player power against enemy difficulty and reshape it into peaks (bosses that demand a strategy and pay off) and flats (empty stretches, spots to rack up gains). Smooth out weak growth stages that would become walls. Playtest the endgame for spikes. **Watch out:** Rest stops at fixed intervals as the only highlights.
*Sakurai's example:* His mountain-climbing analogy; Mushiking's growth stages checked so none hit a wall in battle. · Episodes: [92](../episodes/notes/092-sodatete-kouchuu-ouja-mushiking.md), [105](../episodes/notes/105-mountain-climbing-is-more-than-just-climbing.md), [213](../episodes/notes/213-better-than-not-being-able-to-beat-the-game.md)

### BAL-10 · If failure means repetition, give players a reason to push on
**Why:** Most players won't enjoy repeating the same content. They keep going because they want to see what's next, and a game that copies hard-game systems doesn't inherit the trust that keeps players pushing in the originals. **Do:** For each likely failure point, list what players replay to get back, then shorten or vary it (randomized equipment, items, events), and hint at what's coming next. **Watch out:** Low-variety stretches that stuck players will see over and over.
*Sakurai's example:* Roguelites that make each run after a defeat play differently; Dark Souls players pushing on to reach what comes next. · Episodes: [117](../episodes/notes/117-make-retries-quick.md), [128](../episodes/notes/128-is-your-game-appealing-enough-to-start-over.md), [189](../episodes/notes/189-randomness-spices-games-up.md)

### BAL-11 · Layer optional depth over a simple core both audiences can use
**Why:** When an audience mixes beginners and veterans, give each something. Making a hard input essential splits players into those who can and can't do it, and shortcuts only experts use narrow a game's appeal. **Do:** Build a minimal core that can finish the game or compete, put optional depth on top, and verify a core-only playthrough. Offer a one-button version of special inputs, with a modest bonus for the full command. **Watch out:** Requiring the depth system to progress or to compete.
*Sakurai's example:* Kirby's Adventure can be finished with inhale-and-spit alone, while copy abilities reward veterans. Smash's Ryu fires his shoryuken with one button or the full command, which earns more power and invincibility. · Episodes: [11](../episodes/notes/011-kirbys-adventure.md), [27](../episodes/notes/027-super-smash-bros.md), [159](../episodes/notes/159-the-shoryuken-command.md), [169](../episodes/notes/169-i-want-to-choose-fast.md), [226](../episodes/notes/226-targeting-and-market-research.md)

### BAL-12 · Size complexity to your audience and lifespan; hold back what they can't feel
**Why:** You can always add rules to boost push and pull, but overcomplicated rules are hard to grasp. A game built for hundreds of matches still needs real complexity, yet moves too hard to perform hide the push and pull. **Do:** Decide whether the game is for casual sessions or long competitive life and size its depth to match. Before adding a rule, check your target players could still grasp it. Lower speed and input density for casual players, simpler controllers or laggy online play. **Watch out:** Constant risk-and-reward pressure that makes the whole game feel too hardcore.
*Sakurai's example:* The original Street Fighter's moves were too hard to feel, and Street Fighter II fixed that. Uprising's land stages were too big and too crowded, with no respawning where you died. · Episodes: [15](../episodes/notes/015-game-essence-in-role-playing-games.md), [41](../episodes/notes/041-the-fiends-cauldron.md), [82](../episodes/notes/082-do-competitive-games-have-to-be-complex.md), [108](../episodes/notes/108-super-smash-bros-brawl.md), [148](../episodes/notes/148-more-game-essence-means-less-broad-appeal.md)

### BAL-13 · Tune risk through explicit dials, not a vague difficulty knob
**Why:** Concrete parameters set how risky play is. Space Invaders' cannon width is key to game balance because it decides how much risk you take, so it's more than visual design. **Do:** List your dials (player hitbox size, partial cover, threat-neutralizing range, hits the player can take, escape tools, control inertia, game speed, target size, enemy durability, how freely players can leave) and set each on purpose. When you loosen risk in one layer, decide whether to tighten another. **Watch out:** Treating size and speed as purely art or feel decisions.
*Sakurai's example:* Casual RPGs that let you leave dungeons at will make individual battles harder. Kirby Super Star made standard enemies survive a few hits so stronger moves stood out. · Episodes: [4](../episodes/notes/004-kirbys-dream-land.md), [6](../episodes/notes/006-risk-and-reward.md), [15](../episodes/notes/015-game-essence-in-role-playing-games.md), [20](../episodes/notes/020-kirby-super-star.md), [100](../episodes/notes/100-development-secrets-of-the-original-kirby.md), [148](../episodes/notes/148-more-game-essence-means-less-broad-appeal.md)

### BAL-14 · Decide on purpose how much your game evens out skill
**Why:** Competitive games are most fun when it's anyone's match, but how much to level the field depends on the game. A casual game needs catch-up systems even though they aren't quite fair. A skill game should keep its core stats honest. **Do:** For casual play, give trailing players boosts or stronger items. For skill-reflecting play, route catch-up through chaos: make the comeback item harder for the leader to get, charge comeback meters faster for losers, and keep stat-changing systems in party modes. For mainly local play, tolerate quirks that friends can house-rule. **Watch out:** Raising attack or reducing knockback for losing players in a game meant to reflect skill.
*Sakurai's example:* Mario Kart's boosts and items vs. Smash, where only the Smash Ball and the Final Smash Meter favor the losing player. Smash for 3DS/Wii U's variable character strength powered Smash Run and amiibo, not serious matches. · Episodes: [135](../episodes/notes/135-super-smash-bros-for-3ds-wii-u.md), [189](../episodes/notes/189-randomness-spices-games-up.md), [202](../episodes/notes/202-handicap-systems.md)

### BAL-15 · Never let luck alone decide a player's loss
**Why:** Losing only to bad luck is hard to accept and wastes hard work. Random dodge chances can feel unfair, and players already suspect computers of cheating with lucky rolls. Randomness earns its place when it adds variety, demands skill, or gives anyone a shot at a comeback. **Do:** Let players respond to or control random events, keep randomness out of strict skill contests, prototype strategy combat without random evasion first, and seed random numbers with something unpredictable like the system clock so resets can't force outcomes. **Watch out:** A luck stat as the fix, since players forget it or won't invest in it.
*Sakurai's example:* Peach's Smash Attack, once random, is now chosen with up or down; the Hero's random command list stays because each command still takes skill. · Episodes: [120](../episodes/notes/120-the-fun-in-picking-sides.md), [189](../episodes/notes/189-randomness-spices-games-up.md), [221](../episodes/notes/221-computer-controlled-players.md), [230](../episodes/notes/230-game-essence-in-strategy-games.md)

### BAL-16 · Let difficulty be a choice with stakes, not a humiliation
**Why:** Asking a failing player whether they'd like Easy mode stings, and playing the whole game on Easy feels unsatisfying. Difficulty works better as a bet, or as a lever inside the world. **Do:** Let players wager currency on higher difficulty for proportional rewards, and pay to lower it. On failure, ease off automatically and take part of the stake. Give in-world levers that push difficulty back, and pay fewer rewards when players choose to overpower content. Put assists behind an unlock step so nobody flips them by accident. **Watch out:** A system that never rewards strong play.
*Sakurai's example:* The Fiend's Cauldron; Xevious, where destroying the radar-station enemy rewinds the enemy table; World of Light's Spirits; The Last of Us Part I's locked combat assists. · Episodes: [41](../episodes/notes/041-the-fiends-cauldron.md), [55](../episodes/notes/055-modern-quality-of-life-features.md), [156](../episodes/notes/156-special-crossover-2.md), [160](../episodes/notes/160-super-smash-bros-ultimate.md), [213](../episodes/notes/213-better-than-not-being-able-to-beat-the-game.md)

### BAL-17 · Check balance with testers and data, but let feel decide
**Why:** Nobody is better at an in-development game than its play testers, and real match data shows whether every option has a shot. Win rates aren't everything, though, and identical setups should come out even or the game isn't fair. **Do:** Staff dedicated testers, use online and tournament results where you have them, run mirror matches to catch built-in advantages, and check multiplayer as well as top-level usage. Make the final call on feel. **Watch out:** A director entering every parameter alone on top of directing, and judging fairness from one slice of play.
*Sakurai's example:* Smash Ultimate's fighters all sit between 47.18% and 51.43% win rate; Brawl had four testers and no online data. · Episodes: [135](../episodes/notes/135-super-smash-bros-for-3ds-wii-u.md), [218](../episodes/notes/218-average-and-mediocre-are-the-same-thing.md), [220](../episodes/notes/220-play-testing-keeps-going.md), [230](../episodes/notes/230-game-essence-in-strategy-games.md), [247](../episodes/notes/247-amplify-both-strengths-and-weaknesses.md)

### BAL-18 · Make CPU opponents human-paced, aggressive and beatable
**Why:** CPUs that react faster than a human feel extremely unfair. Players who pick a high CPU level want a strong opponent, not one that waits around or perfect-shields everything. **Do:** Add a reaction delay, let effects like reversed controls confuse CPUs briefly before they adapt, bias high levels toward offense, cap perfect defense, and test for contradictions between behavior modes. **Watch out:** CPUs that could be made invincible, which would be no fun at all.
*Sakurai's example:* Smash Ultimate's CPUs have a "reflex" and were tuned more aggressively than before. · Episodes: [221](../episodes/notes/221-computer-controlled-players.md)

### BAL-19 · Show rank so most players feel they are succeeding
**Why:** Leaderboards give purpose, but a raw position tells nearly everyone they're losing, and seeing the gap to the top makes people give up. Matchmaking also has to balance finding a match against fair skill gaps. **Do:** Use a number that rises with skill, measured against the whole player base. Hide the world's #1 from general players, give top players an elite tier of their own, and widen matchmaking range by region and hour only as far as needed. **Watch out:** Telling a player in a huge player base that they rank somewhere in the millions.
*Sakurai's example:* Smash Ultimate's Global Smash Power and Elite Smash. · Episodes: [170](../episodes/notes/170-the-pros-and-cons-of-leaderboards.md)

### BAL-20 · Re-check difficulty after late performance changes, on real hardware
**Why:** When game speed is tied to processing speed, an optimization can quietly make the game harder, and dev boards don't always run like retail units. **Do:** Find timings tied to processing speed, freeze them before final tuning, and replay difficulty-critical content after each optimization and on production hardware. Take it seriously when several people independently say something feels too hard. **Watch out:** Assuming smoother performance is purely a win.
*Sakurai's example:* Meteos got harder than he tuned it, Mushiking's pet got hungry faster on the retail unit, and Smash Ultimate's CPUs felt too strong at launch. · Episodes: [74](../episodes/notes/074-meteos.md), [92](../episodes/notes/092-sodatete-kouchuu-ouja-mushiking.md), [141](../episodes/notes/141-unexpected-results.md)

### BAL-21 · Check that co-op doesn't make the game harder than solo
**Why:** A second player can make a game harder when resources and camera were tuned for one. **Do:** Playtest co-op for power-ups budgeted for one player, players blocking each other, and a camera or scroll point that strands the player behind. Consider a lead player the camera follows, with partners who warp back. **Watch out:** Dropping a second player into single-player tuning unchanged.
*Sakurai's example:* Two-player Darius (ships in each other's way, power-ups for one) and arcade Contra's forward scroll point; Kirby Super Star's Helper system. · Episodes: [20](../episodes/notes/020-kirby-super-star.md), [157](../episodes/notes/157-special-crossover-3.md)

### BAL-22 · Give mismatched players handicap tools, and use them yourself
**Why:** The skill gap between players can be painfully large. Settings can close it, asymmetric rules can turn a mismatch into shared fun, and simply easing off may be the most effective handicap there is. **Do:** Expose difficulty and resources as per-player settings, offer asymmetric fail conditions and an automatic handicap option, and go easy on newcomers unless they want you to go all out. **Watch out:** Framing every mismatched session as a winner-and-loser contest.
*Sakurai's example:* In The Genji and the Heike Clans he took the hardest difficulty and fewest candles while Arino Kacho took the easiest and most; Sakurai crushing a couple who'd only come to try The King of Fighters '95. · Episodes: [155](../episodes/notes/155-special-crossover-1.md), [157](../episodes/notes/157-special-crossover-3.md), [202](../episodes/notes/202-handicap-systems.md), [242](../episodes/notes/242-being-kind-to-beginners.md)

## When the advice pulls in two directions

- **Kindness vs. challenge.** Most of these episodes push toward easier: be kind to beginners [148](../episodes/notes/148-more-game-essence-means-less-broad-appeal.md), make it easier than you think [256](../episodes/notes/256-give-yourself-a-handicap-when-balancing-your-game.md), never let anyone get stuck [213](../episodes/notes/213-better-than-not-being-able-to-beat-the-game.md). But Sakurai also says abandoning kindness for intense battles is a valid aim, since some pleasures only come from it [213](../episodes/notes/213-better-than-not-being-able-to-beat-the-game.md). He also says there's real joy in persevering through hard challenges [256](../episodes/notes/256-give-yourself-a-handicap-when-balancing-your-game.md), and he aimed Kid Icarus: Uprising at hardcore players [125](../episodes/notes/125-kid-icarus-uprising.md). The resolution is that hardcore must be an explicit choice for an explicit audience, and even then the game should leave a way forward for players who stall (the Cauldron goes down to 0.0) [41](../episodes/notes/041-the-fiends-cauldron.md).
- **Fast retries vs. meaningful failure.** Retries should be nearly instant [117](../episodes/notes/117-make-retries-quick.md), yet infinite respawns where you died feel mindless, and the loss should be felt [117](../episodes/notes/117-make-retries-quick.md) [238](../episodes/notes/238-how-good-were-the-players-actions.md). Keep the downtime short and the cost real, scaled to how bad the mistake was.
- **Adjustable difficulty vs. rewarding strong play.** Sakurai agrees that modern games should let you change difficulty freely, but wishes strong play could still be rewarded [41](../episodes/notes/041-the-fiends-cauldron.md). Wagered difficulty and fewer rewards for overpowered play [160](../episodes/notes/160-super-smash-bros-ultimate.md) are his ways to have both.
- **Distinctness vs. fairness.** He accepts that parts of the game can look unfair in isolation for the sake of individuality [220](../episodes/notes/220-play-testing-keeps-going.md), while also insisting identical setups must draw [230](../episodes/notes/230-game-essence-in-strategy-games.md). Start from a fair symmetric baseline, then build distinct options on top and check them with win-rate data [218](../episodes/notes/218-average-and-mediocre-are-the-same-thing.md).
- **Skill vs. chaos.** He calls catch-up systems a must for casual games, yet keeps Smash's core stats honest and leaves luck to items and stages [202](../episodes/notes/202-handicap-systems.md). He removes randomness from fighters where it decides outcomes, but keeps random items for comebacks [189](../episodes/notes/189-randomness-spices-games-up.md). Which side applies depends on whether your game promises that anyone can win or that the better player wins.
- **Depth for longevity vs. clarity.** Hundreds of matches need complexity [82](../episodes/notes/082-do-competitive-games-have-to-be-complex.md), but overcomplicated rules lose broad appeal [148](../episodes/notes/148-more-game-essence-means-less-broad-appeal.md). Add only rules that create push and pull your audience can actually feel.
- **Rewarding execution vs. staying casual.** From a game-essence standpoint Ryu's command shoryuken should have been even stronger, but making it essential would have split players, so Smash kept it modest [159](../episodes/notes/159-the-shoryuken-command.md).
- **Exploits vs. balance.** Debugging usually removes balance-breaking tricks, yet some within-the-rules exploits are fun and memorable and worth keeping [48](../episodes/notes/048-let-players-cheat-the-system.md) (chapter 02, ESS-20).

## Common mistakes

- Tuning difficulty to the best player on the team, or to your own ability to scrape by [128](../episodes/notes/128-is-your-game-appealing-enough-to-start-over.md) [256](../episodes/notes/256-give-yourself-a-handicap-when-balancing-your-game.md).
- Punishing small slips with the harshest penalty: instant death for a missed jump, all power-ups wiped [4](../episodes/notes/004-kirbys-dream-land.md) [156](../episodes/notes/156-special-crossover-2.md).
- Locking difficulty at the start, or putting an impossible spike at the end of a long game [213](../episodes/notes/213-better-than-not-being-able-to-beat-the-game.md).
- Bluntly asking failing players to switch to Easy, or leaving the easy end of the system unexplained [41](../episodes/notes/041-the-fiends-cauldron.md).
- Long death sequences and loads before a retry, or respawning players into enemy fire [117](../episodes/notes/117-make-retries-quick.md).
- Copying hard-game systems and assuming players will push on [128](../episodes/notes/128-is-your-game-appealing-enough-to-start-over.md).
- Shaving strengths and patching weaknesses until every option feels the same [218](../episodes/notes/218-average-and-mediocre-are-the-same-thing.md) [247](../episodes/notes/247-amplify-both-strengths-and-weaknesses.md).
- Cutting a signature mechanic outright, or rewriting core systems in a late patch, when a narrow change would do [11](../episodes/notes/011-kirbys-adventure.md) [141](../episodes/notes/141-unexpected-results.md).
- Keeping player and enemy strength in direct proportion, or scaling enemies to the player's level [105](../episodes/notes/105-mountain-climbing-is-more-than-just-climbing.md).
- Letting luck alone decide losses, or leaving random results that players can force by resetting [189](../episodes/notes/189-randomness-spices-games-up.md).
- Stat buffs for losing players in a game meant to reflect skill, or no catch-up at all in a casual party game [202](../episodes/notes/202-handicap-systems.md).
- CPUs with superhuman reactions or perfect defense [221](../episodes/notes/221-computer-controlled-players.md).
- Raw leaderboards that show most players a hopeless rank [170](../episodes/notes/170-the-pros-and-cons-of-leaderboards.md).
- Tuning on a dev kit and never replaying the final build on retail hardware [141](../episodes/notes/141-unexpected-results.md).

## Review questions

1. Who is the target player, in experience terms, and what does a "minor" risk feel like to them?
2. Can someone using only the simplest core finish the game or hold their own?
3. What happens to a player who fails the same spot ten times?
4. How many seconds pass between failing and the next input?
5. Who set this difficulty, how well do they know the game, and were they handicapped?
6. After this balance change, does every option still have a clear peak and valley?
7. Could a player lose purely because of luck?
8. Does each penalty match how bad the mistake was, and is there a way back?
9. How much does the game help the player who is behind, and was that a decision?
10. Where are the peaks and flats on your difficulty curve?

## Exercises

1. **Write the target, then audit the dials (1 hour).** Write one sentence naming your target player by experience. List every risk dial in your game (see the technique table) with its current value, and mark each one too harsh, right, or too soft for that player [148](../episodes/notes/148-more-game-essence-means-less-broad-appeal.md).
2. **One-handed run (half a day).** Play your hardest stretch with one hand, or with half health. Note every place you fail, then lower the difficulty there, and lower it one more notch than feels right [256](../episodes/notes/256-give-yourself-a-handicap-when-balancing-your-game.md).
3. **Stopwatch the failure loop (1 hour).** Record five deaths and time each one from the moment of failure to the next input. Cut until the failure effect is about a second and the respawn is safe [117](../episodes/notes/117-make-retries-quick.md).
4. **Stuck-player path (2 hours).** Pick the spot where testers fail most. Design and implement what the game offers on the third and fifth failure (an easier option, a skip, a cheaper retry path), and write the wording so it doesn't humiliate [213](../episodes/notes/213-better-than-not-being-able-to-beat-the-game.md) [128](../episodes/notes/128-is-your-game-appealing-enough-to-start-over.md).
5. **Peaks-and-valleys patch (2 hours).** Take your strongest character, weapon or move. List all its parameters, mark the defining one, and nerf only secondary ones. Then compare it side by side with its nearest neighbor and push them further apart [247](../episodes/notes/247-amplify-both-strengths-and-weaknesses.md).
6. **Fairness declaration (1 hour).** Write down whether your multiplayer promises that anyone can win or that the better player wins, then list every catch-up, random and handicap element and remove any that contradicts the promise [189](../episodes/notes/189-randomness-spices-games-up.md) [202](../episodes/notes/202-handicap-systems.md).
