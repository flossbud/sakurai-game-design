# 10 · Directing, Supervising and Working With a Team

> Sakurai has directed teams of every size, from a handful of people to hundreds, and he often arrives as an outsider who joins someone else's studio. His stance stays the same throughout. A game needs one person who sets its course, and that person's job is to decide fast, trim hard and keep everyone looking at the same game. Everyone else's job is to give that person accurate information early, with a proposed fix attached. Feedback should be shown rather than described, specific enough to act on, given to everyone involved at once, and returned the same day. Authority isn't rank: when a director gives direction, it's really a request to the people doing the work. All of this matters because the team is the only group that can still fix the game. Players can only complain.

## The big ideas

**One course, one captain.** A director is, by definition, the person who decides the course a project moves along ([258](../episodes/notes/258-a-directors-job-is-to-trim.md)). That's why Sakurai wants one director with the final say ([234](../episodes/notes/234-directors-and-producers.md)) instead of votes: in creative work, majority decisions drift toward the safest, most mediocre option ([158](../episodes/notes/158-the-majority-shouldnt-always-rule.md)). That authority comes with duties. The director has to decide quickly, on good information, and also has to *trim*, cutting excess when hardware, deadlines or workload push the plan off course. And the authority doesn't make the director anyone's superior. The director stands farthest upstream, but every instruction is really a request to the people who will do the work ([260](../episodes/notes/260-up-is-down-and-down-is-up.md)).

**Information is the fuel.** Every decision is a fork that commits the team to a long stretch of production ([191](../episodes/notes/191-dont-put-decisions-off.md)), and a decision built on a wrong premise makes everything after it wrong too ([258](../episodes/notes/258-a-directors-job-is-to-trim.md)). So most of his team advice is about how information flows. Raise problems the moment you see them, with a fix ([060](../episodes/notes/060-dont-wait-to-speak-your-mind.md)). Say you're stuck before the deadline, not at it. Give everyone a board for opinions. Post a daily report so the whole team sees the whole game ([043](../episodes/notes/043-sharing-info-within-a-team.md), [123](../episodes/notes/123-daily-report-suggestions.md)). Explain feedback to every discipline at once, so it can't get garbled as it's passed along ([114](../episodes/notes/114-explain-ideas-to-everyone-at-once.md)).

**Show it, say it precisely, say it today.** His supervision is concrete. A five-minute retouch beats a paragraph of description ([090](../episodes/notes/090-supervising-art-through-retouches.md)). Notes name the exact axis and spacing to change ([224](../episodes/notes/224-all-to-destroy-one-wall.md)). Sound notes give the sound's role, a comparison everyone knows and a link, never just "more boom" ([185](../episodes/notes/185-giving-feedback-for-sound-effects.md)). Every review request gets an answer the day it arrives ([177](../episodes/notes/177-finish-everything-within-the-day.md)). He judges work by whether it does its job, not by whether it matches the picture in his head, and when several rounds stop getting closer, he accepts the work and moves on.

**Delegate the doing, keep the eye.** On his early games Sakurai entered every parameter himself on top of directing, and later said he should never have done it ([135](../episodes/notes/135-super-smash-bros-for-3ds-wii-u.md), [220](../episodes/notes/220-play-testing-keeps-going.md)). So he trusts play testers, planners and specialists with the work. The director plays the build, supervises, steps in when delegated core work stalls ([059](../episodes/notes/059-kirby-air-ride.md)), and intervenes mostly to protect what makes the game and its characters distinctive. Developers also know their game too well to judge it, so he gets outsiders and children to play while the team quietly watches ([248](../episodes/notes/248-elementary-school-play-testers.md), [256](../episodes/notes/256-give-yourself-a-handicap-when-balancing-your-game.md)).

## Principles

### TEAM-01 · Give one director the final say; never settle design by majority vote
**Why:** In creative work, having more people want something doesn't make it right. Ideas everyone opposed in planning often turn out fun once they're built, and split opinions or unclear goals derail a project. **Do:** Name one person who holds the vision of the whole game and makes the content calls. If you divide the role (two co-leads on a jam team, say), write down exactly who decides what. When the team is split, the director decides and explains why. Run a poll only as input or out of curiosity. If the person with the vision believes in an unpopular idea, build it and judge it in the game. **Watch out:** The opposite failure is dictatorship. Orders barked with paper-thin reasoning aren't direction, and the decider needs the team's full information.
> Ideas decided by majority end up being the safest ideas. In other words, the most mediocre. ([158](../episodes/notes/158-the-majority-shouldnt-always-rule.md), 01:01)

*Sakurai's example:* Metal Gear Solid 3's wound-treatment system reportedly had only four supporters on its team at first. When Sakurai polled his own team on Mii Fighter maid outfits, the outfit chosen was A, and he then made a director's call to color the socks white so the character's movement reads better. · Episodes: [158](../episodes/notes/158-the-majority-shouldnt-always-rule.md), [234](../episodes/notes/234-directors-and-producers.md), [50](../episodes/notes/050-jobs-in-game-development.md), [191](../episodes/notes/191-dont-put-decisions-off.md), [70](../episodes/notes/070-directors-need-to-be-unique.md), [228](../episodes/notes/228-echo-chambers.md)

### TEAM-02 · Hold the concept steady and steer with small, frequent corrections
**Why:** He calls the concept a lighthouse. If you change direction midway, the team gets lost, months of art and programming get thrown away, and morale drops. But single decisions set your path toward that light, and a wrong one should be fixed, not defended. **Do:** Before production, write the concept as one sentence about the game's appeal (his examples: the fun of sending opponents flying, the thrill of sprinting down a straight path). Don't start full production until it's that clear. When you hit fatigue or doubt, check decisions against the concept instead of changing course. Correct bad calls quickly, and build up fun you discover along the way if it still points toward the concept. Keep restating the concept, because many teammates won't feel a new game's appeal from documents. **Watch out:** There are two failures here: changing what the game is about because it isn't turning out as hoped, and clinging to a decision you now know is wrong just because it was already made. (See *Tensions*, below.)
> Come storm or dark of night, the one thing you should never change is your trajectory toward that light. ([187](../episodes/notes/187-staying-true-to-your-concept.md), 01:03)

*Sakurai's example:* After projects shipped, teammates have told him they finally saw what he had been getting at. That's proof that a design document alone rarely carries the fun. · Episodes: [187](../episodes/notes/187-staying-true-to-your-concept.md), [191](../episodes/notes/191-dont-put-decisions-off.md), [29](../episodes/notes/029-spec-changes.md), [60](../episodes/notes/060-dont-wait-to-speak-your-mind.md), [109](../episodes/notes/109-dont-rely-on-a-plan-b.md), [224](../episodes/notes/224-all-to-destroy-one-wall.md)

### TEAM-03 · Raise problems the moment you see them, with facts and a fix
**Why:** Players can't fix anything, but the team still can. Saying "I knew that was wrong" after the project only feeds your ego and upsets your teammates. The costliest case is someone who struggles in silence and then says at the deadline that they can't finish, which throws away the time an early fix would have saved. **Do:** Make it a team rule, with no exceptions: tell someone who can act, right away, and share everything you know. Don't argue about how to bring it up. Bring the problem and at least one possible fix together. If you're stuck on your own task, flag it early with an honest status, so someone can take over or help in time. If the team decides to keep things as they are, ask for their reasons. **Watch out:** "This isn't good" with nothing else attached; saving complaints for the postmortem.
> Make the problem and the potential solution a set package. ([060](../episodes/notes/060-dont-wait-to-speak-your-mind.md), 01:57)

*Sakurai's example:* He wants people to tell him everything they know and leave nothing out, because the less accurate the information, the more likely the decision is a mistake. · Episodes: [29](../episodes/notes/029-spec-changes.md), [60](../episodes/notes/060-dont-wait-to-speak-your-mind.md), [258](../episodes/notes/258-a-directors-job-is-to-trim.md), [191](../episodes/notes/191-dont-put-decisions-off.md), [158](../episodes/notes/158-the-majority-shouldnt-always-rule.md), [249](../episodes/notes/249-never-get-into-fights.md)

### TEAM-04 · Decide on the spot instead of putting decisions off until later
**Why:** Every minute the team waits is a minute the issue stays unsolved, and holding up a key person's decision can put their whole subteam behind. A quick, firm decision helps more people than you'd expect. **Do:** Make sure someone with authority to decide is in each decision meeting, and push them to decide before it ends. If you're the decider, decide as soon as a teammate brings you the issue. If facts are missing, look them up now. Lay out every option, choose the one that works in both the short and long term, and leave room to adjust later. **Watch out:** Ending meetings by promising to decide later, or gathering second opinions while people sit blocked.

*Sakurai's example:* He tries to make a call the moment a team member comes to him and does any research he needs on the spot. · Episodes: [191](../episodes/notes/191-dont-put-decisions-off.md), [29](../episodes/notes/029-spec-changes.md), [60](../episodes/notes/060-dont-wait-to-speak-your-mind.md), [177](../episodes/notes/177-finish-everything-within-the-day.md)

### TEAM-05 · Show, don't tell: give visual feedback as quick retouches and sketches
**Why:** Palette, contrast, mood and pose are hard to explain in words, and text-only notes get misread, especially on remote teams. A retouch of about five minutes, ten at most, gets the idea across faster than explaining it. **Do:** For any visual note, make a rough paintover, a mouse sketch, a photo of a posed figure or a diagram. Send it next to the original, with one line on what to change. Label it as an example of the direction you want. The artist still does the release-quality work. **Watch out:** Don't treat your retouch as final art, and don't try to retouch everything. Save it for things words can't carry.
> When supervising a game's graphics, there's no better way to explain what you want than by just showing it. ([090](../episodes/notes/090-supervising-art-through-retouches.md), 00:03)

*Sakurai's example:* He retouched Smash Bros. Ultimate backgrounds in Photoshop using only a mouse: a Spirits cave whose palette was too understated, the Marx boss arena, a World of Light path. For Sora's forward aerial, he posed a figure to show how twisting the upper body adds information to a pose. · Episodes: [90](../episodes/notes/090-supervising-art-through-retouches.md), [165](../episodes/notes/165-odds-and-ends-of-supervising.md), [224](../episodes/notes/224-all-to-destroy-one-wall.md), [253](../episodes/notes/253-odds-and-ends-of-supervising-sora-edition.md), [14](../episodes/notes/014-assigning-animations.md), [100](../episodes/notes/100-development-secrets-of-the-original-kirby.md)

### TEAM-06 · Make every note concrete enough to act on without guessing
**Why:** Vague notes make people guess what's in your head, and every guess costs another round. The same word can also mean different things to different people. **Do:** Name the exact change: the axis, the spacing, the repeated pattern, the frame. For sound, state the sound's role ("a strong attack"), compare it only to sounds everyone knows ("like a jet engine"), and link a real example. When you give a reference track, say which quality to keep and which signature element *not* to copy. If revisions keep coming back opposite to your notes, play the reference together and agree on what your key terms mean. If you lack a discipline's vocabulary, route your notes through someone who has it. Send notes as bullet points. **Watch out:** "Make it look better," "more clang," or assuming people know the sound you're picturing.

*Sakurai's example:* Arrangements of the Athena theme kept coming back as the opposite of his feedback. He and the musicians meant different melodies by "main melody." On Smash Bros. Ultimate's Mishima Dojo wall, he asked for the boards to stay flat on the X axis, with jagged edges only on Y and Z. · Episodes: [224](../episodes/notes/224-all-to-destroy-one-wall.md), [185](../episodes/notes/185-giving-feedback-for-sound-effects.md), [222](../episodes/notes/222-using-other-songs-for-audio-reference.md), [146](../episodes/notes/146-the-original-piece-is-always-right.md), [127](../episodes/notes/127-arranging-music.md), [261](../episodes/notes/261-masahiro-sakurai-on-creating-games-finale-special.md), [165](../episodes/notes/165-odds-and-ends-of-supervising.md)

### TEAM-07 · Give feedback to everyone involved at once, in the open
**Why:** Instructions passed down a chain (director to planner to programmers) can come back as the exact opposite of what was meant. When everyone hears the same explanation, they understand the goals, and seeing the feedback others get, not just your own, teaches the whole team. **Do:** Gather everyone who touches a feature (planner, programmers, artists, animators, effects, sound, and their leads). Show the build on a big screen, take questions at the end, and let anyone else sit in. Hold group reviews within each discipline too. When you ask for changes, explain them to everyone involved, not only the person who requested the review. **Watch out:** These meetings only pay for themselves if they stay quick.

*Sakurai's example:* On Kid Icarus: Uprising, instructions relayed through planners came back reversed. So from Smash for 3DS/Wii U on, every relevant member had to attend his feedback: about 30 people on average, up to 50, around a huge monitor, with a mic. · Episodes: [114](../episodes/notes/114-explain-ideas-to-everyone-at-once.md), [14](../episodes/notes/014-assigning-animations.md), [177](../episodes/notes/177-finish-everything-within-the-day.md), [43](../episodes/notes/043-sharing-info-within-a-team.md)

### TEAM-08 · Answer every review request the same day it arrives
**Why:** The longer a reply takes, the longer people wait. Fast replies also make the second and third rounds easier, and every reply you put off makes tomorrow worse. **Do:** Use one predictable channel. His is an email titled "Review Request," sent once the work is finished. Handle requests in the order they arrive and reply the same day. Set a daily delivery cutoff so reviews never start late at night; his was 8 PM. Block a fixed period, such as mornings, with no reviews, so your own work gets done. **Watch out:** If you stay open to reviews all day, your schedule breaks into useless fragments.

*Sakurai's example:* He answers every review email within the day, however many pile up. For this YouTube channel, his video editors (HIKE) had to deliver by 8 PM, and each episode got about six to eight rounds of bullet-point feedback. · Episodes: [177](../episodes/notes/177-finish-everything-within-the-day.md), [261](../episodes/notes/261-masahiro-sakurai-on-creating-games-finale-special.md), [253](../episodes/notes/253-odds-and-ends-of-supervising-sora-edition.md)

### TEAM-09 · Write specs teammates can build from without guessing
**Why:** Programmers and artists build from the spec, and anything vague gets misread or never gets made. Exact numbers, units and images let values land close the first time, and they let you catch errors when the build doesn't match. **Do:** For animators, list every move with its length and the frame its attack becomes active. Add photos of a posed figure for key poses, and give four values per attack: standby pose, attack pose, start frame, and total frames until the move can be canceled. Write timing in frames, not "quick," and give every speed a unit. Wrap each tunable value in plain-language brackets, so programmers know what to expose as an adjustable setting. Script every grunt before a voice session. Tell artists and animators what job their piece does in play: which surfaces have collision, and when a hit lands. Clean up your private outline before it goes on the shared drive. **Watch out:** Terse names like "X-velocity" that others will misread, and raw idea dumps posted as the spec.

*Sakurai's example:* An average Smash fighter's spec has 70–80 pages of posed-figure images. ### TEAM-10 · Start specs from the player's ideal, then negotiate feasibility with programmers
**Why:** Design documents often come back built around what's convenient for the developers. Players only care whether the game is good, and they won't go easy on you because development was hard. A proposal that says "we could do A, or B" hands the decision to someone else. **Do:** Pick the option you believe is best and delete Plan B. Flag every choice made for the team's convenience, and keep it only if players won't suffer for it. Bring programmers in to balance the ideal against what's technically possible. Fall back to an alternative only when a real problem shows up. **Watch out:** Don't bend the core of the proposal out of shape up front for the sake of efficiency. It's the skeleton of the game.

*Sakurai's example:* His standard reply to convenience-first design documents: players don't care why developers do what they do, only whether the game is good. · Episodes: [21](../episodes/notes/021-try-telling-that-to-the-player.md), [109](../episodes/notes/109-dont-rely-on-a-plan-b.md), [12](../episodes/notes/012-presentations-are-all-about-speed.md), [52](../episodes/notes/052-prep-your-proposals-hot-and-fast.md)

### TEAM-11 · Make sure everyone knows the goal and their piece's role in it
**Why:** Roles only make a game when they work together, and that takes shared understanding. Art made without knowing what it does in play can hide the ground players stand on. An animator who thinks gameplay is the designer's job may put a weak pose on the hit frame. And everyone finishing their own task list still doesn't guarantee a finished game. **Do:** Share the objectives with every role, not just the leads. Form subteams that mix disciplines, with the director in each. Tell artists which elements have collision, and tell animators when collision is active. Give everyone working on a character one sentence about who that character is at heart. Keep a visible list of everything still needed to ship. **Watch out:** Treating art as standalone pictures.

*Sakurai's example:* A Mii Fighter costume of Nakoruru matched her King of Fighters XIV design but still felt off until a revision captured her essence. His usual note on Smash stages is that the backdrop is outshining the terrain. · Episodes: [50](../episodes/notes/050-jobs-in-game-development.md), [187](../episodes/notes/187-staying-true-to-your-concept.md), [243](../episodes/notes/243-establishing-characters-through-their-design.md), [30](../episodes/notes/030-emphasize-objects-with-collision.md), [225](../episodes/notes/225-always-keep-attack-collision-in-mind.md), [110](../episodes/notes/110-canceled-games.md)

### TEAM-12 · Show the whole team the whole game, every day
**Why:** On big projects people can feel like someone making one screw without knowing it becomes a car, and working without the bigger picture is stressful. Team-wide emails often go unread, but people check a page that has something new every day. **Do:** Post a daily report. Put a current screenshot at the top, then 3–5 topics (news, what you're working on, checklists, issues to fix, sometimes a game you played), with comments open. Put important announcements there, and invite teammates to post what they finished. Every few months, have each discipline or subteam present its finished work to everyone. **Watch out:** Skipping those presentations because they're a hassle to schedule. He says they're absolutely worth it.

*Sakurai's example:* He wrote a Daily Report every working day from Smash Bros. Brawl onward. When he announced there that he'd reveal the next fighter (Sephiroth) at 5 p.m., the dev team alone crashed the server. ### TEAM-13 · Playtest with fresh outsiders and children, and watch instead of asking
**Why:** Developers know their game too well to be the standard. Even an outsider stops being fresh after one session, so you can't call the same person back a second time and get a first impression. Children's honest reactions show where the controls are hard and what bores them. Problems like motion sickness stay invisible on a team where nobody gets sick. **Do:** With permission, invite staff members' kids and their friends. Provide snacks so it feels like a fun session, then stand back and note where they struggle or lose interest. Have each new tester start from the very beginning, and treat each one as a single use. Recruit testers who get motion sick to check the camera. **Watch out:** Interviewing testers instead of watching them; signing off on difficulty or comfort using only the team's judgment.
> Compared to us jaded adults, children are the purest of the pure! When they're playing a game, you'll always get their honest reactions. ([248](../episodes/notes/248-elementary-school-play-testers.md), 00:31)

*Sakurai's example:* One child playing the original Super Smash Bros. shouted with excitement at how far a Falcon Punch sent someone flying. ### TEAM-14 · Delegate execution to specialists and testers; keep the director supervising
**Why:** He set every parameter himself on the early Smash games and Kirby Air Ride, and entered them all on Meteos and Kid Icarus: Uprising, on top of supervising, approvals and marketing. He says he should never have done that. Full-time play testers who play each other every day understand balance better than the director does. **Do:** Build tools so a few planners can enter and tune the numbers. Staff dedicated play testers and trust what they find. Give specialties, such as post-processing, a clear owner. Let composers choose pieces they feel attached to, and otherwise assign work to people's strengths. Keep the director playing the build and supervising, not doing data entry. **Watch out:** A director who is the only person able to change a value; dictating every detail of creative work you've handed off.

*Sakurai's example:* From Smash for 3DS/Wii U on, a small team of planners handled tuning, and on Ultimate he only supervised. He still plays about 30 minutes of multiplayer at lunch. ### TEAM-15 · Step in personally when delegated core work stalls
**Why:** Trusting people doesn't mean leaving a stalled core alone forever. Once the assets exist, clear new specs and a director's return can rescue a project quickly. **Do:** Let leads own their work, but keep an eye on the core. If the central gameplay still isn't working after a long stretch, step back in, write new specs and rebuild the logic on top of the finished assets. If tuning stops giving the results you expect, have strong programmers hunt for bugs in the code, because no amount of number-tweaking fixes broken code. When someone says they're stuck, take over the work, re-scope it or try another approach. **Watch out:** Dictating content when what the team actually needs is hands-on help. Sakurai remembers Iwata never telling him how to approach his games, yet jumping in whenever a team hit trouble.

*Sakurai's example:* After a year of delegated development on Kirby Air Ride, not a single Warp Star worked properly. At the lead programmer's request he returned as director, they wrote new specs, and the game was finished in three and a half months, since the courses and machines were mostly done. In Melee's final stretch, Iwata worked directly with the programmers to find the code bugs. · Episodes: [59](../episodes/notes/059-kirby-air-ride.md), [259](../episodes/notes/259-mr-iwata.md), [258](../episodes/notes/258-a-directors-job-is-to-trim.md)

### TEAM-16 · As director, own the trimming when plans slip
**Why:** Hardware limits, slipping deadlines and growing workloads always push plans off course. It's faster for the director to spot the problems and make the cuts than to wait for a tug-of-war between a cost-minded producer and a quality-minded director. **Do:** When things slip, see the damage coming and drop or scale back the riskiest work. That's his ship metaphor: offload the heavy or flammable cargo. Cut detail no player will ever see, but allow small fun extras that cost no schedule time or performance. Set your hard requirements early, then trim content until they hold. Before the expensive final stretch, check honestly whether the project can be finished. **Watch out:** Finishing almost always means painful cuts, so thank the staff who got through them with you ([087](../episodes/notes/087-mastering-up.md)).
> That's because a director not only creates, but is also responsible for trimming the excess. ([258](../episodes/notes/258-a-directors-job-is-to-trim.md), 01:11)

*Sakurai's example:* He had the stage team tone down car tires that actually turned in the background of Inkling's stage, a detail truly nobody would see. On Smash for 3DS he treated 60 FPS as non-negotiable and cut things back until it held. · Episodes: [258](../episodes/notes/258-a-directors-job-is-to-trim.md), [44](../episodes/notes/044-distinguishing-between-major-and-minor-elements.md), [135](../episodes/notes/135-super-smash-bros-for-3ds-wii-u.md), [110](../episodes/notes/110-canceled-games.md), [87](../episodes/notes/087-mastering-up.md)

### TEAM-17 · Protect individuality: intervene when tuning sands distinctiveness down
**Why:** If you balance purely on strength, everything levels out. Weaknesses get patched, strengths get cut, and every character ends up average, which he considers the same as mediocre. Sakurai wants every fighter highly unique and players' opinions divided, and he'll accept some unfairness in isolated spots to get that. **Do:** Hand the balance analysis to others, but have the director step in mainly when a change threatens a character's, or the game's, individuality. Tell the tuning team to keep characters unique by exaggerating their weaknesses to offset their strengths. Take the team's concerns seriously, but tell them apart from pressure that would only make the game more ordinary. **Watch out:** Echo chambers. Hearing only agreement can make you play it too safe or push too far.

*Sakurai's example:* When he does speak up about Smash balance, he says it's most often because a fighter's individuality is under threat. · Episodes: [220](../episodes/notes/220-play-testing-keeps-going.md), [218](../episodes/notes/218-average-and-mediocre-are-the-same-thing.md), [228](../episodes/notes/228-echo-chambers.md), [70](../episodes/notes/070-directors-need-to-be-unique.md), [158](../episodes/notes/158-the-majority-shouldnt-always-rule.md)

### TEAM-18 · Judge work by its purpose, not your mental image, and know when to stop
**Why:** His goal isn't to make everything match his personal ideal. Work that differs from what you pictured is fine if it does its job and the quality is there, and sometimes it turns out better. Supervision often moves things a half-step when you hoped for a full one. **Do:** Write each asset's goal in one sentence (for the dojo wall: it has to look like the wooden boards really broke), and approve anything that meets it. Point out flaws as soon as you see them. But if several rounds still don't reach the bar, put the asset in the game as-is and spend your time elsewhere. Be clear about what you want from the start, so first-try approvals are possible. **Watch out:** Asking for big swings and then pulling back can feel to the team like the direction changes every round. The director and the artist share responsibility for that.

*Sakurai's example:* The destructible wall in Smash Ultimate's Mishima Dojo stage took several rounds of retouched feedback before it looked like broken wood. ### TEAM-19 · Lead by request and respect, never by rank
**Why:** People with ideas are powerless without others. Nobody likes a demanding customer, and acting superior only breeds resentment. The industry is small, so fights and badmouthing come back to you. **Do:** Phrase direction as requests to the people who'll do the work. Address everyone politely, new and much younger hires included. Treat disagreements as normal between allies, and raise issues as constructive feedback on the work, never as complaints about a person behind their back. Don't act subservient either, and leave every team on good terms. **Watch out:** Leaving a project midway to jump to your next job; it will follow you.
> Up is down, and down is up. Be sure to remember this, especially if you're in a leadership or directorial position. ([260](../episodes/notes/260-up-is-down-and-down-is-up.md), 02:53)

### TEAM-20 · Meet trouble with calm analysis and shared responsibility, not blame
**Why:** Results come from a whole chain: the visuals, the title, advertising, competing games, timing, platform. Poor sales can't be pinned on one person. **Do:** When something goes wrong, work out the cause and suggest a fix before you react. In post-release reviews, look at the whole chain instead of naming a culprit. When roles clash, ask what you can do so the other side can do their best work. **Watch out:** Losing your temper or taking offense. The team is in the same boat.

*Sakurai's example:* He remembers Iwata relying on logic rather than instinct, staying calm and offering analysis or suggestions in situations where most people would lose their temper. · Episodes: [259](../episodes/notes/259-mr-iwata.md), [162](../episodes/notes/162-making-games-as-part-of-a-company.md), [224](../episodes/notes/224-all-to-destroy-one-wall.md), [60](../episodes/notes/060-dont-wait-to-speak-your-mind.md)

### TEAM-21 · Give everyone an open, answered channel for opinions and requests
**Why:** People with different skills, roles and tastes notice different problems, in the game and in how the team is run, but some won't speak up unless it's easy. **Do:** Set up an opinion board where anyone can post about how development is going. The director replies or forwards each item to the right team. Survey everyone soon after any big change in how you work, and fix the problems people share first. Make time for one-on-ones. When you're the one listening, stay flexible and decide quickly. **Watch out:** Posts that get lost with no reply, which teaches people to stop posting.

*Sakurai's example:* He set up an opinion board whenever he joined a team. ### TEAM-22 · Separate the director's content role from the producer's business role
**Why:** The director has the final say on content and is best placed to own the concept, which is hard to shape without a feel for the game's core appeal. Producers weigh development costs against expected sales, handle staffing and marketing (a good game is worthless if nobody hears of it), and give interviews because directors are busy. **Do:** Write down who owns content and concept, and who owns budget, staffing and publicity. On a tiny team one person can wear both hats, but know which hat each decision belongs to. Build enough trust that disagreements don't become standoffs. **Watch out:** If you go independent, estimate how much running a company would eat into your creative time before you hire anyone.

*Sakurai's example:* He often takes on a producer's job, since Smash fighter reveal videos are marketing. ### TEAM-23 · Budget for overhead: ten people produce about seven people's work
**Why:** Running a team takes time. Someone has to manage workloads, talk to outside parties and supervise the creative side, and a strong creator promoted to lead stops producing what they used to. The bigger the team, the longer it takes to get everyone aligned, and that includes tools, security and rules. **Do:** Plan around roughly 70% of headcount as real output, with more overhead as the team grows. Name who handles each management job. Cover a promoted lead's old production work. On a team of just a few people, expect closer to full output. **Watch out:** Big changes cost output too. He estimated remote work cut productivity by about 30%.

*Sakurai's example:* His rule of thumb, which he says depends on the situation, is that ten people can generally produce seven people's worth of work. · Episodes: [88](../episodes/notes/088-ten-people-can-produce-seven-peoples-work.md), [66](../episodes/notes/066-soras-work-from-home-strategies.md), [53](../episodes/notes/053-sora-ltd.md), [114](../episodes/notes/114-explain-ideas-to-everyone-at-once.md)

### TEAM-24 · Give every task and bug a ticket with one owner and a status
**Why:** Work that lives only in conversation falls through the cracks. Whoever holds a ticket is responsible for the work, so ownership and progress are obvious. Every fix risks creating a new bug, so a disciplined loop beats simply being careful. **Do:** Split each piece of content into one ticket per discipline, each with one owner, a status and a deadline that a lead reviews. Leave slack between tasks that depend on each other. For bugs, test only on the latest daily build. File a ticket once the bug can be reproduced, grade its severity, send it to the discipline that owns the problem (programmers aren't always the right fixers), have the tester check the fix in several setups, and mark it resolved instead of deleting it. **Watch out:** Lining dependent tasks up so tightly that downstream people have to start on unfinished work.

*Sakurai's example:* Smash sometimes ran computer-controlled battles all night to find bugs, and near the end of a project, testers in Japan, North America and Europe pass save data back and forth. · Episodes: [94](../episodes/notes/094-using-tickets-to-manage-tasks.md), [208](../episodes/notes/208-bug-testing-systems.md), [50](../episodes/notes/050-jobs-in-game-development.md), [110](../episodes/notes/110-canceled-games.md)

### TEAM-25 · Present ideas fast: core idea first, no justifications or alternatives
**Why:** Creators have to communicate their ideas, and his secret is speed: trim the fat and get to the heart of it. Fast doesn't mean short, and a long deck works if every page moves. **Do:** Open with the core idea. Drop justifications, alternatives and Plan B. Use one image and about two lines of text per slide, and group slides into categories so people know where they are. Talk around your slides instead of reading them. Explain even the basics simply. Describe what's new about your game instead of "it's X, but with Y." **Watch out:** Pitching by comparison invites the question of why not just play the original.

### TEAM-26 · Build a warm team: thank, celebrate and play together
**Why:** Sakurai sees a good relationship between director and team as how game development should be. ## Tensions and how they fit

Some of his advice seems to pull in opposite directions. In each case, the two pieces apply to different layers of the work.

- **"Never change direction" ([187](../episodes/notes/187-staying-true-to-your-concept.md)) vs. "frequent slight adjustments" ([191](../episodes/notes/191-dont-put-decisions-off.md)).** The concept is the lighthouse, and individual decisions are the steering. Episode 187 forbids changing the destination, meaning what the game's appeal *is*, and even it allows adjusting to bring out fun you discover along the way. Episode 191 is about single calls: each one sets your path, so a wrong call gets fixed instead of defended. Episode 029 adds that you shouldn't dig in on an idea that isn't working. A useful test: does this change what's fun about the game, or only how we get there? There's also a third trap, which is swinging hard in a new direction with every feedback round ([224](../episodes/notes/224-all-to-destroy-one-wall.md)). From the team's side, that looks like changing course too.
> Since decisions set the trajectory of your project, frequent slight adjustments can ensure that things stay on the right path. ([191](../episodes/notes/191-dont-put-decisions-off.md), 01:55)
- **One person decides ([158](../episodes/notes/158-the-majority-shouldnt-always-rule.md), [234](../episodes/notes/234-directors-and-producers.md)) vs. hear everyone ([060](../episodes/notes/060-dont-wait-to-speak-your-mind.md), [228](../episodes/notes/228-echo-chambers.md)).** Gather input widely, then decide as one person. The opinion board, surveys and polls feed the decision. They don't make it. The decider owes the team their reasons.
- **The director has the final say ([234](../episodes/notes/234-directors-and-producers.md)) vs. nobody is higher than anyone else ([260](../episodes/notes/260-up-is-down-and-down-is-up.md)).** Final say is a responsibility, not a status. Decisions are delivered as requests, with reasons. Orders barked with paper-thin reasoning are what 158 calls dictatorship.
- **Decide on the spot ([191](../episodes/notes/191-dont-put-decisions-off.md)) vs. decide on accurate information ([029](../episodes/notes/029-spec-changes.md), [258](../episodes/notes/258-a-directors-job-is-to-trim.md)).** Speed comes from the team bringing facts and options with every problem. When something is missing, research it now rather than postpone.
- **Point out every tiny flaw ([253](../episodes/notes/253-odds-and-ends-of-supervising-sora-edition.md)) vs. accept it and move on ([224](../episodes/notes/224-all-to-destroy-one-wall.md)), and cut what nobody sees ([044](../episodes/notes/044-distinguishing-between-major-and-minor-elements.md)).** Flagging a flaw is cheap and should happen at once. Chasing perfection has a stopping point, set by what the asset is for, not by your ideal.
- **Share problems immediately ([029](../episodes/notes/029-spec-changes.md), [060](../episodes/notes/060-dont-wait-to-speak-your-mind.md)) vs. don't talk about a proposal until it's written ([144](../episodes/notes/144-keep-your-internal-pressure-high.md)).** These are different things at different stages. A proposal you're still creating gains force from being kept in until the first draft exists; then you consult. A problem you've found in production should be shared at once.
- **Commit to one spec with no Plan B ([109](../episodes/notes/109-dont-rely-on-a-plan-b.md)) vs. stay flexible ([029](../episodes/notes/029-spec-changes.md), [060](../episodes/notes/060-dont-wait-to-speak-your-mind.md)).** Commit when you write the spec, and adapt as a team when a real problem appears. 109 says you may end up at Plan B anyway, but you shouldn't start there.
- **Delegate and trust ([220](../episodes/notes/220-play-testing-keeps-going.md), [135](../episodes/notes/135-super-smash-bros-for-3ds-wii-u.md)) vs. review nearly everything ([177](../episodes/notes/177-finish-everything-within-the-day.md)) and step in ([059](../episodes/notes/059-kirby-air-ride.md)).** Hand off the doing and keep the reviewing. Step in hands-on only when core work has stalled or individuality is at stake.
- **Keep it fast and short ([012](../episodes/notes/012-presentations-are-all-about-speed.md)) vs. a 200-page proposal ([038](../episodes/notes/038-writing-game-proposals.md)).** He settles this one himself: speed is about pace, not length.

## Common mistakes

- Settling design questions by vote, or running a project with two leads who each think the other decides.
- Saving objections for the postmortem, or struggling silently and admitting you can't finish only at the deadline.
- Saying "this isn't good" without suggesting a way forward.
- Ending meetings with "we'll decide later" while people sit blocked.
- Changing what the game is about midway because it isn't turning out as hoped, or refusing to fix a small decision you now know is wrong.
- Long text descriptions of visual problems; "more clang" in sound notes; vague notes like "make it look better."
- Passing the director's intent through one person and hoping it arrives intact.
- Letting review requests pile up overnight, or starting reviews late at night.
- Specs with "fast" instead of frame counts, speeds without units, and A-or-B hedging.
- Writing specs around the developers' convenience instead of the player's experience.
- The director doing data entry while delegated core gameplay quietly stalls.
- Balancing every character toward average, then calling it fair.
- Rejecting good work because it doesn't match the picture in your head, or never-ending feedback rounds.
- Only the dev team ever playing the game; interviewing testers instead of watching them.
- Barking orders, badmouthing teammates, or blaming one person for a flop.
- Planning as if headcount equals output.

## Review questions

1. For each open design question, who decides, and does everyone know it?
2. How long does a problem take to go from noticed, to raised, to decided?
3. Can every teammate state the concept in one sentence, and their task's role in it?
4. Is our feedback shown, specific, and heard by everyone it affects?
5. What is the oldest unanswered review request or pending decision?
6. Could a new teammate build this spec without asking a question?
7. When did someone new to the game last play it from the start while we watched in silence?
8. Is the director spending time where only the director can help?
9. Are we keeping what makes the game and its characters distinctive?
10. Does the team see the whole game, and feel heard and respected?

## Exercises

1. **Problem-package hour (60 min).** Everyone writes down the top three problems they've been sitting on, each with a proposed fix. The director rules on every one before the meeting ends: decide it, assign it, or assign research due today. Track how many had been known for more than a week.
2. **Retouch versus words (45 min).** Take three assets that need changes. For each one, the reviewer writes a text-only note, then spends no more than ten minutes on a paintover or sketch. Give each artist both versions and ask which one they could act on without asking a question.
3. **The no-questions spec (half a day).** Write one attack or ability spec using the four values, frame counts, units, and bracketed adjustable values. Hand it to a teammate to implement without talking to you. Have them log every question they had to ask, then revise the spec template so those questions never come up again.
4. **Silent outsider playtest (2 hours).** Bring in two or three people who have never seen the game, and have each start from the very beginning. Nobody on the team speaks or helps. Log every spot where a player struggles with the controls or loses interest. Don't reuse these testers as "fresh" next time.
5. **One-week daily report.** For five working days, the lead posts a screenshot plus 3–5 topics, with comments open. Put every important announcement there instead of in chat or email. At the end of the week, ask the team what they learned about parts of the game outside their own.
6. **Concept check (15 min).** Everyone writes the game's concept in one sentence, plus why their current task matters to it. Read them aloud. Wherever the answers differ, the director restates the concept, and any task that doesn't serve it gets questioned.
