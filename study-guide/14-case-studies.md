# 14 · Case Studies: How Sakurai's Games Were Designed

> Sakurai's design rules didn't come from theory. They came from a string of concrete problems: a first game for people who had never played one, a sequel that had to please beginners and veterans at once, a co-op mode for a scrolling platformer, a fighting game in which the opponent still matters after the first hit, a puzzle game from a man who is bad at puzzle games, two versions of Smash on very different hardware, and a roster of every fighter ever. In each case he named the problem, picked a concept the team could actually finish, let the hardware and the audience shape the answer, and then lived with the results, regrets included. This chapter walks through each game in the order he made them, as the Game Concepts and Special episodes tell it, and closes with the patterns that repeat. For the planning habits behind them, see chapter 04.

## The big ideas

**Every game starts with a player and a problem.** Kirby's Dream Land was "tailor-made for newcomers" ([2](../episodes/notes/002-masahiro-sakurais-creative-works.md), [4](../episodes/notes/004-kirbys-dream-land.md)). Kirby's Adventure had to satisfy beginners and veterans in a mature market ([11](../episodes/notes/011-kirbys-adventure.md)). Smash began as a response to combo-heavy fighters in which the opponent stopped mattering after the first hit ([27](../episodes/notes/027-super-smash-bros.md)). Kid Icarus: Uprising aimed at hardcore players because casual DS owners were already served ([125](../episodes/notes/125-kid-icarus-uprising.md)). He says most of his concepts are logical: they solve something ([40](../episodes/notes/040-super-smash-bros-melee.md)). The exception proves the rule. Melee's concept was simply "power up Smash Bros. by leaps and bounds", because the GameCube made that possible ([40](../episodes/notes/040-super-smash-bros-melee.md)).

**Feasibility picks the concept; circumstances make the bold ones possible.** Smash was chosen over a promising RC adventure because it could be finished sooner ([27](../episodes/notes/027-super-smash-bros.md)). Kirby Air Ride was a racer because a racer was quicker to make than an action game ([59](../episodes/notes/059-kirby-air-ride.md)). Ultimate's "Everyone is here!" was affordable only because the team, office and tools carried over from the previous game ([160](../episodes/notes/160-super-smash-bros-ultimate.md)). He reads the situation (time, team, hardware, market) and builds the concept to fit it.

**Hardware is a design input, and so are its limits.** The analog stick gave Smash its flick input ([154](../episodes/notes/154-flick-input.md)). Glasses-free 3D made Uprising a game about depth ([125](../episodes/notes/125-kid-icarus-uprising.md)). The 3DS and Wii U split into "the one" and "the many" ([135](../episodes/notes/135-super-smash-bros-for-3ds-wii-u.md)). The Game Boy's tiny memory produced mirrored bosses and shared sprite halves ([100](../episodes/notes/100-development-secrets-of-the-original-kirby.md)). The same habit explains his regrets: Meteos lost its intended difficulty to a late change in processing speed, and Mushiking was tuned on a board that didn't behave like the retail unit, so both shipped with timing problems he still remembers ([141](../episodes/notes/141-unexpected-results.md)).

**Variety comes from parameters and reuse, not from new code for everything.** Air Ride's machines share one behavior and differ only in numbers ([59](../episodes/notes/059-kirby-air-ride.md)). Meteos' planets change the rules through field size, gravity and piece behavior ([74](../episodes/notes/074-meteos.md)). Smash for 3DS/Wii U built a "variable character strength" layer that powered half a dozen features ([135](../episodes/notes/135-super-smash-bros-for-3ds-wii-u.md)). Ultimate's Spirits rebuilt the solo mode out of assets Smash already had ([160](../episodes/notes/160-super-smash-bros-ultimate.md)). Ideas also travel between games: City Trial became Smash Run, the flick input moved from Smash to Uprising, and the Checklist became Smash's Challenges ([135](../episodes/notes/135-super-smash-bros-for-3ds-wii-u.md), [154](../episodes/notes/154-flick-input.md), [151](../episodes/notes/151-planning-your-games-rewards.md)).

## The case studies

### Kirby's Dream Land (Game Boy, 1992)
**The design problem.** NES-era games were known for being hard, partly because limited cartridge memory meant little unique content, so an easy game was beaten too quickly. That difficulty left beginners out in the cold. Sakurai, 19 when he wrote the proposal, wanted a game that would show newcomers how fun games can be. The game was largely designed for 512 kilobits of ROM.

**The decisions.** The starting idea was that "it would be fun if the player could use enemies": first a tongue to grab them, then inhaling and spitting them out. He gave Kirby flight because losing a life for a missed jump felt too harsh, though falling into a pit still costs a life. He accepted that flight lets players skip enemies, because the Game Boy screen was so small. Inhaling removes an approaching enemy before the risk gets high, and Kirby can take six hits. Bosses are big so slightly off-target attacks still connect. To fit the memory, Waddle Dee and Waddle Doo share a back half, Gordo is one image flipped, and Whispy Woods is one mirrored piece stuck to the background. Enemy paths were hand-placed without terrain collision, which was cheap on processing and helped make Cappy bouncy. Stages begin as their musical intros end.

**Why.** He wanted the first Kirby to be as accessible as possible, even when staff asked "Are you sure about this?". He holds that more game essence means less broad appeal, and that a minor risk for a veteran can be a brick wall for a beginner ([148](../episodes/notes/148-more-game-essence-means-less-broad-appeal.md)). He targeted *beginners*, not *children*: beginners can be any age, and the game still has enough "oomph" for advanced players ([226](../episodes/notes/226-targeting-and-market-research.md)).

**The lesson.** Choose the player first and set the risk for them. Let memory and screen limits suggest design instead of only cutting it. Replay value can come from fun instead of difficulty: experienced players finish it in 20 minutes, yet it sold over 5 million copies ([100](../episodes/notes/100-development-secrets-of-the-original-kirby.md)). His 1990 design document even described Smash-style knockback and off-screen KOs, which he had forgotten by the time he made Smash ([100](../episodes/notes/100-development-secrets-of-the-original-kirby.md)).
> I gave him the ability to fly because I thought it was too harsh to lose a life just for missing a jump. ([4](../episodes/notes/004-kirbys-dream-land.md), 01:48)

### Kirby's Adventure (NES)
**The design problem.** Right after Dream Land, the company was insolvent and in settlement proceedings, and Sakurai was told to bring Kirby to the NES. Planning began in 1992. The NES was chosen over the Super NES because the company needed a game it could make and sell quickly. By then the NES market had matured and few beginners were starting on it: "How do I make something both beginners and veterans can enjoy?" The larger NES display also made Kirby look smaller and wider, and flight became strong enough to skip almost any challenge.

**The decisions.** The copy ability took "using enemies" one step further. Novices can finish by inhaling and spitting alone; experienced players dig into abilities for tricks. Every enemy gained meaning, and each new encounter became something to look forward to. Instead of removing flight, he changed its context: a bigger info panel limited vertical space (and made characters look bigger and gave room for each ability's picture), mid-boss fights stayed on one screen, and flight got slower. Three one-button minigames for novices sat behind entrances on the world map, so others could skip them.

**Why.** He had to satisfy both groups, not just tolerate one. Cutting a signature mechanic would have cost the character's appeal; changing its surroundings rebalanced it. Under Iwata, HAL pulled people in from other teams to finish the game ([259](../episodes/notes/259-mr-iwata.md)).

**The lesson.** Layer optional depth on a core a novice can finish, and when a feature becomes too strong, try changing its context before cutting it. The copy ability went on to shape the rest of the series ([11](../episodes/notes/011-kirbys-adventure.md)).
> Players who aren't used to complex game mechanics can finish the game just by inhaling enemies and spitting them out. ([11](../episodes/notes/011-kirbys-adventure.md), 02:02)

### Kirby Super Star (Super NES, 1996)
**The design problem.** Shigeru Miyamoto asked whether a two-player Kirby was possible. In side-scrollers, players tend to drag each other along as the screen scrolls, and copy abilities moved at very different speeds, which made it maybe harder than for Mario. Separately, Sakurai felt too many games were taking too long to beat.

**The decisions.** The Helper system split players into a lead, whom the camera follows, and a supporter who may go off-camera; a supporter too far off-screen "Space Jumps" back. Combined with copy abilities, enemies became playable partners. Copy abilities grew from one move each to many (repeat presses, directions, commands), so standard enemies now survived a few hits and the health bar became more analog. Content was packaged as an omnibus: scenarios pinned to a cork board, each its own game with its own rules and theme, from a food-eating race to a story-driven adventure. He sketched abilities at their real size in a Famicom tool while waiting for SNES tools, and Fighter's moveset and the Final Cutter combo survived to release ([100](../episodes/notes/100-development-secrets-of-the-original-kirby.md)). A mouth-sealed survival scenario, "Kagero Mansion", was cut for schedule and ROM space.

**Why.** A structural answer (one lead, one helper) fixed what equal-weight scrolling couldn't. Short, distinct scenarios offered lots of content without wearing players out on one style. Two decisions he regrets: adding the copy ability to the first scenario, Spring Breeze, at a producer's request, which left nothing setting it apart from the next, and switching mid-project to CG backgrounds following Donkey Kong Country. The staff didn't know the technique and it didn't suit Kirby's pieced-together terrain, though he still values learning new skills.

**The lesson.** Solve hard requests with structure, keep each content chunk distinct, and check that a new technique fits the team before adopting it mid-project.
> Instead of letting both characters scroll the screen with equal weight, I placed the focus on only the lead character. ([20](../episodes/notes/020-kirby-super-star.md), 02:30)

### Super Smash Bros. (Nintendo 64)
**The design problem.** In 1996 Sakurai taught himself 3D and proposed two N64 games: a four-player fighter and an RC robot adventure. Both prototypes were praised. He felt combo-focused fighters had lost push and pull: after the first hit the outcome depended on stringing attacks, the opponent's skill stopped mattering, and newcomers just watched themselves lose. Command inputs had become very complicated, and a console fighter usually arrived with 8 to 12 unknown main characters at once.

**The decisions.** The fighter won because the team urgently needed a finished game and the RC adventure would have taken two years. Its prototype, "Dragon King: The Fighting Game", which Iwata programmed, had no special moves, dodges or items but already used Smash's rules. Accumulated damage means opponents react differently to every hit, and a ring-out decides the stock whatever the damage. Controls became a stick direction plus a button, and the smash input read how quickly the analog stick is flicked ([154](../episodes/notes/154-flick-input.md)). Nintendo's characters were negotiated later to solve the unknown-cast problem, and Iwata chose "Brothers" for the title so the fighting reads as friends settling a disagreement ([259](../episodes/notes/259-mr-iwata.md)). Rules were flexible: chaotic four-player battles with items, or tense one-on-one without, the start of "playing what you want, how you want." Break the Targets! was built to teach each character's controls ([173](../episodes/notes/173-teaching-players-how-to-play.md)). Fox and Captain Falcon, pilots who do little on foot in their own games, left room to invent the Falcon Punch ([188](../episodes/notes/188-faithfulness-comes-first-in-licensed-games.md)).

**Why.** He calls Smash an "antithesis" to fighting games, not a rejection: he wanted more room for interplay and improvisation. When sales staff, wholesalers and magazines dismissed the idea and ignored his push-and-pull documentation, he turned it into the Smash Bros. DOJO!! players' guide site ([143](../episodes/notes/143-official-websites.md)).

**The lesson.** Name the genre's problem and design a rule that removes it; prove it with a bare prototype; pick the concept you can finish; and if people don't get it, explain the depth yourself. A child playtester shouting "Whoa! Look how far he flew!" at a Falcon Punch confirmed the core landed ([248](../episodes/notes/248-elementary-school-play-testers.md)).
> Instead of inputs alone determining the outcome, I wondered if we could make a game with more room for interplay and improvisation. ([27](../episodes/notes/027-super-smash-bros.md), 03:51)

### Super Smash Bros. Melee (GameCube)
**The design problem.** The N64 had been very limiting: a fighter was about 300 polygons, about 200 with three or more on screen, with 64x64 or even 32x32 textures. The GameCube was far stronger and easier to develop for. The question was whether a sequel was worth the immense labor.

**The decisions.** The concept was simply to power up Smash Bros. by leaps and bounds: fighters, stages, graphics, effects, animation, sound, content, play feel, items and bonus features. Streaming playback allowed orchestral arrangements with real instruments, which led to the PRESS START concerts ([116](../episodes/notes/116-press-start.md)). He drew the storyboards for his first CG movie over his New Year's holiday. Trophies brought in characters from across Nintendo's history who couldn't all be playable.

**Why.** Most creative works have to do what they can to stand out from the crowd. Melee became the top seller among GameCube titles. The cost showed too: Sakurai entered every parameter himself, each conversion to a test build took ten minutes ([46](../episodes/notes/046-making-your-game-easy-to-tune.md), [220](../episodes/notes/220-play-testing-keeps-going.md)), and Iwata had to debug code with the programmers in the final stretch so it could ship before the end of 2001 ([259](../episodes/notes/259-mr-iwata.md)). Its speed thrilled top players but was too hard on newcomers, which Brawl later corrected ([108](../episodes/notes/108-super-smash-bros-brawl.md)).

**The lesson.** A sequel's headline can be "more", as long as every element gets a real upgrade and the new hardware does something the old couldn't.

### Kirby Air Ride (GameCube)
**The design problem.** The Kirby anime began airing in Japan in October 2001, and a stronger lineup of games was needed alongside it. Sakurai was chief director of the series, overseeing the anime too, while Melee was at the height of development. He judged a racing game quicker to make than a traditional action game. Then, after a year of delegated development, not a single Warp Star worked properly.

**The decisions.** He analyzed why drifting is fun (losing control with walls ahead is thrilling, getting through feels great) and added a charge: braking into a drift builds power, releasing at the end gives a burst ([57](../episodes/notes/057-deconstruct-analyze-and-rebuild.md)). Instead of rewarding one perfect line, the game widened the range of play styles, above all in City Trial, a sandbox with varying power-ups and stadiums. The Checklist gave casual players a sense of accomplishment before console achievements existed. At the lead programmer's request he returned as director; with new specs they rebuilt the game logic and finished in three and a half months, helped by courses and machines that were mostly done. Every machine shares one behavior; apart from star versus bike types, the differences are parameters, all of which he wrote.

**Why.** Faster drifting alone wouldn't be enough; squeeze and release made it a game. Convergence on a single optimal method narrows play. Parameter-driven machines let a few numbers change a machine's whole character.

**The lesson.** Build on one analyzed piece of fun, design for variety rather than perfection, and when delegated core work stalls, step in with fresh specs. After Air Ride, he left HAL Laboratory and went independent.
> Instead of encouraging perfection, a major concept of the game was expanding the possible play types. ([59](../episodes/notes/059-kirby-air-ride.md), 02:40)

### Meteos (Nintendo DS)
**The design problem.** Now freelance, and supervising other teams' games without credit, Sakurai was asked by Tetsuya Mizuguchi for a falling-block puzzle game, a genre already seen as a bit outdated. He is, by his own account, really bad at such games.

**The decisions.** He worked out the genre's essence first: stacked blocks are the risk, the higher the greater, and careful planning turns that risk into a big reward all at once. Then he asked whether blocks really had to be erased. Renaming the clear "vaporized" or "demolished" wouldn't change anything; the core concept had to. Matched blocks ignite and blast off into space, which allows secondary ignitions and air docking. He says the launch idea took about five minutes because he already understood the genre's essence ([86](../episodes/notes/086-game-essence-in-falling-block-puzzle-games.md)). The proposal used PowerPoint animations and came with a background story of an interplanetary war. Each planet changes block shapes, field width, launch behavior, music and sound effects. He checked the blocks through a color-blindness filter and separated similar hues by brightness ([214](../episodes/notes/214-a-world-with-less-color.md)). Meteos was also where his menus began using icons sized by importance ([147](../episodes/notes/147-make-important-elements-bigger.md)).

**Why.** Understanding the essence let him design in a genre he couldn't play well. Planets that change the rules make controls feel drastically different, so players pick favorites.

**The lesson.** Change the core verb, not its name; make stages change the rules; and lock processing speed before final tuning. A late optimization tied to processing speed made the game faster and harder than his per-planet tuning intended, which he calls a disappointing memory ([141](../episodes/notes/141-unexpected-results.md)).

### Sodatete! Kouchuu Ouja Mushiking (handheld LCD toy)
**The design problem.** Tadashi Takezaki, then at Sega, asked him to cross the arcade card game Kouchuu Ouja Mushiking with handheld digital pet games. It had the smallest team of any game he has made. He worked on it remotely while starting Brawl, on a custom development board just under one square meter.

**The decisions.** The arcade game's rock-paper-scissors, with each Mushi specializing in one shape, already had push and pull, so he kept it. Effort went to the pet side: growth stages were checked so players wouldn't hit a wall in battle, the Mushi grazes on its food instead of scarfing it down, and hidden behaviors reward attention (wake it early and it shifts its schedule). The tiny device holds affection, likes and dislikes, items, illness, training, fatigue, mutations, presents, farewells and runaways, plus infrared-link battles.

**Why.** Reusing a proven loop freed the small team to sell the fantasy. But he never tested on the production handheld. Possibly because its processing speed differed from the board's, the Mushi gets hungry faster than planned and almost always wants food, adding stress he never intended ([92](../episodes/notes/092-sodatete-kouchuu-ouja-mushiking.md), [141](../episodes/notes/141-unexpected-results.md)).

**The lesson.** When adapting, keep the core that works and spend effort on what the new format adds; and test timer-driven systems on the real hardware.
> Before it was done, I never got the chance to test the game out on the production handheld. ([92](../episodes/notes/092-sodatete-kouchuu-ouja-mushiking.md), 08:13)

### Super Smash Bros. Brawl (Wii)
**The design problem.** At E3 2005, the day after the Wii was announced, Iwata asked Sakurai, by then freelance, to make the next Smash. The game had been announced before any development began. If he had declined, Melee's 26 fighters might simply have come back with online added ([132](../episodes/notes/132-the-team-behind-super-smash-bros-brawl.md)). The Wii targeted casual players, Brawl had to be playable on a sideways Wii Remote, and fully synchronous online play suits Smash poorly.

**The decisions.** He turned down his other projects except Mushiking, and Nintendo built a new team in Tokyo around him, its core from Game Arts. Between E3 and the October start, he wrote the design documents and pitched each character to its original creator; the team grew past 100 people ([132](../episodes/notes/132-the-team-behind-super-smash-bros-brawl.md)). The concept had two pillars: online support and a single-player mode of appropriate length, the Subspace Emissary, which needed separate jump and movement parameters for every fighter. Final Smashes gave each fighter a unique super move instead of a generic item. Assist Trophies and My Music brought in more of Nintendo's history; Snake and Sonic joined as guest fighters. Speed came down a little from Melee's for casual play, the Wii Remote and online frame skips. Brawl was the first Smash with playtesters: four of them ([220](../episodes/notes/220-play-testing-keeps-going.md)). The Smash Bros. DOJO!! site posted a new detail every weekday in six languages ([245](../episodes/notes/245-smash-bros-dojo.md)).

**Why.** "If my job is to do it, I'll do it": online was essential on a console with it built in, even though he wrote in the proposal that Smash is in some ways unsuited to online play ([202](../episodes/notes/202-handicap-systems.md)). Every character deserved a moment in the spotlight. The per-fighter movies he made as rewards were posted online from pre-release copies, so he later turned showcase movies into fighter reveals instead ([163](../episodes/notes/163-fighter-reveal-videos.md)).

**The lesson.** Anchor a sequel to the pillars its platform and audience demand, use pre-production to finish docs and win stakeholders, and don't make shareable movies the reward. Brawl sold over 12 million units ([2](../episodes/notes/002-masahiro-sakurais-creative-works.md)).

### Kid Icarus: Uprising (Nintendo 3DS)
**The design problem.** After Brawl, Iwata asked Sakurai to consult on the unannounced 3DS's specs and make an exclusive for it, with no genre specified. Casual DS players were already served by the Touch! Generations titles. The new hardware offered glasses-free 3D, and the screen was small.

**The decisions.** He went the other way: a rich, meaty game for hardcore players, with depth as part of the fun, so a third-person shooter. The Kid Icarus motif came at the end of the proposal: Pit can't fly, so Palutena grants him the Power of Flight for five minutes. Each chapter opens with a rail-shooter Air Battle, then moves to Land Battles. Standard enemies are four or five times Pit's size so players aren't squinting at tiny targets ([42](../episodes/notes/042-a-small-window-into-the-world.md)). Flicking the Circle Pad dashes or dodges, the touch screen aims like a trackball, and one attack button chooses ranged or melee by weapon and distance. Story plays during battle like a radio program, with no full briefings, and can be turned off; he wrote it himself because Air Battles had to line up with story beats and music. There are 108 weapons in nine categories, the Fiend's Cauldron wager difficulty ([41](../episodes/notes/041-the-fiends-cauldron.md)), and Project Sora was set up as a one-time company for the game.

**Why.** Starting with free shooting would have asked beginners to dodge, aim and fire at once. Fixed-length Air Battles let the music hit exact beats ([227](../episodes/notes/227-gameplay-as-part-of-a-live-performance.md)). He felt existing shooters were stuck in by-the-book theory and wanted to present the genre in a new way, as Smash did for fighting games. Not everything meshed: the Fiend's Cauldron made the game feel too hardcore and its way of lowering difficulty wasn't clear enough to newcomers, land stages were too big with too many enemies ([41](../episodes/notes/041-the-fiends-cauldron.md)), a twin-stick peripheral would have cost about 5% processor load the game didn't have, and a team from many work cultures sometimes got relayed instructions backwards, which changed how he briefed teams afterward ([114](../episodes/notes/114-explain-ideas-to-everyone-at-once.md)).

**The lesson.** Aim the concept at the hardware's strength and the market gap, ease players in with a simpler slice of the core, and tell the story without stopping play.
> Starting with the TPS portion would've required the player to immediately learn how to dodge, aim, and fire— which would be a lot for beginners. ([125](../episodes/notes/125-kid-icarus-uprising.md), 04:00)

### Super Smash Bros. for 3DS / Wii U (released 2014)
**The design problem.** Smash had never been on a handheld, which gave a 3DS version its own appeal, but the 3DS's processing and graphics were a challenge. The Wii U's main offer was power, which made it hard to do something new with it alone. Building both at once risked double the work.

**The decisions.** He framed the pair as "the one" (a handheld, one player per machine) and "the many" (a home console). Mechanics, animation and balance were shared, so two versions didn't mean double the work. The weaker hardware set the limits: Ice Climbers were dropped, knockback flight was made slower so action stayed readable on the 3DS screen, and overall speed sat between Brawl and Melee. Four players, stereoscopic 3D and a stable 60 fps on 3DS were non-negotiable, so content was pared down until it worked. Each platform got its own modes: Smash Run on 3DS (five minutes to collect power-ups, rules taken from Air Ride's City Trial, possible because everyone has their own screen) and 8-Player Smash and Smash Tour on Wii U. A "variable character strength" framework powered Smash Run, Smash Tour, custom moves, Mii Fighters and amiibo, kept out of level-playing-field matches. Parameter entry was finally delegated to a small team of planners, backed by more playtesters. Online updates and paid DLC arrived for the first time, and fighter reveal videos began ([163](../episodes/notes/163-fighter-reveal-videos.md)).

**Why.** Each platform needed something only it could offer. He says that with all his supervision, approvals and marketing work, he should never have been entering parameters himself. The price of updates was a schedule that ran until spring 2016.

**The lesson.** Share data across versions, let the weakest target set shared limits, treat a stable frame rate as a hard requirement and trim until it holds, and build one parameter layer that many features can stand on.

### Super Smash Bros. Ultimate (Nintendo Switch, 2018)
**The design problem.** It was the last mission he received from Iwata. Earlier Smash games had each been made by an entirely new team, so assets and know-how didn't carry over and almost everything was rebuilt. Each fighter has its own rights holders and costs a great deal, so new games usually drop old fighters. The cartridge capped the ROM at about 16 GB, and the solo content of earlier games was too expensive at Ultimate's scale.

**The decisions.** Smash for 3DS/Wii U and Ultimate were made by the same studio, in the same office, with many of the same people, on similar hardware. That made "Everyone is here!" feasible, with only seven missing veterans to rebuild, and he saw it as the one chance. He finished the 200-plus-page proposal on the day the last Smash for 3DS/Wii U presentation went out, to keep the team together. New fighters drew on a fan survey of about 1.8 million valid votes, and he listed 70 to 100 potential changes per fighter across every discipline. The tempo was raised again, staying slower than Melee, so every parameter had to be redone. There were fewer brand-new stages, because they take too long to design, and compression fit the music into a quarter of its old space. Spirits replaced Trophies: characters earned by winning battles that recreate their source games with Smash's own borrowed characters, worlds and sounds, gathered into World of Light; overpowered players earn fewer rewards. DLC ran three years, finished partly under pandemic remote work ([66](../episodes/notes/066-soras-work-from-home-strategies.md)).

**Why.** Continuity made the bold concept cheap, and the solo mode was rebuilt from strengths Smash already had. The scale had costs. Spirits made testing enormous ([136](../episodes/notes/136-bug-testing-never-ends.md)). CPU fighters felt too hard at launch, cause unknown, and the patch was deliberately narrow (easier Challenger fights, a Very Easy setting for World of Light) to avoid re-testing the core ([141](../episodes/notes/141-unexpected-results.md)). The hands-on technique tutorial was cut for schedule, which he regrets more than almost anything ([173](../episodes/notes/173-teaching-players-how-to-play.md)). Even so, online win rates ran only from 47.18% to 51.43% across 87 fighters, despite each keeping strong individuality ([218](../episodes/notes/218-average-and-mediocre-are-the-same-thing.md)), and every DLC fighter got its own system ([206](../episodes/notes/206-using-parameters-to-establish-characters.md)).

**The lesson.** Take stock of what carries over and pick the ambition it makes possible; rebuild unaffordable modes from existing assets; budget the test cost of every system; and protect must-ship features like tutorials.
> I knew if there would ever be a chance to include everyone, this would be it! ([160](../episodes/notes/160-super-smash-bros-ultimate.md), 02:23)

### Masahiro Sakurai on Creating Games (YouTube channel)
**The design problem.** After the last Ultimate DLC, his next game was approved but its team wouldn't be assembled for several months, a rare gap for a director. He wanted to leave his knowledge in a lasting form, found lectures and conferences inefficient, and would have to run the channel alongside directing a game.

**The decisions.** He wrote scripts before anything else: a few hundred one-line topic ideas, sorted into categories with a set number of videos each, weak ones removed, then outlines and a script for every heading. That took about a month at roughly 12 scripts a day. Every script except the finale existed before production, fixing the episode count (256 planned) from the start. A pilot of three real episodes (C03, C11, D03) tested whether he could actually do it and won Nintendo's support. An Excel sheet spread the categories evenly into a release order and doubled as the progress tracker. He recorded alone at night, set an 8 PM delivery cutoff with his editors and cleared every review the same day. The whole thing cost about 90 million yen and earned nothing.

**Why.** For a director of his scale, running a channel and a game at once was only feasible if everything was decided in advance. The cost was that he couldn't take requests or change course based on comments. Speed beat polish: "Short and snappy! Speed above all else!"

**The lesson.** When a project's content can be known in advance, plan and write all of it before production, and prove feasibility with a finished-quality pilot. (Compare chapter 04's tension between planning everything and discovering fun in the build.)

### The arcade lineage (Special Crossovers #1–#3)
**The design problem.** Not a game of his, but the roots of his rules. Sakurai holds that you can't talk about gaming history without arcade games, so with Arino Kacho of *Retro Game Master* he played 25 arcade games from 1978 to 1987 as simultaneous challenges, with handicaps to even out their skill.

**What they show.** Greed built into scoring: Galaxian flagships worth more if their escorts go first, escalating points for eating fleeing ghosts in one go ([155](../episodes/notes/155-special-crossover-1.md)). A hidden lever on difficulty: destroying one Xevious enemy rewinds the enemy table to an easier point ([156](../episodes/notes/156-special-crossover-2.md)). A "trial time" in Space Harrier when you can die as much as you want; difficulty and lives settings used as handicaps; co-op that makes things harder when power-ups are budgeted for one ship (Darius) or the scroll point sits too far forward (Contra); attack tells you can read, like Punch-Out!!'s yellow eyes ([157](../episodes/notes/157-special-crossover-3.md)). The simultaneous-start format later became his advice for turning any game into a contest ([241](../episodes/notes/241-turning-anything-into-a-competitive-game.md)), and the three-minute rule in chapter 04 comes from the same arcade world ([17](../episodes/notes/017-just-let-them-play.md)).

**The lesson.** Play the foundational games yourself and note which idea each one introduced. "You can see how all these clever ideas built on top of each other."

## Patterns across his games

Read in sequence, the games show a consistent method. He starts from the target player and a named problem. He weighs concepts by whether they can be finished, and looks for what his current team, tools and hardware make uniquely possible. He treats each platform's strengths and limits as design input. He builds variety from parameters and reuses what already works. And he keeps a long memory for his mistakes. The regrets are as instructive as the successes: Meteos' difficulty shifted by a late optimization and Mushiking tuned on a dev board, Super Star's CG backgrounds and its Spring Breeze copy ability, Uprising's too-hardcore difficulty system, Ultimate's cut tutorial, and the screen shake in Kirby's Adventure's ending that was supposed to be a white flash ([67](../episodes/notes/067-youll-forget-the-hardship-but-the-game-will-endure.md)). The principles below are those lessons, generalized.

### CASE-01 · Set the game's risk by its target player, not by your own skill
**Why:** Sakurai wanted the first Kirby to be as accessible as possible, so inhaling and flying both lowered the risks newcomers faced, even when staff asked if he was sure. More game essence means less broad appeal, and a minor risk to a veteran can be a brick wall to a beginner. The same logic ran the other way when audiences changed: Brawl slowed down for casual Wii players, and Uprising aimed at hardcore ones. **Do:** Write down the target player before tuning any risk. For newcomers, soften the harshest penalties while keeping some (fly over pits, but falling still costs a life); for experts, let them raise the risk themselves. Re-check risk whenever the platform or audience shifts. **Watch out:** Overshooting works both ways. Uprising's Fiend's Cauldron made the game feel too hardcore, and its way of lowering the difficulty wasn't clear enough to the newcomers who needed it.

*Sakurai's example:* Kirby's Dream Land: inhale before an enemy gets dangerous, six hits of health, flight at any time. · Episodes: [4](../episodes/notes/004-kirbys-dream-land.md), [148](../episodes/notes/148-more-game-essence-means-less-broad-appeal.md), [108](../episodes/notes/108-super-smash-bros-brawl.md), [125](../episodes/notes/125-kid-icarus-uprising.md), [41](../episodes/notes/041-the-fiends-cauldron.md), [213](../episodes/notes/213-better-than-not-being-able-to-beat-the-game.md), [226](../episodes/notes/226-targeting-and-market-research.md)

### CASE-02 · Layer optional depth on a core that a novice can finish
**Why:** Kirby's Adventure had to satisfy beginners and veterans in a mature market. Copy abilities sat on top of inhale-and-spit, so novices could finish with the core while veterans dug in. Extras for one audience stayed off the critical path. Smash's configurable rules carried the idea into multiplayer. **Do:** Play through using only the core verbs and confirm the game can be finished that way. Put depth systems and audience-specific extras where players choose them: a map entrance, a rule toggle, an optional mode. Let the depth layer give each encounter something to look forward to. **Watch out:** Don't gate progress behind the depth system, and don't force extras on players who don't want them.

*Sakurai's example:* Copy abilities and the three one-button minigames entered from the world map; Smash's items-on chaos versus items-off one-on-one. · Episodes: [11](../episodes/notes/011-kirbys-adventure.md), [27](../episodes/notes/027-super-smash-bros.md), [226](../episodes/notes/226-targeting-and-market-research.md), [51](../episodes/notes/051-the-potential-of-one-button.md), [4](../episodes/notes/004-kirbys-dream-land.md)

### CASE-03 · Build the concept as the answer to a named problem
**Why:** Sakurai says most of his concepts are logical: they solve a problem. Combo-heavy fighters left the opponent nothing to do, so Smash used accumulated damage and ring-outs. Games took too long, so Super Star became an omnibus. Shooters seemed stuck in by-the-book theory, so Uprising rethought them. For Meteos he found the genre's essence and asked whether blocks even needed to vanish. **Do:** Write the pain point of the genre or brief in one sentence, then design a core rule that removes it. Test it: does it change what the player does, or only what the action is called? **Watch out:** An "antithesis" is a response, not a rejection; he admires the push and pull of the fighting games Smash answered.

*Sakurai's example:* Smash's accumulated damage; Meteos' blocks that blast off instead of disappearing. · Episodes: [27](../episodes/notes/027-super-smash-bros.md), [74](../episodes/notes/074-meteos.md), [86](../episodes/notes/086-game-essence-in-falling-block-puzzle-games.md), [20](../episodes/notes/020-kirby-super-star.md), [125](../episodes/notes/125-kid-icarus-uprising.md), [57](../episodes/notes/057-deconstruct-analyze-and-rebuild.md)

### CASE-04 · Let the platform's unique strength and the market gap shape the concept
**Why:** Glasses-free 3D made depth central to Uprising, and Touch! Generations already served casual DS owners. The 3DS and Wii U became "the one" and "the many". The N64 stick gave Smash flick input, GameCube streaming gave Melee orchestral music, and the Wii's built-in online made online essential for Brawl. **Do:** List what the target hardware does that others can't and which audiences competitors already serve, then aim at the gap. On several platforms, give each version at least one mode built on its own strength while sharing mechanics, animation and balance. **Watch out:** Don't shoehorn in every function a device has; Brawl deliberately ignored much of what the Nunchuk and pointer could do.

*Sakurai's example:* Smash Run on 3DS, where every player explores a huge map on their own screen, versus 8-Player Smash on Wii U. · Episodes: [125](../episodes/notes/125-kid-icarus-uprising.md), [135](../episodes/notes/135-super-smash-bros-for-3ds-wii-u.md), [154](../episodes/notes/154-flick-input.md), [40](../episodes/notes/040-super-smash-bros-melee.md), [108](../episodes/notes/108-super-smash-bros-brawl.md)

### CASE-05 · Choose the concept your situation can finish, and uniquely enables
**Why:** Smash beat the RC adventure because the team needed a finished game and the RC game needed two years. Air Ride was a racer because a racer was quicker to make. Kirby's Adventure went to the NES because an SNES setup would take too long. Ultimate's continuity of team and tools made "Everyone is here!" a one-time chance. **Do:** Estimate each candidate concept's development time. Inventory what carries over (team, tools, assets, hardware architecture) and look for the bold idea that inventory makes cheap. **Watch out:** Feasibility is not timidity. The point is to spend your situation's advantages, not to play it safe.

### CASE-06 · Change a feature's context before cutting it
**Why:** On the NES, Kirby could fly past almost any challenge. Instead of removing flight, Sakurai enlarged the info panel, kept mid-boss fights to one screen and slowed flight, and felt it ended up nicely balanced. Super Star made co-op scrolling work by restructuring roles rather than dropping the request. **Do:** When a signature mechanic breaks balance or a request seems impossible, list the context levers (screen space, arena bounds, speed, camera, player roles, HUD) and try them before cutting. **Watch out:** Equal camera weight for every co-op player in a jump-heavy side-scroller is the kind of default that needs restructuring, not tuning.

### CASE-07 · Build variety as rule-changing parameters on one shared core
**Why:** Air Ride's machines feel totally different but share one behavior: apart from star versus bike types, everything is parameters. Meteos planets change block shapes, field width, launch behavior and audio, so players pick favorites. Smash for 3DS/Wii U's variable character strength layer powered several modes at once, and Uprising's weapons change Pit's speed, not just damage. **Do:** Implement one shared behavior, then make each vehicle, stage, character or weapon a parameter set that changes how it plays (size, gravity, speed, field, piece types) plus its own sound. Keep strength-changing layers out of modes that need a level playing field. **Watch out:** A character select that only swaps art is a missed chance.

*Sakurai's example:* Meteos' Boggob gains height with each jump, Hevendor's blocks vanish when matched, Brabbit moves very slowly. · Episodes: [59](../episodes/notes/059-kirby-air-ride.md), [74](../episodes/notes/074-meteos.md), [135](../episodes/notes/135-super-smash-bros-for-3ds-wii-u.md), [125](../episodes/notes/125-kid-icarus-uprising.md), [206](../episodes/notes/206-using-parameters-to-establish-characters.md)

### CASE-08 · Tune and test on the final build and the real hardware
**Why:** Meteos' speed was tied to processing speed, so a late optimization made it harder than intended. Mushiking was tuned on a dev board and its Mushi gets hungry too fast on the real unit. Ultimate's CPU fighters felt too hard at launch for reasons he still doesn't know. "When you're working with computers, sometimes there's no telling what you might get." **Do:** Lock processing speed and timing before final tuning. After any optimization, replay difficulty and timer-driven systems on the retail unit. If launch reveals a problem too risky to fix at its core, ship a narrow, contained fix. **Watch out:** Never let game speed depend on processing speed without re-tuning when it changes.

### CASE-09 · Give each sequel one headline concept its new situation makes possible
**Why:** Each Smash sequel had a clear headline tied to what changed: Melee's power-up of every element, Brawl's online and solo-mode pillars, the 3DS/Wii U's one and many, Ultimate's everyone. A sequel that repeats the original can expect roughly half the sales. **Do:** Name the headline in one line and tie it to what is new (hardware, audience, team continuity). Then list every element (characters, stages, graphics, effects, animation, sound, play feel, extras) and set an upgrade target for each. **Watch out:** "More of the same at the same scale" is not a headline.

### CASE-10 · Treat hard limits as design fuel
**Why:** Dream Land's 512-kilobit target produced mirrored bosses, shared sprite halves and hand-placed enemy paths that were cheap to run and lively to watch. Uprising scaled enemies up for a small screen; the 3DS's LCD led to slower knockback; Ultimate compressed its music to fit the cartridge. **Do:** When memory, processing or screen size is scarce, write the limit down and ask which design answers it (reused or mirrored art, authored patterns instead of simulation, bigger targets, slower motion) before cutting content. **Watch out:** Spend scarce detail where players look, not where nobody will see it.

### CASE-11 · When a mode is out of budget, rebuild it from strengths you already have
**Why:** At Ultimate's scale, a Subspace-style adventure and Trophies were too costly, and a string of versus fights would satisfy nobody. Spirits reused Smash's borrowed characters, worlds and sounds to recreate their source games, and World of Light tied them together. Smash Run took City Trial's rules; Melee's Trophies brought in characters who couldn't be playable. **Do:** Inventory the assets and systems you have and design the missing mode around them. Pick lightweight formats when you want volume. Let players over-power themselves as a chosen difficulty, but pay them less when they do. **Watch out:** Cheap formats still need a reason to exist; collecting for its own sake turns into a chore.

### CASE-12 · Earn replay value with varied, fun play, not punishing difficulty
**Why:** NES games were often made hard so they'd last, which left beginners out; Sakurai's view is that a fun game gets played again and again. Air Ride rejected a design where everyone converges on one perfect line and widened the play types instead. Randomness makes each session go differently. **Do:** Add at least one mode built for variety rather than perfection (a sandbox, random power-ups and events) so players don't converge on one method. Make failure cheap and the next attempt worth seeing. **Watch out:** Restart-heavy designs only work if players want to see what's next.

*Sakurai's example:* Kirby's Dream Land, finished in about 20 minutes by experienced players, sold over 5 million copies; City Trial makes every session different. · Episodes: [4](../episodes/notes/004-kirbys-dream-land.md), [59](../episodes/notes/059-kirby-air-ride.md), [100](../episodes/notes/100-development-secrets-of-the-original-kirby.md), [128](../episodes/notes/128-is-your-game-appealing-enough-to-start-over.md), [189](../episodes/notes/189-randomness-spices-games-up.md)

### CASE-13 · When adapting, keep the proven core and spend effort on the new format
**Why:** Mushiking's arcade rock-paper-scissors already had push and pull, so Sakurai spent his effort on the pet: smoothing growth-stage walls, a Mushi that grazes, hidden behaviors. In licensed work fans want faithfulness first, so he invented where the source showed little: pilots Fox and Captain Falcon left room for the Falcon Punch. **Do:** Identify the source's proven loop and keep it. Put design effort into what the new format adds. Stay faithful where fans care, invent where the source is silent, and redesign characters to fit their new role. **Watch out:** Freely rewriting what fans love costs you the product's value to them.

*Sakurai's example:* Meta Knight's mask slit has taken three shapes to suit each game's role and era. · Episodes: [92](../episodes/notes/092-sodatete-kouchuu-ouja-mushiking.md), [188](../episodes/notes/188-faithfulness-comes-first-in-licensed-games.md), [27](../episodes/notes/027-super-smash-bros.md), [100](../episodes/notes/100-development-secrets-of-the-original-kirby.md), [108](../episodes/notes/108-super-smash-bros-brawl.md)

### CASE-14 · When delegated core work stalls, step in with fresh specs
**Why:** A year into delegated development on Air Ride, not one Warp Star worked. At the lead programmer's request Sakurai returned as director, wrote new specs and rebuilt the logic, finishing in three and a half months on mostly finished courses and machines. Iwata did the same kind of rescue on Melee's code. **Do:** Set a checkpoint for the core gameplay. If it still doesn't work, have the person with the vision rewrite the specs and rebuild the logic on the existing assets, briefing every discipline at once so instructions aren't distorted in relay. **Watch out:** Stepping in is a rescue, not a habit; he later handed parameter entry to planners so he could supervise (see chapter 10).

*Sakurai's example:* Kirby Air Ride's rebuild; the relay problems on Uprising that led him to brief everyone together. · Episodes: [59](../episodes/notes/059-kirby-air-ride.md), [259](../episodes/notes/259-mr-iwata.md), [114](../episodes/notes/114-explain-ideas-to-everyone-at-once.md)

### CASE-15 · If people don't get the concept, explain its depth yourself
**Why:** Before the original Smash shipped, sales staff, wholesalers and magazines dismissed Nintendo characters fighting and called the game shallow, and ignored his documentation of its push and pull. He turned it into the Smash Bros. DOJO!! site, writing the HTML himself; for Brawl it posted a detail every weekday in six languages. **Do:** Write a plain explanation of how to play and why the game is deep, in the words of whoever knows it best, and publish it on a regular schedule where players will find it. **Watch out:** A new kind of game rarely explains itself, and the press may not do it for you.

*Sakurai's example:* Smash Bros. DOJO!!, which he later saw as the root of the effort behind Ultimate's fighter reveals. · Episodes: [27](../episodes/notes/027-super-smash-bros.md), [143](../episodes/notes/143-official-websites.md), [245](../episodes/notes/245-smash-bros-dojo.md)

### CASE-16 · Check a new technique fits the team and the game before adopting it
**Why:** Mid-development, Kirby Super Star switched to CG backgrounds in the wake of Donkey Kong Country. The staff didn't know the technique and it didn't suit Kirby's pieced-together terrain. Still, he says, learning new skills matters, because what you make today leads to what you make tomorrow. **Do:** Before switching technique mid-project, confirm the team knows it and that it suits how your levels are built. If you go ahead, budget for the learning. **Watch out:** Following a trend because big games use it is not a reason.

*Sakurai's example:* Kirby Super Star's CG backgrounds. · Episodes: [20](../episodes/notes/020-kirby-super-star.md)

### CASE-17 · For a fixed-scope side project, script it all and pilot it first
**Why:** Running a channel next to a large game was only feasible if all the content was decided in advance. A three-episode pilot tested whether he could really do it and won Nintendo's cooperation. He chose speed over perfection and cleared reviews the day they arrived. **Do:** When the content can be known up front, plan and write all of it before production, pilot a few pieces at final quality, and track everything in one sheet that holds the release order and progress. **Watch out:** You give up the ability to respond to feedback mid-run, so this suits fixed content, not novel mechanics.

*Sakurai's example:* 256 planned episodes, every script but the finale written first. · Episodes: [261](../episodes/notes/261-masahiro-sakurai-on-creating-games-finale-special.md)

## Common mistakes

- Tuning risk to the dev team's skill instead of the target player ([4](../episodes/notes/004-kirbys-dream-land.md), [148](../episodes/notes/148-more-game-essence-means-less-broad-appeal.md)).
- Making a game hard so it lasts, instead of fun so it's replayed ([4](../episodes/notes/004-kirbys-dream-land.md)).
- Cutting a signature mechanic before trying to change its context ([11](../episodes/notes/011-kirbys-adventure.md)).
- Equal camera weight for every player in jump-heavy co-op ([20](../episodes/notes/020-kirby-super-star.md)).
- Content chunks that end up playing the same ([20](../episodes/notes/020-kirby-super-star.md)).
- Novelty that renames the genre's verb without changing it ([74](../episodes/notes/074-meteos.md)).
- Choosing a concept without estimating how long it takes to finish ([27](../episodes/notes/027-super-smash-bros.md)).
- Character, stage or weapon variants that only change the art ([74](../episodes/notes/074-meteos.md)).
- Tuning on dev hardware and never on the production unit; optimizing after balance is signed off ([92](../episodes/notes/092-sodatete-kouchuu-ouja-mushiking.md), [141](../episodes/notes/141-unexpected-results.md)).
- A sequel at the same scale with similar content ([164](../episodes/notes/164-making-sequels.md)).
- A director entering every parameter alongside supervision, approvals and marketing ([135](../episodes/notes/135-super-smash-bros-for-3ds-wii-u.md)).
- Variable-strength systems leaking into level-playing-field modes ([135](../episodes/notes/135-super-smash-bros-for-3ds-wii-u.md)).
- Using every function of new hardware because it's there ([154](../episodes/notes/154-flick-input.md)).
- Assuming a new kind of game will explain itself ([143](../episodes/notes/143-official-websites.md)).
- Adopting a trendy technique mid-project that the team doesn't know ([20](../episodes/notes/020-kirby-super-star.md)).

## Review questions

1. What named problem, platform strength or market gap does our concept answer?
2. Can a novice finish with only the core, and what does the optional depth give veterans?
3. Given our team, tools and remaining time, can we finish this concept, and what does our situation make uniquely possible?
4. Do our characters, stages or vehicles change the rules, or only the art?
5. Has the final balance been checked on production hardware after the last optimization?
6. Before cutting a feature or content, have we tried changing its context or using the limit as a design idea?
7. For a sequel: what is the one-line headline, and does every element have an upgrade target?
8. If players or partners don't get the game, where can they learn why it's deep?

## Exercises

1. **Write your own caption (30 minutes).** Episode 2 sums up each of Sakurai's games in one line naming its audience or its hook. Write that line for your game, then the problem it solves in its genre in one more sentence. If the second sentence is hard, that's the work to do next.
2. **Core-only playthrough (1–2 hours).** Play your game using only its most basic verbs, ignoring every depth system. Can you finish it? If not, find where the depth system is mandatory and decide whether a beginner should need it.
3. **Context before cuts (2 hours).** Pick the mechanic your team most wants to nerf or remove. List five context levers (space, bounds, speed, camera, roles, HUD) and prototype two of them before touching the mechanic itself.
4. **Parameter roster (half a day).** Take two characters, vehicles or stages and make them share one behavior. Express their differences only as parameters, push those differences until each can be described in a word or two, and give each its own sound.
5. **Retail-hardware pass (2 hours).** Run your build on the slowest device you'll ship on. Time every timer-driven system (spawns, hunger, cooldowns, day cycles) against the spec, and check that game speed doesn't depend on frame rate.
6. **Situation inventory (1 hour).** List what your team already has (people, tools, assets, finished systems, a platform's unique feature). Write one bold idea that inventory makes cheap, as continuity made "Everyone is here!" possible.
