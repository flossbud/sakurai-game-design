# 06 · Animation

> For Sakurai, animation in an action game is gameplay. The attack animation is the main thing that tells players what they did, whether it worked and how heavy it was, so he judges it first by responsiveness and readability and only then by beauty. He snaps to a big pose on the frame the button is pressed. He puts the strongest pose exactly where hit stop will freeze it, makes damage poses extreme and follow-throughs big, and exaggerates everything, because a small CG character on a small screen loses most of the information a real body gives off. He is suspicious of convenient tools (interpolation, automatic blending, single-axis rotation, centered pivots, realistic rigs), because their defaults quietly sand away the impact.

## The big ideas

**Every attack is four parts, and each part has a gameplay job.** Standby is the base every other pose builds on. The lead-in acknowledges the input and warns the opponent. The attack is the key pose that hit stop freezes, and "everything hinges on this moment." The follow-through shows how heavy the move was and is the window when the attacker can't act; cancel frames hand control back ([36](../episodes/notes/036-breaking-down-attack-animations.md), [77](../episodes/notes/077-follow-throughs-make-the-impact.md)). Because each part is gameplay, Sakurai sets the timing in frames before anyone animates: standby pose, attack pose, attack start frame and total frames ([14](../episodes/notes/014-assigning-animations.md), [36](../episodes/notes/036-breaking-down-attack-animations.md)).

**Readability beats realism.** On a small screen, an attack that looks merely "OK" isn't nearly far enough, and game characters lose much of the information real people give off. So too much is just right ([28](../episodes/notes/028-too-much-is-just-right.md), [91](../episodes/notes/091-exaggerate-to-make-up-for-information-loss.md)). Poses must cut a clear, asymmetric silhouette from the game's own camera, damage poses must look nothing like standby, faces must face the viewer and be pushed past "done" ([62](../episodes/notes/062-attack-poses.md), [142](../episodes/notes/142-damage-animations.md), [181](../episodes/notes/181-flipped-animation.md), [166](../episodes/notes/166-facial-animations.md)). Every judgment is made in motion, at gameplay size.

**Distrust the defaults.** Interpolation buries key poses under in-betweens, long blends kill rhythm, one-axis rotation looks mechanical, centered pivots look bland, and realistic skeletons forbid the exaggeration that gives animation its greatness. Sakurai reaches key poses fast, keeps blends short, rotates on two axes, shifts pivots off center and asks for rigs built to bend ([150](../episodes/notes/150-the-perils-of-interpolation.md), [194](../episodes/notes/194-animation-blending.md), [251](../episodes/notes/251-rotation-on-two-axes.md), [255](../episodes/notes/255-what-happens-when-you-shift-the-center-of-gravity.md), [236](../episodes/notes/236-the-limitations-of-skeletons.md)).

**Animation and collision are one design.** A designer can make any swing connect by placing a hitbox, but if the visual and the collision disagree, the game feels broken. Animators have to know when and where every attack is active, and the strongest pose belongs on the first clean-hit frame ([225](../episodes/notes/225-always-keep-attack-collision-in-mind.md), [165](../episodes/notes/165-odds-and-ends-of-supervising.md)).

## Principles

### ANIM-01 · Snap to a big lead-in pose on the frame the button is pressed
**Why:** A smooth transition out of standby takes too long. Jumping straight to a big pose tells players their input registered and cues the opponent to dodge. Network and TV lag can never be fully removed, but the input can still be acknowledged at once; in Smash, responsiveness comes first. **Do:** Make the first frame after input a drastically different windup pose, with no easing out of standby. Charge moves hold that pose and shake in place. Light, spammable moves can skip the lead-in entirely. If a windup reads as slow, pull straight into the pose rather than lengthening the ease. **Watch out:** Interpolating from idle just to keep the animation from skipping, which delays the answer to the press.
*Sakurai's example:* Smash Ultimate's Smash Attacks snap to the lead-in pose on the press, then hold and shake while charging; Zero Suit Samus's neutral attack is out one frame after input; Sora's Sonic Blade windup was fixed by pulling directly into the pose. · Episodes: [36](../episodes/notes/036-breaking-down-attack-animations.md), [49](../episodes/notes/049-making-lead-ins-instant-and-impactful.md), [150](../episodes/notes/150-the-perils-of-interpolation.md), [253](../episodes/notes/253-odds-and-ends-of-supervising-sora-edition.md)

> "By instantly jumping to a big pose, the player can immediately know their input was recognized." ([36](../episodes/notes/036-breaking-down-attack-animations.md), 00:56)

### ANIM-02 · Animate where and when the attack's collision actually is
**Why:** Any animation can be made to connect by placing collision, but a big gap between visuals and collision feels wrong, and an animation in the wrong place feels even more broken. Hits that should land but don't, or that keep hitting after the swing, feel off. Animation shapes game feel so much that animators can't leave gameplay to the designers. **Do:** Show animators each attack's active frames and hitbox placement, and review with collision displayed. Make every attack cross the median line where opponents stand and stay in the gameplay plane. When a hit feels wrong, change the arc or pose and the hitbox together. Watch stance changes that move the target's top node. **Watch out:** Stretching the hitbox to meet a misplaced animation.
*Sakurai's example:* Pyra's forward aerial collision was shortened because it felt like it kept hitting; Mythra's slash was lined up with a diagonal; Sacred Arrow's blade was raised slightly so it wouldn't pass straight through opponents; Steve's grab arc was pulled in. · Episodes: [225](../episodes/notes/225-always-keep-attack-collision-in-mind.md), [165](../episodes/notes/165-odds-and-ends-of-supervising.md), [62](../episodes/notes/062-attack-poses.md), [142](../episodes/notes/142-damage-animations.md), [253](../episodes/notes/253-odds-and-ends-of-supervising-sora-edition.md), [50](../episodes/notes/050-jobs-in-game-development.md)

> "When crafting attack animations, it's important that the animators are always aware of when and where collision occurs." ([225](../episodes/notes/225-always-keep-attack-collision-in-mind.md), 01:52)

### ANIM-03 · Make the hit stop frame the attack's strongest, clearest pose
**Why:** Hit stop freezes the instant of contact, so that pose is the one players remember. On a finishing blow the screen stops completely, and a weak pose there is no good. In games about trading blows, attack animations affect fun more than any others. **Do:** Treat the contact frame as a key pose: put it in your reference and review it on its own. On sword swings, start collision partway into the swing once the pose is strong, so hit stop kicks in after the blade has swung out a bit. Check the pose on both sides of the screen. **Watch out:** Collision from the very first frame of motion, which freezes a half-formed pose.
*Sakurai's example:* Smash sword-swing collision doesn't start until the sword is partway through the swing. · Episodes: [62](../episodes/notes/062-attack-poses.md), [225](../episodes/notes/225-always-keep-attack-collision-in-mind.md), [36](../episodes/notes/036-breaking-down-attack-animations.md), [179](../episodes/notes/179-blade-trails.md), [142](../episodes/notes/142-damage-animations.md)

### ANIM-04 · Exaggerate past "OK": too much is just right
**Why:** Attacks are small things on a small screen, so the usual problem is not going nearly far enough; if a pose looks OK, there's still room. Polygon characters lose a wide range of the information real humans give off, and big performances make up for it. Pushing beyond common sense is what gives animation its greatness. **Do:** Push twists, reach and extension until the rig's weighting nearly breaks. Treat motion capture as a starting point and go bigger. Make a sword swing more than just a sword swing. Push harder for characters that appear small or far away. **Watch out:** Staying within what's physically possible.
*Sakurai's example:* Kazuya's Roundhouse to Triple Spin Kick twists so far it could look like a bug, and Sakurai says they should have gone further; Virtua Fighter's upper and lower body sometimes face opposite directions; film director Kinji Fukasaku's over-the-top Clock Tower 3 cutscenes. · Episodes: [28](../episodes/notes/028-too-much-is-just-right.md), [91](../episodes/notes/091-exaggerate-to-make-up-for-information-loss.md), [166](../episodes/notes/166-facial-animations.md), [236](../episodes/notes/236-the-limitations-of-skeletons.md), [62](../episodes/notes/062-attack-poses.md), [150](../episodes/notes/150-the-perils-of-interpolation.md), [142](../episodes/notes/142-damage-animations.md), [50](../episodes/notes/050-jobs-in-game-development.md)

> "If you're animating an attack, the best way to think of it is that 'too much is just right.'" ([28](../episodes/notes/028-too-much-is-just-right.md), 00:38)

### ANIM-05 · Reach key poses fast; don't let interpolation water them down
**Why:** Tools interpolate conveniently, "but convenience doesn't mean quality": in-between frames overpower the key poses and make an animation feel slow even at the same length. Time saved on in-betweens goes to the lead-in and the strike, and key poses on screen for even a single frame still leave an impression when the attack lands. **Do:** Scrub interpolated attacks and cut in-between time. Pull the windup further back and hold it relatively long, then pass through each key pose in as little as one frame. Give poses more energy than the reference. Judge snappiness by eye in game, not by frame count. **Watch out:** Default curves between reference poses, especially when moving 2D animation to 3D.
*Sakurai's example:* A Smash Ultimate kick recreated with straight interpolation felt too slow at 8 frames; the release version is still 8 frames and much snappier. Ryu's crouching heavy punch holds a pulled-back anticipation, then shows the original's three key poses for one frame each. · Episodes: [150](../episodes/notes/150-the-perils-of-interpolation.md), [194](../episodes/notes/194-animation-blending.md), [36](../episodes/notes/036-breaking-down-attack-animations.md), [49](../episodes/notes/049-making-lead-ins-instant-and-impactful.md)

### ANIM-06 · Make the follow-through big, held and unlike standby
**Why:** The hit itself lasts a fraction of a second. The follow-through takes the most screen time and has to show what the character just did and how heavy it was. It is also the opening when the player can't act, so it must read clearly. **Do:** Keep the motion going with big movements that carry the strike's momentum. Hold a pose clearly unlike standby until the cancel frames. Give it its own trajectory instead of reversing the attack. Give landings a full follow-through, placed to read from the game's camera. **Watch out:** Interpolating straight back to idle and letting the move fizzle out.
*Sakurai's example:* Ryu's uppercut in Smash Ultimate takes a new return path instead of the original's backwards playback; Sora's forward-aerial landing was reworked to follow through fully. · Episodes: [77](../episodes/notes/077-follow-throughs-make-the-impact.md), [36](../episodes/notes/036-breaking-down-attack-animations.md), [150](../episodes/notes/150-the-perils-of-interpolation.md), [253](../episodes/notes/253-odds-and-ends-of-supervising-sora-edition.md)

> "The follow-through after an attack determines the impact of the attack itself. Don't let it fizzle out—keep those actions expressive right to the end." ([77](../episodes/notes/077-follow-throughs-make-the-impact.md), 04:01)

### ANIM-07 · Make the first damage frame an extreme pain pose
**Why:** Hit stop holds the first frame of the damage animation, so that frame is what players actually see. A pose nothing like standby makes it clear the character was hit, even from far away, so both players feel the impact. **Do:** Author that frame as an exaggerated, asymmetric pain pose. Segue into it with a quick blend from the pose at impact (about four frames in Smash) rather than a snap. Choose high, middle or low reactions by the nearest impact point and small, medium or large by strength, plus a separate knockback set. Twist bodies in knockback so arms and legs read. Start damage immediately, even from special attacks. **Watch out:** Damage poses close to standby, and bodies that just fly sideways.
*Sakurai's example:* Smash's nine grounded damage reactions; the extreme Kirby damage pose he drew in the Kirby Super Star design document; a fighter hit by a Final Smash should enter the damage animation immediately. · Episodes: [142](../episodes/notes/142-damage-animations.md), [47](../episodes/notes/047-eight-hit-stop-techniques.md), [165](../episodes/notes/165-odds-and-ends-of-supervising.md), [166](../episodes/notes/166-facial-animations.md), [253](../episodes/notes/253-odds-and-ends-of-supervising-sora-edition.md)

### ANIM-08 · Pose for a clear, asymmetric silhouette from the game's camera
**Why:** Smash is seen from the side, so profile silhouettes come before everything else, and one small silhouette tweak can make a huge difference. Symmetric poses and plain sideways bends are dull; a twist lets the limbs read. A character's form should be exaggerated even in solid black, and if the basic pose is off, no finishing touch will save it. **Do:** Make all four limbs readable from the main view. Break symmetry unless it's deliberately comic. Twist the torso instead of bending sideways, and give torso, hips and legs different lines. Check poses on both screen sides and as a black silhouette. Adjust swings until their blade trails read from the camera. **Watch out:** Horizontal slashes whose trails disappear from the side.
*Sakurai's example:* Pyra's hit pose as submitted and after his correction; Sora's forward aerial, where upper-body twist brings more information to the pose; the Pit figma, default and adjusted. · Episodes: [62](../episodes/notes/062-attack-poses.md), [142](../episodes/notes/142-damage-animations.md), [106](../episodes/notes/106-posing-suggestions.md), [243](../episodes/notes/243-establishing-characters-through-their-design.md), [253](../episodes/notes/253-odds-and-ends-of-supervising-sora-edition.md), [179](../episodes/notes/179-blade-trails.md), [225](../episodes/notes/225-always-keep-attack-collision-in-mind.md), [28](../episodes/notes/028-too-much-is-just-right.md)

### ANIM-09 · Specify each animation's frames and key poses before animators start
**Why:** Getting animation ideas across to the team is vital, and timings should be chosen with push and pull in mind before animation begins. Four values are often enough for animators to start. Preparing that data, and catching errors when the build differs, takes a feel for frames. **Do:** Keep an animation list: every animation in text, with its length and when the attack occurs; mark idle and walk loops "Loop" or "Any." For each attack give the standby pose, attack pose, attack start frame and total frames until it can be canceled. Add photographed figure poses and source screenshots. Treat it as a first step that animators fill in. **Watch out:** Treating the director's spec as the finished animation.
*Sakurai's example:* Smash fighter spec sheets run 70 to 80 pages of figure photos per fighter on average. · Episodes: [14](../episodes/notes/014-assigning-animations.md), [36](../episodes/notes/036-breaking-down-attack-animations.md), [76](../episodes/notes/076-learn-to-count-frames.md), [225](../episodes/notes/225-always-keep-attack-collision-in-mind.md)

### ANIM-10 · Judge animation in motion, at gameplay size and camera
**Why:** A model can look warped in a paused frame, and correcting that kills the animation's energy. Motion that works up close feels far too understated when the character is small or far away, and faces in Smash are often too small to make out. **Do:** Review at full speed, at real screen size and from the gameplay camera. Turn down freeze-frame fixes when the move reads in motion. For close checks, use slow motion, hidden backgrounds and dev-mode transparency for capes and clothes. **Watch out:** Approving work in a close-up model viewer.
*Sakurai's example:* Smash Ultimate's developer mode makes capes translucent so poses can be checked without editing the model. · Episodes: [28](../episodes/notes/028-too-much-is-just-right.md), [91](../episodes/notes/091-exaggerate-to-make-up-for-information-loss.md), [166](../episodes/notes/166-facial-animations.md), [179](../episodes/notes/179-blade-trails.md), [217](../episodes/notes/217-transparency-features.md), [79](../episodes/notes/079-visual-effects-in-slow-motion.md)

### ANIM-11 · Keep blends short; use them for cancels, segues and directional poses
**Why:** Blending lets the same follow-up start naturally from very different poses. But making animation too smooth reduces its rhythm and impact, and automatic blends can distort the pose, so keep them "quick and snappy." **Do:** On cancels, blend from the current pose into the new animation. Segue into damage poses with a short blend. Check every blend for distortion. For directional stances, blend a fixed center pose with poses around a circle. In full 3D, layer upper and lower body, plant feet on terrain and let slopes affect turns. **Watch out:** Long, smooth blends that sap rhythm.
*Sakurai's example:* Smash's idle-to-walk and attack-to-walk use the same walk and both look natural; shield shifting blends circular movement with the center stance. Sakurai notes blending matters less in a fast game like Smash than in fully 3D games. · Episodes: [194](../episodes/notes/194-animation-blending.md), [47](../episodes/notes/047-eight-hit-stop-techniques.md), [142](../episodes/notes/142-damage-animations.md)

### ANIM-12 · Squash and stretch while keeping volume constant
**Why:** Rotation and translation are used everywhere, but scaling is overlooked; with it a bouncing ball feels almost touchable. The key rule is to keep mass roughly constant. **Do:** Squash on landings, crouches and bounces and compare with and without. When squashing one axis, widen the others. On 3D models, sculpt the squashed shape (an oval-track profile or a blend shape) instead of scaling one axis. **Watch out:** Vertical-only squash, which loses physical presence.
*Sakurai's example:* Kirby has squashed on crouches and landings since the Game Boy; for the 3D model Sakurai asked in a Daily Report for more volume at the sides. · Episodes: [121](../episodes/notes/121-squashing-and-scaling.md), [232](../episodes/notes/232-manga-like-visuals.md)

### ANIM-13 · Move bodies like bodies: two axes, linked joints, bent wrists
**Why:** A head turned on one axis looks mechanical, and a wrist twisted on one axis looks like a drill. Connected joints move together in real bodies. Wrists that follow a straight line from the arm look limp, and a perpendicular grip on a swung weapon looks like an action figure. **Do:** Layer a dip or tilt onto every Y-axis turn of heads, wrists and torsos. Move the shoulder and collarbone with a twisting wrist. Bend the wrists. Use a diagonal grip for anything swung with the forearm. Plan head turns with the eye line, and shrink the head turn once the eyes do part of the work. **Watch out:** Turning the whole head before the eyes exist.
*Sakurai's example:* His own one-axis head turn as the bad example; revving a motorcycle, where the shoulder and collarbone move with the wrist; Sora's losing clap, limp until the wrists bent. · Episodes: [251](../episodes/notes/251-rotation-on-two-axes.md), [253](../episodes/notes/253-odds-and-ends-of-supervising-sora-edition.md), [106](../episodes/notes/106-posing-suggestions.md), [210](../episodes/notes/210-portraying-fingers.md)

### ANIM-14 · Give mechanical motion personality: off-center pivots, wobble and recoil
**Why:** Designers tend to move things in straight lines and smooth circles, but animation means putting a soul into something that doesn't move, and software defaults look bland. **Do:** Shift the pivot of thrown and spinning objects or swung weapons slightly off center. Layer a small looping offset onto straight-flying projectiles. Snap weapons back on every shot. Hand-tune rotation curves (offset the two axes, add an occasional counter-motion). Mock up the alternative during review. **Watch out:** Leaving linear moves and centered spins as the tool produced them.
*Sakurai's example:* Shotzo's "wobbly cannonballs" in Kirby; Smash Ultimate's bomb spins on an off-center axis; Pyra's Blazing End pivot was moved off the grip; Star Force's side cannons pull back with every missile. · Episodes: [255](../episodes/notes/255-what-happens-when-you-shift-the-center-of-gravity.md), [156](../episodes/notes/156-special-crossover-2.md), [251](../episodes/notes/251-rotation-on-two-axes.md), [150](../episodes/notes/150-the-perils-of-interpolation.md)

### ANIM-15 · Build rigs that allow exaggeration from the start
**Why:** Arms rigged directly to the torso made adequate animation impossible in the first Smash. Joints naturally stop at 180 degrees, but bending past straight adds oomph; backward-bending joints make IK flip, and fixes like extra joints cost processing and need animators with cartoon sensibilities. **Do:** Decide early whether the style needs exaggeration. Let shoulders move forward and back and the upper and lower body rotate independently. Budget extra joints where limbs must bend past straight. When processing is tight, lock finger joints and swap preset finger shapes. **Watch out:** Letting a convenient standard rig decide what poses are possible.
*Sakurai's example:* He proposed past-straight joints at the start of Smash development; processing load and staffing left it unsolved. · Episodes: [236](../episodes/notes/236-the-limitations-of-skeletons.md), [28](../episodes/notes/028-too-much-is-just-right.md), [210](../episodes/notes/210-portraying-fingers.md)

### ANIM-16 · Keep faces toward the viewer in side-view games
**Why:** Without mirroring, a character on the right faces away, hiding the expression and giving players little information; a pure-profile run hides the face for half its movement. Non-realistic tricks can express a character better than anatomy. **Do:** Mirror the animation set when the character changes facing instead of turning the model. Angle runs diagonally toward the camera. Plan item hand-swaps and flipped handedness, and skip mirroring for asymmetric characters like sword fighters. Try putting the mouth on the side of the face in profile. **Watch out:** Rejecting a technique only because it isn't realistic.
*Sakurai's example:* Smash adopted flipped animation after Brawl; Kirby's run angles toward the camera; Mega Man Legends and Sonic put the mouth on the side of the face. · Episodes: [181](../episodes/notes/181-flipped-animation.md), [232](../episodes/notes/232-manga-like-visuals.md)

### ANIM-17 · Push facial expressions a step past done, shaped by anatomy
**Why:** CG loses information, so "too much" is about right for realistic and cartoony faces alike, and most artwork Sakurai received had expression changes that were too small. When the source has no facial range, the source comes first. **Do:** "Get it to where it feels done, then go a step further and make the movements even bigger." Show eye white above and below wide-open eyes, offset upper and lower teeth, and size mouths to the jaw. Retouch key expressions before modeling. Carry emotion through the body when the source face only blinks. **Watch out:** Subtle expressions on faces players see at tiny sizes.
*Sakurai's example:* Wolf's damage face was retouched to suit a wolf's jaw; Steve's only facial animation is a blink, so his movement does the rest. · Episodes: [166](../episodes/notes/166-facial-animations.md), [165](../episodes/notes/165-odds-and-ends-of-supervising.md), [253](../episodes/notes/253-odds-and-ends-of-supervising-sora-edition.md), [232](../episodes/notes/232-manga-like-visuals.md)

### ANIM-18 · Give movement weight, and give fast motions enough frames to register
**Why:** A motion that's over too fast doesn't land, ordinary legs can't keep up with very fast movement, and weight shifts and solid landings make movement believable. **Do:** Add frames to secondary motion that vanishes before it registers. For speeds a walk cycle can't support, use bounding or sliding. Shift the weight forward in walks. Keep feet planted on landing. **Watch out:** Sped-up realistic leg cycles and sliding feet.
*Sakurai's example:* Kid Icarus: Uprising's Pit bounds and slides because normal legs couldn't keep up; Kazuya's wings retracted in two frames on landing, so frames were added for impact; Sora's foot slid too far on landing. · Episodes: [84](../episodes/notes/084-units-of-speed.md), [165](../episodes/notes/165-odds-and-ends-of-supervising.md), [253](../episodes/notes/253-odds-and-ends-of-supervising-sora-edition.md), [121](../episodes/notes/121-squashing-and-scaling.md)

### ANIM-19 · Honor the source's feeling, not its literal frames
**Why:** 2D poses don't survive a literal trip to 3D, and an exact copy can miss what makes a character feel right. Sakurai wants to honor the feeling and do what works best. **Do:** Keep signature poses and quirks, but change what the new medium needs: a new first frame for momentum, a new follow-through path instead of reverse playback. Compare against source screenshots for feel, not just shape. Keep the source's limits and compensate elsewhere. **Watch out:** Forward-then-backward playback of 2D poses in 3D.
*Sakurai's example:* Ryu's uppercut; Kazuya's extreme spin-kick twist and knocked-down pose kept from his own game; the Nakoruru Mii costume, revised until it was clearly her. · Episodes: [150](../episodes/notes/150-the-perils-of-interpolation.md), [165](../episodes/notes/165-odds-and-ends-of-supervising.md), [166](../episodes/notes/166-facial-animations.md), [28](../episodes/notes/028-too-much-is-just-right.md), [243](../episodes/notes/243-establishing-characters-through-their-design.md)

### ANIM-20 · Check every variant: ground and air, every direction, both facings
**Why:** Variants drift apart. Sora's Sonic Blade had very different ground and aerial poses and lost reach when aimed upward, which counts as a bug. Poses look different on the left and right of the screen, and a stance change on hit can push a target out of a combo. **Do:** Play the ground and aerial versions of every special side by side. Test reach in every direction. Check poses on both screen sides. Run multi-hit strings against every reaction stance. **Watch out:** Reviewing only the default version of a move.
*Sakurai's example:* Sora's Sonic Blade fixes; the neutral-attack combo that missed because of a top-node shift. · Episodes: [253](../episodes/notes/253-odds-and-ends-of-supervising-sora-edition.md), [62](../episodes/notes/062-attack-poses.md), [142](../episodes/notes/142-damage-animations.md), [181](../episodes/notes/181-flipped-animation.md)

### ANIM-21 · Find and communicate poses with a physical figure
**Why:** There's no formula for posing; handling a physical figure teaches what makes a pose cool and which angle works best. A photographed pose communicates more clearly than words, and showing beats telling in feedback. **Do:** Keep a posable figure at your desk and play with it ("bundodo," zooming it around by hand). For specs, pose it, photograph it, cut out the shape and raise the contrast. In reviews, show a posed figure or a retouched frame. **Watch out:** Polishing details while the base pose is off.
*Sakurai's example:* His Pit figma, posed plainly and then adjusted; Sora's forward aerial explained with a posed figure. · Episodes: [106](../episodes/notes/106-posing-suggestions.md), [14](../episodes/notes/014-assigning-animations.md), [62](../episodes/notes/062-attack-poses.md), [253](../episodes/notes/253-odds-and-ends-of-supervising-sora-edition.md)

## Where the advice pulls both ways

- **Snap vs. blend.** Sakurai snaps the lead-in with no interpolation ([36](../episodes/notes/036-breaking-down-attack-animations.md), [49](../episodes/notes/049-making-lead-ins-instant-and-impactful.md)), yet blends the victim into the damage pose over about four frames ([47](../episodes/notes/047-eight-hit-stop-techniques.md), [142](../episodes/notes/142-damage-animations.md)) and blends cancels into the next action ([194](../episodes/notes/194-animation-blending.md)). The split is about who caused the change. When the *player* acts, the first frame must prove the input landed, so snap. When the game *reacts* (a hit received, a cancel into walking), a pop would look like a glitch, so blend, but briefly.
- **Reach key poses fast vs. hold and add frames.** Key poses should arrive quickly and can be on screen for a single frame ([150](../episodes/notes/150-the-perils-of-interpolation.md)), yet the anticipation is held relatively long, the follow-through as long as possible ([36](../episodes/notes/036-breaking-down-attack-animations.md)), and Kazuya's two-frame wing retraction needed *more* frames ([165](../episodes/notes/165-odds-and-ends-of-supervising.md)). Speed belongs to the transitions between key poses. Holds belong to anticipation and recovery. Secondary motion needs enough frames to be seen at all.
- **Hold the follow-through vs. cancel frames.** Hold the pose for the eye, let cancel frames end the vulnerability at the moment players expect, and blend out ([36](../episodes/notes/036-breaking-down-attack-animations.md), [77](../episodes/notes/077-follow-throughs-make-the-impact.md), [194](../episodes/notes/194-animation-blending.md)).
- **Exaggeration vs. fidelity to the source.** "Too much is just right" ([28](../episodes/notes/028-too-much-is-just-right.md)), but Steve keeps a blink-only face and Kazuya keeps his original poses ([166](../episodes/notes/166-facial-animations.md), [165](../episodes/notes/165-odds-and-ends-of-supervising.md)). Exaggerate the *feeling* of the source, not past its identity; where the source sets a limit, compensate through body movement.
- **Exaggeration vs. the rig and the budget.** Past-straight joints add oomph, but they make IK flip and cost processing and specialist staff ([236](../episodes/notes/236-the-limitations-of-skeletons.md)); fingers are the first thing cut when processing is tight ([210](../episodes/notes/210-portraying-fingers.md)). Decide on exaggeration at the start, when the rig can still be built for it.
- **Generous collision vs. matching animation.** In fast games collision should be wider than the weapon ([225](../episodes/notes/225-always-keep-attack-collision-in-mind.md)), but the animation must still sit where the collision is; some mismatch is acceptable only when it makes the game more fun ([225](../episodes/notes/225-always-keep-attack-collision-in-mind.md), [165](../episodes/notes/165-odds-and-ends-of-supervising.md)).
- **Hit stop on the extended-hitbox frame vs. impact.** Applying hit stop the moment a lengthened hitbox touches has merits, but Smash chooses the frame that best sells the impact ([142](../episodes/notes/142-damage-animations.md)).
- **Speed isn't a goal in itself.** Not every game needs to rush its animations ([150](../episodes/notes/150-the-perils-of-interpolation.md)), and blending matters far more in fully 3D games than in fast Smash ([194](../episodes/notes/194-animation-blending.md)). The constant is the question: does this animation match the game, and is it exaggerated enough?

## Common mistakes

- Easing out of standby into the windup, so the press isn't acknowledged on the first frame.
- Letting collision start on the first frame of a swing, so hit stop freezes a weak, half-formed pose.
- Damage poses that look like standby; knockback bodies flying flat and sideways.
- Stopping at "OK," importing raw motion capture, and keeping poses within real-world limits.
- Accepting the tool's interpolation between reference poses; judging speed by frame count alone.
- Follow-throughs that blend straight back to idle or replay the attack in reverse.
- Long, smooth blends that kill rhythm, left unchecked for distortion.
- Symmetric, front-facing poses; sideways torso bends; horizontal slashes that vanish from the side camera.
- Approving animation in a paused close-up and "fixing" warps that only show in freeze-frames.
- Single-axis head and wrist turns, straight wrists, perpendicular weapon grips, centered pivots, straight-line projectiles.
- Animators working without seeing collision, and designers stretching hitboxes to cover misplaced animation.
- Reviewing only the default version of a move and missing aerial, directional or mirrored problems.
- Discovering at the end that the rig can't bend the way the style demands.

## Review questions

1. Does the first frame after every attack press show a big pose clearly different from standby?
2. Pause on each attack's contact frame: is it the strongest, clearest pose, and does the hitbox sit on it?
3. From the farthest gameplay camera, can you read every attack and every hit reaction at a glance?
4. Does the follow-through show what just happened and how heavy it was, and look nothing like standby?
5. Is any motion mushy from interpolation or long blends, or too fast to register?
6. Does anything move like a machine: single-axis turns, centered pivots, straight-line flight, straight wrists?
7. Filled in black, from the gameplay camera, on both sides of the screen, is every key pose readable and asymmetric?
8. Has every move been checked in all its versions: ground and air, each direction, each facing and each target stance?

## Exercises

1. **Snap pass (2 hours).** Pick three attacks. Delete any easing out of standby so the first frame after input is a big, different lead-in pose. Record before and after at 60 fps and show both to someone who hasn't seen them; ask which feels more responsive.
2. **Contact-frame review (2 hours).** For every attack, pause on the first active frame with collision displayed. Fill the character in black and check the silhouette from the gameplay camera on both screen sides. Move collision later in the swing, or rework the pose, for the three weakest.
3. **Interpolation surgery (half a day).** Take one attack and keep its total frame count. Cut the in-betweens so key poses arrive fast, pull the windup back and hold it longer, then flash through the strike poses at one or two frames each. Compare the old and new versions in game.
4. **Damage set (one day).** Author high/middle/low by small/medium/large ground reactions whose first frames are extreme, asymmetric pain poses. Blend into them over about four frames. Run your multi-hit strings against every stance and fix any root offset that pushes the target away.
5. **"Too much" pass (2 hours).** Take three animations everyone agrees look "OK." Make a version of each that's clearly too big, view both at the farthest gameplay camera, and keep whichever reads better.
6. **Personality pass (2 hours).** List every single-axis rotation, centered pivot and straight-flying projectile in your game. Add a second axis, shift a pivot, add an overshoot frame or a six-frame wobble loop, and compare each side by side.
